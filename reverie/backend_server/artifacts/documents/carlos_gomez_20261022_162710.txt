The River at Dawn

The river smells like cold iron and wet stones. Before sunrise, it pushes through the narrow canyon with a sound like a fist striking a wooden table over and over. Mist curls around my ankles, damp and stubborn. Every leaf drips. Every twig snaps.

I stand on the bank in my old jacket, breathing air that tastes of mud, pine, and rain. The water is black, but the sky above it is beginning to bruise purple. Somewhere behind me, a bird calls once and falls silent, as if it has forgotten the rest of its song.

Stupid bird. I almost say it aloud.

Almost.

Instead, I write it down.

The current carries dead branches downstream. They spin, collide, and twist apart. I watch them disappear beneath the surface, and for a second, I imagine the river swallowing words—whole arguments, bitter memories, all the noise people make when they cannot sit still inside their own heads. It should be easy. The river does not fight what it carries. It simply moves.

But I am Carlos Gomez...
