The First Poem of Winter

January came in with its mouth full of snow,  
spilling white syllables across the fields,  
burying the fences, frosting the pines,  
while I stood at the window  
and shouted at the wind like it owed me money.

The hills were broad, ugly, and honest—  
no false colors, no perfume,  
just frozen grass flashing gray beneath the sun.  
I liked that. I liked the sky’s cold shoulder.  
I liked how the branches  
looked like cracked ribs  
reaching toward heaven.

I had come inside to write,  
but the room refused to be beautiful.  
It smelled of ink, stale coffee,  
and the stubborn heat  
of an iron pressed too long against a shirt.

Still, I pulled the paper close  
and listened to the pipes knock awake.  
Somewhere beneath the weather,  
a hidden river complained  
that no one remembered its name.

I wrote: The season does not soften us.  
Then I crossed it out  
because it sounded like a lesson.

Instead, I wrote:  
The river knows my name,  
and the wind ...
