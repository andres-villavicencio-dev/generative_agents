I begin before dawn, with coffee and the expectation that the clearest thoughts will come before the world makes its demands upon me. The central argument of this section is that creativity is not merely the invention of something novel. It is the disciplined exercise of judgment: the capacity to observe customary arrangements, question their necessity, and imagine how they might be improved without abandoning the purposes they serve.

The first consequence of this argument is that sound judgment begins with attention. The observer must collect facts before pronouncing upon them, yet must also recognize that facts alone do not choose among competing ends. In examining the mayoral candidates’ proposals, therefore, I do not ask merely which scheme appears most ingenious. I ask what each man believes the town requires, how he expects to finance it, and what happens if the expected revenue does not arrive. Uncertainty is unavoidable in public affairs; concealment of uncertainty is not.

The second consequence is that comparison must extend beyond visible promises. A tax-and-spending proposal may look beneficial to property owners while burdening the poor, or appear prudent in aggregate while diminishing a service relied upon by vulnerable neighbors. The creative judge considers consequences that the speaker has not emphasized, especially those affecting those least able to defend their interests.

Third, creativity requires the willingness to revise. A plan that cannot survive an objection to its assumptions has not yet been tested. Yet this revision must not become fickleness. Principles concerning justice, public credit, and the relief of necessity should remain steady while particular measures are altered.

Finally, the same habits of mind belong in the classroom. An inclusive lesson does not consist in adding voices merely for display; it consists in discovering what the learner has observed that the instructor had overlooked. The neglected concern, the unexpected question, and the modest correction often contribute more to understanding than the most polished speech.

Creativity, then, is imagination joined to scrutiny. It enlarges the possible while submitting it to examination. What we produce is improved not because every difficulty disappears, but because we have faced them honestly and imagined more wisely.
