I rose at four, before the town had fully stirred, and began in silence. The lamp cast a narrow circle upon my table; coffee sent up its bitter fragrance as I considered what the day required. The first hour must remain undisturbed. Reflection is not idleness, nor is it delay. It is the clearing away of inherited assumptions, so that one may see whether the opinions beneath them can bear the weight of a day’s work.

I therefore returned to the manuscript on creativity. The example required expansion—not merely description, but an account of its consequences in practice. A community confronted with an unused courtyard might first imagine it as a garden, a market, a classroom, or a place of disorder. The creative act lies not in selecting the prettiest vision, but in discovering what the place permits, what its inhabitants need, and what materials, labor, and knowledge are actually available. A useful idea must survive contact with ordinary life. It must be tested against expense, maintenance, accessibility, and the habits of those who are expected to use it. Invention, rightly understood, is judgment joined to imagination.

This is where thought becomes dangerous. The Architecture of Unthinking warns that mental stillness can become intellectual sloth, just as constant motion can prevent understanding. The mind must be permitted to wander, but it must also be obliged to return: to compare, revise, and submit its conclusions to external facts. I have too often admired the elegance of an idea before asking who would pay for it or whom it might exclude.

After coffee I shall continue the chapter, examining how an inclusive lesson might be advanced without being diluted into vagueness. Inclusion is not the addition of agreeable sentiments at the end of a design; it is the discovery of concerns that the original design concealed. The neglected vendor, the child without a quiet place to study, the neighbor unable to climb an entrance—all may reveal more about the project than its architect first perceived.

No appointments are confirmed, and I will not fill the day merely to prove my diligence. I shall preserve the rhythm that has served me: deliberate research, concentrated revision, and honest reflection. I begin with purpose, if not yet with perfect clarity. Purpose may prove enough for the first morning; the work itself will supply the remainder.
