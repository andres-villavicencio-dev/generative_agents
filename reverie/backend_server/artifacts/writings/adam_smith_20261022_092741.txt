The first obligation of a creative writer is not invention, but attention. We must discover what the subject has been trying to say while we believed ourselves to be directing it. My work on the creativity book has taught me this more clearly than any other undertaking. We do not create in isolation. We create through conversation—with history, with strangers, and with those whose interests are least likely to appear in our own conclusions.

Thursday begins, as it should, before the noise of the city. At four in the morning, with coffee in hand, I will compare the manuscript once more against the outline. I will remove ornament that conceals rather than clarifies. Every chapter should answer a genuine question, and every argument should preserve the difficulty of the problem. Where the language is merely impressive, it must go. Where an example has more life than the principle surrounding it, the example should lead.

The mayoral proposals demand the same discipline. Revenue is uncertain, and therefore promises must be examined without sentimentality. We should ask what each proposal enables, what it postpones, and whose burdens increase when public services are strained. The vulnerable among us rarely appear in a ledger as abstractions; they appear as longer waits, deteriorating buildings, reduced meals, and families forced to choose between private hardship and institutional neglect. A policy that appears prudent on paper may be unjust in practice. I will not accept that account merely because it is convenient.

My inclusive lesson must advance beyond good intentions. I shall consider overlooked neighborhood concerns, particularly those that are easily dismissed because they lack powerful advocates. Listening is not a preliminary exercise. It is part of the work itself.

The Architecture of Unthinking continues to occupy me. It reminds me that many inherited categories are merely habits disguised as reason. To think clearly, we must repeatedly test the boundaries of our concepts and remain willing to be corrected. That is the spirit I hope the creativity book embodies: not certainty masquerading as wisdom, but disciplined openness—curiosity joined to rigorous judgment, and the courage to revise.
