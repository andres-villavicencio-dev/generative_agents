I have spent many evenings considering a single question: what makes a society capable of enduring change? I am not merely asking whether a nation possesses the capacity for improvement, but whether its institutions, customs, and public spirit are sufficiently free to allow ordinary men to discover, develop, and share that capacity.

Creativity, I believe, is not the province of the gifted alone. It is the quiet work of combining what we already know with what we may yet imagine. Invention, art, literature, science, and industry all proceed from the same source: the ability to observe carefully, to question established opinions, and to present new ideas with confidence. This is why a society that values creativity must protect the thinker as well as the producer. Ideas should not be suppressed because they appear uncertain or unconventional. Innovation often begins precisely when the conventional seems to hold too tightly.

Yet creativity must not be allowed to remain private. Its greatest benefit to society comes when it is joined by practical attention, honest judgment, and an interest in the public good. We must encourage the creative mind to think in relation to community, to consider the consequences of its inventions, and to collaborate rather than compete. Education, public discourse, and institutions that reward useful progress together form a fertile ground for genuine invention.

I am also thinking about the next mayoral election. If a leader is to shape a society, he must value the capacity to create. He must listen to those whose ideas are still in the formative stage, protect time for experimentation, and be willing to change his own opinions when the evidence requires it. A community is only as strong as its willingness to welcome new voices.

The path forward is not to abandon tradition, but to extend the boundaries of what tradition protects. We must make the most useful ideas visible, accessible, and supported. When we do so, creativity will not merely add to the world; it will help reshape it.
