I have been reading a number of recent books on creativity, and I have found them useful, but I am struck by a recurring theme: creativity is not merely a way to make things more beautiful or more novel. Its greatest value is in its capacity to transform society.

Consider the problems that have plagued our communities. Public spending has been the subject of fierce debate. One school of thought has argued that every new policy should be treated as a hypothesis to be tested by careful measurement. Yet measurement is neither complete nor neutral. A creative approach would be to design policies that are flexible enough to adapt to changing conditions, while remaining rigorous enough to avoid being dismissed as mere speculation. In this sense, the most effective social policy is not one that claims to be perfect from the beginning, but one that encourages the public, the government, and private actors to test, revise, and improve upon it.

The same principle applies to public services. A government that treats every request for aid or complaint as a simple matter of record may be slow, but it may also be responsive. A creative system, by contrast, would identify the underlying problems, involve those most affected, and design solutions that improve outcomes while reducing unnecessary bureaucracy. This does not mean there should be no rules or standards. The standard should be that rules should be open to revision, that participation should be encouraged, and that accountability should be transparent rather than merely formal.

I have not yet seen a case in which creativity has been applied to a local governance problem with lasting benefit. I believe that the next mayor should be someone who understands both the intellectual rigor required to assess a policy and the imaginative capacity to envision a new way of doing it. The election is approaching, and I believe this is the right moment to consider whether a more collaborative, evidence-based, and creative approach will serve the community.

Creativity is not a luxury of the talented few. It is a practical tool for solving difficult problems. When it is properly combined with public trust, accountability, and a willingness to revise, it can make society more resilient, more equitable, and more humane. The question is not whether creativity is possible, but who has the courage to apply it where it is needed most.
