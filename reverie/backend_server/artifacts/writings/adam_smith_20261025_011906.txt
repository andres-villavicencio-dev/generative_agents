In this election, I have asked myself whether the candidates’ promises truly belong to the people who live here, or whether they merely belong to those who stand to gain from their success. The community has long valued stability, public order, and the improvement of its institutions. These values are not abstract ideals; they are reflected in the schools, the courts, the markets, and the institutions through which our lives are organized.

The candidates’ first proposal is to broaden municipal services. Their plan is ambitious and practical, yet it requires a different judgment. A service that cannot be administered effectively undermines the trust that makes governance work. I do not reject the idea of expanded services, but I question whether every improvement should be judged solely by convenience. A school that is beautiful but inefficient, a market that is lively but unfair, or an institution that appears generous while remaining ineffective—these may be more attractive than useful.

The second proposal concerns the funding of public works. It promises greater funding, but it does not specify how the money will be distributed, who will bear the cost, or what standards will govern the work. Good public policy must answer such practical questions. It must protect the property of the poor, prevent the abuse of public funds, and ensure that every dollar serves a purpose beyond the speaker’s ambition.

Finally, the candidates ask me whether the community should become more participatory. The idea deserves encouragement, but participation must not become mere performance. The community should decide what matters, but it should not be persuaded to agree with every proposal merely because it has a popular voice. We must be able to distinguish between a genuine expression of the people’s will and a mere imitation of that will.

Creativity, I believe, is essential to a thriving society. A city that constantly reinvents itself without regard for its enduring values can become a collection of fashionable projects rather than a community capable of enduring change. But creativity should not replace judgment. It should be guided by a careful examination of what the community actually needs, what it has been taught to value, and what it has been taught to fear.

The candidates offer bold promises, but they must be tested against the community’s real priorities. A leader who cannot defend those priorities cannot claim to represent them. Until the election has been properly assessed, I remain cautious. True leadership is not found in the loudest claims, but in the willingness to admit that a proposal may be wrong, and in the courage to revise it.
