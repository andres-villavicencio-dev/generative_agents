On this Monday, February 13, I find myself surrounded by two distinct concerns: the election of our local mayor and the question of whether creativity is not merely a talent but the very condition of a useful life.

At the polls, I must decide whether to support a candidate who promises order and stability, or one who promises progress and experimentation. Order, in its narrow sense, may protect what is already secure. Progress, by contrast, requires us to revise our habits, question our assumptions, and accept that our circumstances will change. Creativity is the practice of both. It is not the invention of every useful object, but the capacity to imagine what is still possible.

My life has taught me that discipline is not the absence of invention. A disciplined mind, therefore, is not one that refuses to be surprised. It is a mind that observes closely enough to notice that the familiar is often merely the old familiar. I have found the greatest inventions not in the first man who thought of something new, but in the second man who looked closely enough at it and said, “I can do better.”

This leads me to a particular conclusion: creativity is not the privilege of the bold or the gifted. It is the responsibility of every person who wishes to improve his own life and the world around him. It is the willingness to say, “I was wrong about that,” and “I have not exhausted every possibility before acting.”

In the election, I will therefore vote for the candidate who encourages this willingness. I will not ask the mayor to make every problem disappear. I will ask him to make every problem worth examining. I will ask him to listen to people who do not agree with him, because disagreement is often the beginning of improvement.

Creativity is not a decoration for a life lived without responsibility. It is the engine of responsibility. A life without invention is not merely dull; it is stagnant. A life without imagination is not merely ordinary; it is confined.

I will go to bed at eight o’clock. I will wake at four. I will eat dinner at five. Between those hours I will read, think, and write. I will not spend too much time worrying about the mayor, though I will notice him carefully. I will spend my evenings thinking about the ideas that change us.

I do not believe that the world will become perfect tomorrow. But tomorrow may contain the first real invention of the day. And that is enough to make every day worthwhile.
