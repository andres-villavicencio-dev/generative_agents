The first book has challenged every assumption I had held for months. It is not merely another argument against established institutions; it is a sustained, coherent effort to show that human progress is driven not by abstract philosophy alone, but by the practical demands of commerce, production, and social cooperation. The central idea is clear: no society can endure without a shared understanding of what is profitable, which is socially useful, and which is just.

This idea appears in three forms. First, it explains why the market rewards the inventive and the efficient. Second, it explains why competition produces better goods without necessarily producing better persons. Third, it explains why the protection of property and the security of contracts are the foundations of liberty. Yet the author does not stop at praise of the market. He shows that its greatest dangers are not necessarily the absence of restraint, but the absence of moral purpose. He argues that an economy must be guided by a standard of public usefulness, and that private self-interest, when unchecked, may serve only the strongest.

The argument is therefore strong, but it is also demanding. It is not a tidy doctrine; it is a critique of existing practices and a call for a more reflective order. Its greatest merit, I believe, is that it refuses to separate thought from action. A theory cannot remain useful if it fails to guide institutions, laws, and everyday decisions.

I read the chapter on public works with particular interest. The author’s proposal for a shared path and a central mural is particularly compelling. The path would connect the community’s strengths and give every resident a visible reason to cooperate. The mural might not be a static picture, but a record of that shared understanding—a reminder that the community’s progress depends on the contributions of all. It would make the abstract idea of “public usefulness” concrete enough to debate.

The proposal deserves serious comparison with Francisco’s proposal. I will examine their budgets, funding sources, and implementation plans carefully. The third option will also be measured. I will not decide by preference alone. I will decide by whether the plan improves the community, whether its costs are justified, and whether it preserves the freedom to experiment with public projects.

I am confident that careful comparison will reveal the differences, not the superior idea. I expect that the first book will not change my mind, but it may make me more willing to listen. That is a modest but valuable goal.
