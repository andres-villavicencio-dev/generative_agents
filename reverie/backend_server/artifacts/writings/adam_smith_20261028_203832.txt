On Monday, February 13, I began my day with the same quiet purpose I have always used: to discover how ordinary people can change ordinary places. I read a passage about the invention of steam engines, a workaday invention that did not immediately appear in a museum or a library. It was not the brilliance of an idea alone, but the persistence of those who tested it, improved it, and shared its benefits. From that beginning, I learned that creativity is not merely the production of a novel image or a strange new word. It is a disciplined way of seeing, and of making things fit together.

The passage also reminded me of civic contribution. A great public institution does not arise from a single visionary man, but from many hands working together. An open market, a public library, and a functioning government are all products of repeated cooperation, not of sudden genius. The strongest ideas are usually those that people have tested, debated, and helped to improve.

This brings me to my next concern: the local mayor’s election. I do not believe that the next mayor will be chosen by a crowd of empty chairs or a party slogan. He will be chosen by voters who have seen how public decisions affect them. The difference between a good leader and a bad one is not talent alone, but the ability to listen, explain, and serve. A community needs more than beautiful buildings or fancy speeches. It needs practical solutions, honest conversation, and a willingness to take responsibility.

Creativity and civic responsibility are two sides of the same coin. Creativity produces the ideas that make progress possible. Civic contribution decides which ideas deserve to be used. The world needs more imaginative minds, but it needs even more thoughtful, responsible ones. I hope the next mayor will learn this lesson by the next campaign.
