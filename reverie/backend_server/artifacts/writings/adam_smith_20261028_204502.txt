On a quiet Tuesday evening, I sat with a cup of tea and a few pages spread before me, wondering whether creativity is merely a gift, a talent, or something we can cultivate. The question has been troubling me for some time, and I thought it might be worth asking again.

Creativity does not appear to be a rare talent reserved for a few exceptional people. It is the willingness to look at ordinary circumstances with fresh eyes. It is the capacity to join two or more distinct ideas and discover a connection between them. It is the habit of asking, “What if?” and the courage to challenge the familiar.

For a long time, I imagined that creativity belonged only to artists and inventors. But I found myself thinking of the ordinary people who improve our world without always speaking in grand terms. A neighbor who fixes a leaky pipe, a teacher who changes a lesson to help a class understand it better, and a voter who carefully considers the consequences of every decision all exercise creativity. They do not always seek fame, nor do they necessarily possess the same boldness as some others. Yet their contributions are as vital to the community as those of the most celebrated.

This was especially clear to me when I looked at the mayoral election. A good leader is not simply a person with the most power. He or she is one who can listen, understand, and persuade others. A strong leader is also one who can imagine a better way forward, even when that way forward is difficult.

So I ask: what makes a person creative? I think it is curiosity, patience, and openness to new ideas. It is also the confidence that one can improve something by trying something different. Creativity is not merely invention; it is also the ability to improve the existing and to make the familiar seem worth reconsidering.

I hope that my book, Creativity and Civic Contribution, will help people understand this. I hope it will encourage us to look beyond the obvious and to consider how our ideas can improve the lives of others. I hope it will make the mayoral election seem less like a contest for power and more like an opportunity to help a community grow.

I want to believe that creativity is not a rare talent. It is a habit, and like any habit, it can be practiced. It is possible to be thoughtful, reflective, and useful without being extraordinary. And, perhaps, it is possible to be extraordinary without being isolated from the people we serve.

I shall write on, reflect on, and reconsider.
