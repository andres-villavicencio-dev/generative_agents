In the quiet hours of early morning, I have often asked myself what creativity means. It is not merely the invention of a new tool or a clever word. It is the courage to question the ordinary, to imagine a different way of arranging our lives, and to ask whether that arrangement serves the common good. When I write about civic contribution, I do not mean that every citizen must become a brilliant inventor. What matters is that creativity can help us notice problems we have taken for granted and that we can act upon them.

Creativity becomes useful when it improves public services. A local school might redesign its library so that reading materials and digital resources are easier to find. A town council could make the appointment process more transparent and easier for residents to understand. A neighborhood health department might offer more accessible appointment times and clearer guidance for those who need help. These changes are not always spectacular, but they can make life more practical and more dignified for ordinary people.

The mayor’s campaign promises may look impressive on paper. A promise to replace streetlights with solar panels is attractive. A promise to hire more park staff is generous. But a better question is whether those changes will be sustainable, whether they will reach the people who need them, and whether the money will be spent wisely. A proposal that requires no new funds and restores what was already being provided is a much stronger one.

My own purpose in writing this book is not to encourage unchecked invention. I believe that creativity should be guided by responsibility. A city should not be a laboratory for radical novelty. It should be a place where improvement is possible. One person can change a law, and a group can change a law more effectively. One person can raise a question, and a public discussion can answer it. That is the core of civic contribution: to think clearly, to speak honestly, and to act constructively for the greater good.

The mayor’s election is approaching, and that makes the issue all the more urgent. I hope that the candidates will listen to the people they will serve and test their ideas against facts rather than promises. We need leaders who value creativity, but only when that creativity is practical and attentive to the needs of ordinary citizens.
