The mind that is not accustomed to fresh thought often resembles a garden neglected by the hand: its branches wither, its roots grow dim, and its fruit remains unripe. For me, this is not merely a philosophical observation; it is a practical warning about public affairs. When our institutions are shaped by established opinion rather than inquiry, they become less capable of answering the pressing questions of our times.

Creativity, I believe, is not the invention of novelty for novelty’s sake. It is the capacity to combine ideas that have never been joined, to imagine consequences that have not yet been tried, and to recognize opportunities overlooked by the herd. A city may be prosperous and orderly, yet if its institutions remain unchanged, its citizens may be left without a voice. Conversely, a community that encourages imaginative inquiry may discover ways to solve its problems that were previously regarded as impossible.

The local mayor’s election is particularly significant in this context. What he proposes should not merely reflect the preferences of his constituents, but should also reflect their capacity to participate. Creativity cannot be imposed upon them by the mayor’s office. The best government is therefore one in which decision-making is a continuing process of consultation, discussion, and improvement.

I would suggest two practical ways to foster this. First, every institution should create an open space in which ideas can be offered without immediate judgment. A council meeting should not be a series of closed debates in which every opinion is silenced. It should be a forum where questions are challenged, where evidence is examined, and where dissent is treated as a useful contribution.

Second, participation should be more than merely attending a meeting. Citizens should be invited to shape the decisions that affect them. This may mean contributing to the preparation of a budget, reviewing a proposed law, or selecting a committee that will deliberate the issue. Participation should not be reduced to a vote upon which a leader can claim responsibility. The people should be partners in the deliberation, and the people who decide should remain accountable to them.

Creativity, I conclude, is not a distant luxury of the philosopher. It is a necessity for a healthy society. When government encourages fresh thought and invites citizens to take an active part in shaping its decisions, it becomes more responsive, more just, and more capable of meeting the challenges of the present.
