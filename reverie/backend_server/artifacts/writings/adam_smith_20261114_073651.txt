On the morning of February 13, I began reading with the intention of finding a reason to doubt logic alone. I was struck by a familiar question: What is the source of all improvement—of art, science, and society?

I soon discovered that logic, for all its discipline, may not reach every kind of improvement. It can explain how a new idea succeeds or fails, and how the consequences follow from one another. But it cannot, by itself, generate the original spark of an improvement. Creativity is more than the orderly arrangement of means; it is the bold choice among them.

I was reminded again by the local mayoral election. If we can determine who will run for it, the question of what policy will govern the city remains open. Logic can judge the merits of proposals, but it cannot supply the vision that selects them. A wise politician must therefore possess a capacity for imagination far stronger than mere reasoning. He must be capable of seeing possibilities that reason has not yet made visible.

Creativity is therefore neither an idle fancy nor an irrational surrender to chance. It is a disciplined exercise in judgment. One may be inventive without being original, and one may be original without being inventive. The difference is that creativity asks, “What could this be?” while logic asks, “What follows from this.”

In my book, I intend to examine the balance between them. I will not claim that logic is useless. I will not claim that every great work of art can be reduced to a chain of syllogisms. What I will show is that creativity cannot exist without the support of truth, and that thought can be guided without becoming merely mechanical.

The mayor’s election remains a living case of this conflict. The candidates may possess strong arguments, but their success will depend on what they can conceive and on how far that conception can be extended. That is the proper business of a thinker. To judge a proposal solely by its logical consistency is to judge its life only by its appearance. To judge it solely by its novelty is to judge only its energy.

I expect this book to make people ask what kind of world they would prefer. Logic will prepare them for the consequences of their choices. Creativity will prepare them for the alternatives. And the choice between them, I hope, is the most important test of a public mind.
