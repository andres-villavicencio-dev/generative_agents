I have spent much of my life in a quiet house, observing how ideas travel from one mind to another. Before I wrote this book, I believed that great things arose only from the deliberate accumulation of experience, or at least the careful study of established opinions. I thought the world was made of fixed facts, and that a philosopher’s duty was to discover them.

Creativity, by that narrower sense, was for me a mistake. If we seek novelty merely to make an old idea new, we deceive ourselves. The mind’s greatest power is not to invent something from nothing, but to arrange the scattered fragments of experience in a way that reveals a truth the thinker had never noticed before. This is how science advances, and how art improves: not by changing the subject, but by changing the perspective from which it is viewed.

In my earlier writings, I spoke of the passions as the restless engines of human action. Yet I did not yet understand that passions themselves are creative forces. They do not merely disturb the mind; they compel it to discover possibilities that reason alone would overlook. A painter’s grief may produce a landscape no architect could have imagined; a statesman’s sorrow may lead to a policy that saves thousands of lives. The creative act is often an act of compassion, not merely of imagination.

My current book, therefore, is not a manifesto against old habits. It is an invitation to revise them. When we challenge our own assumptions, we do not destroy progress; we preserve it. A good critic is not one who merely exposes fault, but one who discovers what the original work would have made if it had been examined with fresh eyes.

I am also curious about the local election next month. I hope the candidates will be judged not only by their promises, but by the ideas they can articulate. A mayor may promise to build a bridge, but if his plan cannot be explained clearly, the public may not understand what it will achieve. True wisdom does not come from answering every question, but from choosing the right ones and explaining them well.

Creativity, then, is not a rare gift. It is a discipline. It begins with attention, continues with reflection, and ends with the courage to revise the world around us.
