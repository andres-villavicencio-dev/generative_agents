My working argument is that Shakespeare presents morality as a struggle among language, power, and self-deception. Macbeth and Lady Macbeth begin with certainty and ambition, but their language gradually becomes unstable. Macbeth admits, “I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent,” and later calls life “a walking shadow,” revealing that unchecked will has isolated him from moral community. Lady Macbeth’s insistence that a little blood will wash the stain only makes her collapse more tragic: her rigid demand that guilt be silenced produces the very mindlessness she fears.

In Othello, Iago’s persuasive control of language shows how easily ethical judgment can be manipulated. His observation that words have a “pernicious effect” explains his power over Othello, while Emilia’s closing statement, “I know a woman; would I were she,” exposes how gender limits who may understand and speak the truth. In King Lear, the promise that “Nothing will come of nothing” similarly links judgment to consequences. Lear, Gloucester, and Edmund must relinquish certainty before they can recognize suffering and responsibility.
