Wednesday, February 15 began with the sort of quiet I had been hoping for. I woke at six, completed my morning routine, and made breakfast before sitting with my research notes from 6:30 to 7:30. Without classes or appointments competing for my attention, the hour felt spacious rather than empty. I highlighted recurring ideas, questioned them, and wrote down one question to carry into the morning: what changes when Shakespeare’s language is treated not as decoration, but as an active force in the scene?

By eight, I was ready for focused research. I spent three hours with the Shakespeare texts and criticism, following the connections and disagreements between passages instead of simply collecting quotations. I like this part of the process because it asks more of me than recognition; understanding a work means noticing its tensions and being willing to sit with uncertainty. At eleven, I closed my notebook with a clearer argument and a short list of sources to consult later.

After lunch, from noon to one, I took a restorative break. I stepped away from the screen, stretched, and let my mind settle. Then I returned to the senior-thesis draft. Between one and four, I revised the second section, moving its central claim earlier, making the sequence of evidence more deliberate, and removing repeated explanations that weakened the momentum. I also tightened a few overly formal sentences and checked that each quotation served a distinct analytical purpose. By four, the section was leaner, sharper, and more honest about what the evidence can actually prove.

At five, dinner and self-care gave me room to reset. I cooked, ate slowly, and used the evening to care for myself rather than fill every minute. From eight to ten, I read leisurely and prepared for bed. I went to bed around ten, grateful that an open day did not feel unproductive. The progress was quiet, but it was real: I moved the thesis forward, trusted my own judgment, and ended the day with enough energy to begin again tomorrow.
