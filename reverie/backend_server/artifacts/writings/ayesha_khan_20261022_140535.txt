Today I returned to the third act of Hamlet and noticed how differently the play sounds when I stop treating Shakespeare’s language as decoration. Every repetition carries intention. Characters interrupt, question, evade, and reinterpret one another, and the words seem to move across the stage like a living argument. I have always believed that good literature rewards close attention, but lately I am learning that attention means resisting the urge to settle on the first interpretation that feels satisfying.

For my thesis, I am examining how language shapes conflict in Shakespeare’s plays, especially when characters use ambiguity to gain control or reveal vulnerability. Tonight I revised the section on dramatic irony. When the audience understands more than a character does, even ordinary language becomes unstable. Every statement can be read on two levels: as what the character believes and as what we know to be true. I rewrote several paragraphs because my earlier argument was too general. I needed specific examples, clearer transitions, and a more honest connection between word choice and characterization.

I also worked on my notes for Macbeth. The witches’ language is deliberately strange, yet their lines invite familiar human fears. Their predictions do not create tragedy; they give Macbeth permission to pursue choices that were already possible. That distinction matters. Shakespeare does not present language as magic working independently of human responsibility. Instead, words influence perception, and perception influences action.

One problem I kept running into was balancing close reading with historical context. I do not want to separate Shakespeare’s language from the world in which it was written, but I also do not want the historical material to overwhelm the text itself. I reorganized my outline so that each section begins with a passage and then moves outward toward the social and philosophical questions surrounding it. This structure feels stronger because it keeps the reader close to the words while still asking larger questions.

I am not fully satisfied with the conclusion yet. It remains too cautious, as though I am protecting myself from making a claim that might not hold up. But a thesis should invite disagreement, not hide from it. Tomorrow I will sharpen that argument.

It is 10:00 p.m., and my mind is still circling through Shakespeare’s sentences. I am tired, but proud of the work I have done today. I will close my notebook, sleep, and return to the argument with fresh eyes in the morning.
