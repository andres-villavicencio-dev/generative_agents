On Monday, February 13, I arrived at Oak Hill College with the same curiosity that has kept me studying since I first opened a Shakespearean text. I want to write a senior thesis about how language shapes the meaning of plays, particularly in the way characters speak, how they reveal their hidden motives, and how repetition and variation carry emotional weight.

The revised body of my thesis must reflect this focus more consistently. I need to compare different plays without relying on the same set of phrases or citations. For example, I should distinguish between the language of character dialogue and the language of stage directions, and between Shakespeare’s direct speech and his use of indirection. My citations should be accurate, including page references and parenthetical notes where needed. I also want to check that every repeated concept has the same supporting evidence.

This is not just an academic exercise. Writing a thesis gives me the structure I need to understand how I organize my thoughts. I can identify which arguments are strongest and which need further development. I can also use my writing class to practice clearer, more precise language.

I still want to explore more literary works. Reading aloud to a group, discussing difficult passages, and revising drafts have helped me notice patterns that I could not see while reading alone. I hope to discover how different genres use language to create meaning.

I should also allow myself rest. I go to bed around 10 p.m. and wake up at 6 a.m., so I need to preserve enough time for reflection and revision. The next several days will be busy, but I will make sure to keep the thesis focused on clear, consistent terminology and properly supported claims.
