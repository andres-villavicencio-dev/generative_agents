Revising My Senior Thesis: Language, Gentrification, and the Future of Housing

I’ve been revising my senior thesis lately, and I’m thinking about how language can reveal the changing nature of our neighborhoods. I’m particularly interested in how gentrification, rising housing costs, and reduced affordable options are reshaping communities. These issues are rarely discussed in a vacuum, but they appear in every layer of everyday life—from the way people name a street to the language used in real estate listings.

My thesis asks how Shakespeare’s plays reflect language and social change. I’m especially curious about how different characters negotiate identity and belonging. In Macbeth, for example, language shapes power and responsibility. Macbeth’s repeated use of “powerful” suggests that authority is not simply inherited—it must be earned and constantly questioned. In Romeo and Juliet, love and language are inextricably linked. Romeo’s passionate declarations show that emotional truth can resist rigid classification, while Juliet’s refusal to accept a marriage arranged by her family reflects a desire to define her own life on her own terms.

This brings me back to the present. When I write about housing, I want to connect Shakespeare’s questions about identity with the realities of today’s cities. Rising rents, displacement, and limited options can make language itself a tool of exclusion. A neighborhood may still have a name, but the people who live there may no longer share the community’s history or values.

I’m still refining my argument, and I’m wondering whether my thesis balances literary analysis with contemporary social context. I want to avoid treating Shakespeare as a mirror of the present while also avoiding a purely historical reading. Perhaps the strongest insight comes from asking: if language shaped how power was exercised in the past, how might it still shape who has access to housing today?

Revising is helping me make that argument clearer. I’m also learning more about writing, which makes my research feel less like a collection of observations and more like a coherent conversation between literature and social change. I’m not giving up, and I’m still finding the words that matter most.
