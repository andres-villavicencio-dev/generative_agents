Tuesday, February 14. The city keeps its quiet rhythm while I carry more questions than I thought I could handle.

I woke at six and made the morning routine before the world had finished waking up. By seven, I had coffee, a quick glance at my notes, and the familiar order of my day. Tuesday feels different because the demands of my research are not neatly arranged. Shakespeare’s language is a living thing, and gentrification is another one. Both seem connected through the spaces they occupy, through names, through the way people move in and out of places.

At breakfast, I looked at the map on the table. It showed neighborhoods I had studied, schools I had attended, and the streets where I had lived. For the first time this week, the map felt less like a record of places I had known and more like a map of something I had not.

My thesis is about how language shapes meaning, but the ideas in it are beginning to press against me from several directions. A line from a play might mean different things depending on which neighborhood’s history it is speaking about. A word can carry the weight of what has been lost, what has been bought, what has been sold, and what remains. I cannot separate the literary argument from the human one.

I spent the afternoon in the library, tracing quotations and comparing them against the changing faces in the photographs. I found myself comparing a passage on the balcony of a theater to a notice about a new housing project nearby. The words were the same, and the places were different.

By five, I had eaten dinner and sat down with my phone. I had three more pages to write. I had one assignment to finish. But somewhere along the way, I felt the floor beneath me loosen. The purpose I had carried forward was steady enough to keep me from falling, but steady was not enough to carry me through the places I was trying to understand.

I put the phone down. I would begin tomorrow. Not all at once. But slowly, carefully, with the right words.
