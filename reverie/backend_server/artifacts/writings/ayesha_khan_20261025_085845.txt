Tuesday, February 14

The sun woke my apartment before I did. I stepped out into the quiet street, feeling the same steady purpose I carried into every other part of my day. My schedule was already full: classes, the senior thesis, creative writing, meals, reflection, social connections, and leisure. I had no fixed appointment this morning, only enough room to move without becoming lost.

I finished the morning routine at 6:00 a.m. and immediately turned to the work I had been focusing on. For a few minutes, I read a short passage in which Shakespeare uses language to reveal how people are forced to choose between what they want to say and what they are willing to endure. In the text, words become weapons or shields. A young man speaks about freedom, yet his language is cautious. He fears the consequences of his words.

That observation made me pause. Language is not just decoration; it is how we navigate difficult situations. It can protect, persuade, or imprison. It can make a small act feel monumental or trivial.

At the same time, my thesis was growing. I had been thinking about how the same themes—identity, displacement, belonging, the cost of speaking—appear in gentrification and housing. I wanted to make the connection clearer. I did not want to treat the two subjects as unrelated. Gentrification often creates a new neighborhood, but it also changes who belongs there. People lose their homes and the spaces where they can live. Literature can help us see that loss, and it can also help us imagine alternatives.

Between classes and creative writing, I took a brief walk. The street was busy, yet I felt grounded. I had not made a decision, and that was enough. I could keep moving forward.

By 10:00 p.m., the apartment felt quiet, and I had finished my writing. I went to bed around ten. Tomorrow, I would wake at six, eat dinner at five, and begin the creative piece that had been waiting for me. No appointment could be confirmed, but I had the time. I had the room. And I had a purpose.
