My senior thesis is finally coming together. I’ve been comparing the language in Shakespeare’s Macbeth with the play’s treatment of jealousy, and I’m beginning to see how the words can reveal a character’s deepest fears. I noticed how Macbeth uses words of power—“thou,” “thou shalt,” “thou shalt not”—to make his ambition feel almost physical. The repetition makes his obsession feel inevitable, as if he cannot stop speaking the same dangerous words.

At the same time, I wonder whether the repetition also shows him trapped. He speaks only what he wants others to hear, so his language becomes a performance. Even when he says “I will not,” the word “will” carries the same weight as the first “thou” in his soliloquy. Jealousy does not just make a character angry; it turns speech into something more dangerous.

I have been studying this at Oak Hill College from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. and then continuing at the library after class. I’ve read passages in Macbeth and Hamlet, looking for how the language changes as the scenes move. I am particularly interested in how Shakespeare creates a sense of unease through repetition, rhythm, and the way characters address one another.

On Monday morning, I walked into the library. The quiet was pleasant, but I felt the pressure of research pressing against me. I found a passage where Lady Macbeth calls her husband a “curse,” while Macbeth describes his wife as “holy.” The contrast made the tension feel immediate. I wrote that language could make a character feel both powerful and vulnerable.

I also noticed how the language changes in later scenes. The speeches become shorter, more urgent, and less polite. I think this mirrors how jealousy can turn affection into cruelty. The language does not simply describe the conflict; it participates in it.

By the time I left the library, I felt more confident than I had this morning. I had found a thread to connect the plays, and I was beginning to understand how a single word can change the entire scene. I still have work to do, but I am not quite as lost as I was before.

My thesis is becoming clearer, and so are the questions I still have. I will keep reading, comparing, and writing, but I will also remember that language is not the truth. It is the truth we choose to hear.
