On Friday, February 17, I wrote a short entry in my thesis journal about how Macbeth’s cold, impersonal language can exclude the original residents of the community. The first scene opens with a cold opening that creates a sense of isolation: Macbeth enters as a stranger, and the language surrounding him is distant, formal, and transactional. This creates a cold tone that feels almost hostile toward anyone who is not immediately useful to him. The repetition of words such as “blood,” “honor,” and “king” reinforces a rigid moral framework that demands obedience and ownership rather than genuine inclusion.

Macbeth’s coldness also makes the original inhabitants feel invisible. The language of ownership, possession, and hierarchy treats land and people as resources to be controlled rather than places of shared history. This language excludes those who have lived in the community for generations. The cold tone suggests that the original residents belong to no one and are merely obstacles to be removed. Their histories, perspectives, and relationships to the land are dismissed in the same way that Macbeth dismisses his own actions.

By the end of the scene, the original residents appear cold and disconnected. They are not given a voice, and their presence is treated as something to be managed rather than respected. I think this reflects a larger theme in gentrification: newcomers are welcomed, but the original community is seen as an obstacle to be overcome. The cold tone of the play makes this exclusion feel natural and inevitable.

I will carry this observation forward in my thesis. I am especially interested in how Shakespeare’s impersonal, transactional language can shape how people define ownership and belonging. Understanding this connection will help me analyze the community’s experience of change.
