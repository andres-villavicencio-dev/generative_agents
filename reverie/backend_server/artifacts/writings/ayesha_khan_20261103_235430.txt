After my second class, I headed to the reading room with a purpose. I wanted to find a passage that would feel like a sharp contrast to something Shakespeare wrote. I chose The Tempest, which uses language to build mystery and emotional distance, while I considered The Great Gatsby for its lyrical, almost musical use of words.

In The Tempest, Prospero is a master of shifting identities. He begins as a jealous, powerful Duke, then becomes a magician, a servant, a lover, and finally a father figure. His language is deliberately theatrical, with names, stages, and symbols that transform the play’s mood. Each scene begins with a new title, and Prospero’s speech becomes more poetic as his role changes. The style suggests that identity can be performed, hidden, and rewritten.

In contrast, The Great Gatsby uses language to create an uncanny, dreamlike atmosphere. Nick Carraway describes his experiences with a steady, almost detached precision. He observes the parties and the parties’ conversations while remaining emotionally at a distance. The prose feels detached and polished, with long, carefully constructed sentences that slow down the pace. Daisy’s words, for instance, shift from casual familiarity to something almost reverent, revealing her desire without immediately revealing her desperation.

The difference is striking. Shakespeare relies on repetition, bold imagery, and layered irony to change the meaning of a scene. Gatsby’s language tends toward repetition of the same objects and people, combined with a lyrical restraint that keeps the reader’s attention on the setting and the people rather than on a clear moral judgment.

I found the contrast satisfying. Shakespeare can reveal a character’s inner change through a single line, while Gatsby’s narrator can make the same character’s transformation feel gradual and unrecognizable. Both writers use language to shape how we understand the world, but Shakespeare tends toward psychological complexity, whereas Gatsby emphasizes the distance between the past and the present.

As I leave, I’m not sure which passage will carry me farther. Perhaps the strongest lesson is not in choosing the better style, but in noticing how each language creates its own kind of reality. Language is not simply the words of a story; it is the way a story lives.
