Tuesday, February 14, 6:00 AM

I woke before the alarm and looked out the window. The city was still dark, but I could hear the distant sounds of people beginning the day. I brushed my teeth, made my bed, and opened my laptop.

My senior thesis was already on my page: Shakespeare’s institutional language and its connection to real-world issues, especially displacement and community cohesion. I wanted to explore whether the language of power, ritual, and authority could also be understood as a language of belonging.

I ate breakfast quickly—the toast, the tea, and a few pages. Then I began reading Macbeth. I did not want to study Shakespeare as a mere text; I wanted to understand how he shaped the world around him. The language was not only polished or poetic. It was a tool of control.

At breakfast, a friend asked, “What are you researching?”

“I’m working on a senior thesis,” I said, setting my mug down. “Shakespeare’s institutional language.”

“You mean words of power?”

“Exactly. How do they shape people, institutions, and community?”

We laughed. Then we talked about how I’d been writing my scenes, how the dialogue felt stiff, and how I was trying to give the characters voices. I told her about reading Macbeth, about the way the language seemed to dictate what was possible, and about how displacement could make any language feel hollow.

She leaned forward, her eyes bright. “You’ve been thinking a lot about power,” she said. “What if it’s not just about making people fearful?”

I paused. “Maybe,” I said, “about reminding people of the structures they carry.”

She smiled. “That’s a good thesis. I’ll be the first person to read it.”

We finished dinner together, sharing a plate and a conversation that felt less like a class and more like the kind of intellectual exchange I’ve always wanted. Outside, the city continued moving. I sat with my laptop, still thinking about how words can both build and divide.
