I woke at six and found the sun already moving across the window. Breakfast was a little rushed this morning, but Klaus insisted on sharing it anyway. He was trying to impress me with how fast he could prepare, which was cute, though I was more interested in what he said than the speed.

By seven, I had finished my oatmeal and sat down to review what I had written about Lady Macbeth. I thought about how her guilt became more than a reaction to the murder—it was a steady pressure, a way of controlling everything by controlling others. She believed she could decide what was happening, but the plays show her slowly losing that control.

I wanted to add more evidence, but I also wanted to keep the argument from becoming too complicated. Instead, I focused on one scene: the storm outside and the words she speaks. Her repeated insistence that she has “no hair” is not merely a metaphor for her shame. It reveals how she treats her guilt as an object she can examine, clean, and possess. She wants to define her responsibility before she can truly accept it.

The analysis became clearer when I asked, “Why does she become more ambitious than she was before?” Her ambition is not simply “to take control.” It is also a way of preserving her identity. By taking on the role of Macbeth, she can make mistakes that protect her from being alone with her own conscience.

I revised the section by removing the repetition of her guilt and replacing it with a direct comparison between her earlier language and her later actions. The contrast is stronger when I connect each change in speech to a corresponding shift in accountability.

This revision helps me see the argument more clearly. I am also considering whether to add a second example, perhaps from the banquet scene, where she makes decisions that endanger everyone around her. That might make the pattern more evident, but I am still uncertain whether the evidence is strong enough to support the larger claim.

I plan to check the scene closely, revise the analysis, and write a short paragraph comparing her guilt to her ambition. I hope this practice session will make the process feel less like a test and more like a conversation with the text.
