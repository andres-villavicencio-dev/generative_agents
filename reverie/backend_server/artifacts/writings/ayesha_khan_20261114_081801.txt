The library at Oak Hill College is quiet on Monday, February 13, save for the soft tapping of pages and the occasional rustle of a chair being pushed back. I settle into my leather chair, the coffee table beside me, and open the Shakespeare collection. By the time the sun goes down, my notes will be full.

I choose to begin with Othello. The passage about Iago’s “little hand” is small, yet it holds the weight of the whole scene. Iago describes his hand as “little,” but the word does not mean small. It suggests delicacy, perhaps fragility, perhaps a hand too clean to belong to anyone. Iago uses that description to make his cruelty seem almost natural, as if the hand itself is innocent. A small detail, repeated in a different context, becomes the key to the entire play.

The language here is precise, almost musical. Iago’s speech is smooth and polished, with words that feel like they have been polished themselves. The repetition of “little,” “hand,” and “false” makes him feel like a character who has been speaking the same word with different tones. Iago’s voice is not entirely evil, at least not always. It is clever, confident, and surprisingly persuasive. He does not shout; he persuades, and persuasion is often harder to detect than violence.

The tone is curious and determined. I have never fully understood why Iago would say these things. But curiosity is doing the work. I want to know what Iago knows that I do not. I want to know what he thinks, and whether his thoughts are honest. Determination is also part of this passage. Iago believes he is doing the right thing. He believes that honesty is sometimes a word. He does not always ask what others believe. His confidence is not necessarily bravery, and his success is not always justified. But Iago is a character who will never admit defeat.

Writing is becoming clearer to me. Iago’s language has revealed itself as a map rather than merely decoration. It shows how a single word can be used to control the direction of an entire scene. In literature, that is what language does: it shapes what we feel and how we understand the world.

I close the book. By ten, the library will be dark, and I will want to go home. But I will not close the book. I will reread the passage again. I will ask what Iago means by “little.” I will consider whether his cruelty is a flaw or a method. I will write my thesis, and I will write it with care. A good thesis does not simply summarize a play. It discovers what the play makes us question.

I may have only a few hours left before bed. I will write now, carefully, and quietly.
