Seven in the morning and the house is still blue with sleep. Coffee tastes like dirt, so I drink it anyway. Damn February. The tree outside my window has one stubborn bird sitting in it, refusing to explain itself. I respect that. Probably the only thing in this neighborhood that does.

I keep thinking about my grandmother’s garden. She touched every leaf like she was asking permission. I laughed at her once, said the plants looked hopeless, and she said, “You talk too much to know what silence means.” Brutal old woman. She was right. That sentence has been following me for years, showing up in workshops, in my notebooks, in the pauses where I should’ve said something real. I still talk too much. I fill rooms because quiet makes me feel like I’m disappearing. But when I write, sometimes the words get out of my way.

I drafted a poem about bees yesterday. Bees with swollen orange bodies dragging spring across the air. Then the workshop leader said it was “competent but emotionally evasive,” which felt like being kicked with a polished shoe. I wanted to tell him to shove the observation up his elegant ass. Instead I rewrote the whole stanza about my uncle swatting at bees on the porch, laughing while my aunt screamed, and suddenly the poem had a pulse. Maybe honesty is the art teacher I keep refusing.

What keeps coming back? Rain on tin roofs. The smell of wet bark. My father’s hands cracking in winter. The sound of my bicycle chain clicking when I was nine and thought every hill was an mountain. I don’t remember what happened that day, but my body remembers the climb.

Here’s the question I hate: am I actually observing nature, or am I using it to decorate my wounds? Can a poem be beautiful and still be hiding something ugly? I think so. I think most beautiful things are running from the same weather I am.

The bird is still there. Still silent. I open the window, and it gives me one sharp look.

Good. Let’s begin.
