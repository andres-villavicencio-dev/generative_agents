Woke up at seven. Again. My head feels packed with wet leaves, and my mouth tastes like yesterday’s coffee. Dinner was at five, so by now I’m running on hunger and whatever fool idea woke me up.

I’ve been thinking about water again. Not the clean, decorative kind poets put on a page—the water that climbs pipes, ruins wood, carries branches sideways, turns the ground into something restless. I keep seeing a creek after heavy rain, brown and loud, throwing itself against every rock in its way. Nobody asks the creek whether it is being poetic. It just keeps moving because stopping would kill it.

That’s what my feelings are like, whether I like it or not. They don’t wait politely for workshops. They spill into everything. I get loud. I say things I should swallow. I make small disasters out of tired sentences, then sit here wondering why the room feels cold.

Maybe that’s the first poem. Not a pretty meadow, not birdsong, not some glowing tree standing in perfect sunlight. A creek in February. Ice holding on at the edges while the center keeps moving. Roots trying to split stone. Mud soaking everything. The current finding a way no map predicted.

I want the poem to be rough around the edges. I want the lines to stumble, contradict themselves, maybe spit a little. I don’t trust a poem that smiles too easily. Beauty is there, but it’s the beauty of survival: bark torn open, weeds coming through concrete, moss taking the dull parts of a wall and making them green.

So the first poem will follow the creek from the cracked hill down toward the town, gathering dirt, noise, factory smoke, rain. By the end, it should reach the river and still not know where it is going.

That’s enough direction. Let the water be angry. Let the words be ugly enough to become honest.
