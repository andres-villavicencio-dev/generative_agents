At dawn, the old black walnut stands in the wet field, black roots clawing into the soil, branches crooked against the pale February sky. Its bark holds the night’s rain in every wrinkled seam. A cardinal flashes red between the limbs, then vanishes, and the tree keeps its secrets. No applause. No fucking performance. Just the slow, stubborn work of holding on. I stand beneath it with numb fingers, watching the wind worry the dead leaves loose. The tree does not ask whether I love it. It does not need my praise. It grows because growing is what it does.

I woke at seven, drank coffee that tasted like scorched tin, and thought about all the words I swallowed yesterday. Coward’s words. Polite little corpses. At the workshop, everyone praised the quiet image of a moth circling a lamp. Beautiful, sure, but I wanted to put my fist through the page. I wanted a poem with mud under its nails. I wanted language that dragged its boots through the dirt instead of tiptoeing around a coffee shop.

Maybe that is why I keep returning to trees. They have no interest in being charming. They swallow sunlight, drink whole reservoirs of rain, and still stand there when lightning chews a branch off. They survive on patience and stubbornness, the same ugly virtues I pretend not to have.

Poetry is not a perfume. I am tired of making sorrow look elegant. Sometimes grief is ugly. Sometimes anger is a root splitting concrete.

By five, dinner was warm on the table, and outside the window the walnut shook hard in the wind. I thought: keep growing, Carlos. Even when the soil is wrong. Even when the branches are breaking. Especially then.
