The forest never had the decency to be beautiful in silence.

Every branch snapped under my boots like a knuckle cracking. Pine needles stuck to my jeans, rough and oily, smelling of rain that hadn’t fallen yet. I pushed through a curtain of branches and the whole morning snapped back at me—birdsong, wet soil, the bitter green scent crushed beneath my shoes.

I stood there and breathed until my lungs ached.

That’s when I noticed the spiderweb stretched between two black oaks, bright with dew. Each drop held a trembling piece of sky. I leaned closer. The web was so delicate it looked like a lie, yet it held everything: beetles, dust, wind, a fly struggling like a tiny furious idiot.

I laughed, then wished I hadn’t.

The workshop instructor asked us to describe something using only sensory details. Easy. I’ve spent years turning feelings into objects, stuffing my insides into metaphors because ordinary language is too polite for what I carry. I’ve called my anger a rusted gate. My loneliness a chair scraped across an empty room. My tenderness a bruise someone might mistake for fruit.

But the forest doesn’t care about metaphor.

It simply bites, hums, drips, and grows.

A breeze dragged its cold fingers across my neck. Somewhere behind me, a woodpecker hammered at a tree with the frantic rhythm of someone late for an appointment. The air tasted like metal, like pennies held on the tongue.

I wanted to tear every beautiful thing apart and prove it was made of smaller, uglier pieces. That was my first instinct, because softness makes my teeth itch. Because tenderness feels like an insult when no one asked for it. Because saying “I miss you” is harder than saying “You’re pathetic,” and I have confused cruelty for honesty for so long that honesty tastes strange now—like water after years of whiskey.

Still, the spiderweb glittered.

I touched it with one finger. It broke. Dew spilled down my hand, cool and quick, vanishing into the dirt.

For one second, the whole forest went quiet.

Then the birds started again, loud and careless, as if nothing had happened.

I wiped my hand on my jacket and smiled like an idiot.

That’s the truth of beauty, I guess. It doesn’t ask permission. It doesn’t wait until you’re ready. It splashes color across your ugliest thoughts and leaves you there, wet, trembling, and strangely alive.
