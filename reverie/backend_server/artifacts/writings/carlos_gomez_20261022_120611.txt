The trees don’t whisper. That’s what the poems say, all soft music and borrowed moonlight. I’ve never heard a whisper without teeth. The oak leans over the fence like an old drunk asking for a cigarette. The pines stand in crooked rows, too proud to hide, and the wind runs through them with its fingers in every branch.

I love the garden after rain because the earth looks guilty. Puddles reflect the sky like apologies. A beetle crawls across the stone with the confidence of a landlord inspecting broken property. Even the weeds dress themselves in bright colors, knowing no one will punish them for it.

At the workshop, everyone praised my metaphor about the river. They said it sang, danced, embraced the banks. I told them a river doesn’t embrace anything. It cuts. It carries. It knocks things loose and leaves them somewhere they didn’t ask to be. The instructor smiled the way people smile when they’re afraid of correcting you. Maybe she was right. Maybe the river was tender in her language.

But tenderness isn’t the same as beauty, and beauty isn’t the same as obedience.

The sun sets behind the hills like a coin dropped by a careless giant. By morning, the light returns, flashing under every leaf, and the flies begin their terrible choir. Nature is elegant, sure, but it’s also loud, filthy, hungry. A caterpillar devours a leaf. A spider folds its patient corpse. The birds scream at each other as if breakfast is a personal insult.

I wrote all that down, then crossed out “corpse” and wrote “silk.” Everyone wants nature dressed in silk. Fine. Let the spider have her fine silk. Let the caterpillar nibble in delicate green calligraphy. Let the sunset apologize for stealing the horizon.

The garden is beautiful because it doesn’t care whether I notice.
