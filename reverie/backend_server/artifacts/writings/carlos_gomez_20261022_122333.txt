The trees don’t ask permission to grow,  
and neither do I.  
I wake at seven, stomping through the cold,  
while February chews the sun  
and spits it out in scraps of ice.  
Good. Let the sky be rude.  
I have spent too long being polite  
to my own thoughts,  
polishing every rough edge  
until the words forgot how to bite.

By night, I will lie down at ten,  
close my loud little eyes,  
and listen to the dark  
argue with the roots.  
I will dream of rivers carrying leaves,  
of birds stitching gold across the blue,  
of forests breathing deeper than me.

I love this world  
the way a storm loves a fence:  
violently, beautifully,  
without giving a damn.  
I love the mud under my nails,  
the damp earth clinging to my shoes,  
the way moss drinks in silence  
while I shout praise at the rain.

Somewhere, a fox drags its hunger  
through the white grass,  
and I understand.  
Nothing soft survives  
without teeth.  
Even a flower opens its petals  
like a trap.

So I will write this stanza  
with sharp lines and crooked grace,  
a little venom in the verses,  
a little thunder in the rhyme.  
Let the words scratch.  
Let them leave marks.  
The birds keep singing anyway,  
and so do I—  
loud, impossible, alive.
