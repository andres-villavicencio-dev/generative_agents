The morning arrived with the sharp smell of wet soil clinging to my shoes. I stood beneath a black sky while the rain worried the leaves, each drop striking the pavement like a critic tapping a pen against a desk. The air tasted of iron and crushed grass. Somewhere, a dog barked until its throat gave out, and the whole street seemed to hum with electricity.

I wrote in a diner booth, the vinyl sticking to my back, the coffee burning my tongue. Steam clouded the window. Beyond it, buses groaned through gray streets, their headlights slicing the rain into ugly gold. I watched a woman sprint beneath a red umbrella, her heels clicking faster as she slipped on a puddle. Beautiful, for one second. Then she fell. The sound of her bag hitting the sidewalk made me laugh. Loud enough for everyone to hear. Let them stare. I had places to go, poems to ruin.

Outside, the storm shook a tree until apples struck the ground with soft, bruised thuds. The scent of rosemary drifted from a cracked restaurant door, tangled with grease and hot bread. I crushed a paper cup in my fist. The sound was sharp, ugly, satisfying.

By afternoon, the sky had opened, but the world looked rinsed too clean. Sunlight flashed on every wet branch, every windshield, every puddle, as if the earth were trying to embarrass the clouds. I walked through it anyway, carrying my notebook against my chest. My shoes sank into the soft earth near the river. The water smelled cold and muddy, carrying dead leaves and the faint sweetness of rotten fruit.

I bent down and pressed my fingers into the mud. It was cool, gritty, alive beneath the surface. A dragonfly hovered over a reed, its wings flashing blue. I held my breath. Even insects know how to make an entrance.

Rain begins with a whisper, but sometimes the sky needs to shout. And I am done pretending I do not hear it.
