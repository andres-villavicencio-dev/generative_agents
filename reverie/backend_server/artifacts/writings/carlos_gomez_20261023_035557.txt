I arrived at the workshop with the room already full of people who sounded like they were trying to sell me something I didn’t want. I wanted to be heard. I wanted to talk about the poems I’d written in my head for years—about rain on cracked pavement, a squirrel stealing a seed, the way a dead bird can still move when the wind moves through the branches. That’s what I came here for.

The instructor read a poem about a river changing color. Her voice was smooth. Calm. Professional. I wanted to scream into the microphone. I wanted to say, “This is not a lesson! You’re making this pretty!”

She finished reading and smiled. She asked me to read my first draft aloud.

I stood in the middle of the room, my voice cracking under the weight of my own expectations. I had practiced reading it three times. I practiced breathing. I practiced speaking so the words would not feel like accusations.

I read it again.

“I was told that beauty is something I could find if I only knew what to look for,” I said.

The room went quiet. A few people laughed. I didn’t care.

I read it again.

“I found beauty in the way an old oak refused to fall down,” I said.

Another laugh.

I read it again.

“I found beauty in the silence between the words,” I said.

I read it again.

This was the first time I had spoken to anyone who was not trying to make me look smart. I had spoken about the river. I had spoken about the squirrel. I had spoken about what I did not like about the workshop, about my own impatience, about the people who thought being loud was a virtue.

I wanted to be rude. I wanted to be loud. I wanted to make everyone uncomfortable so they would notice that I was here.

I wanted to be heard.

And now I had.
