I woke up at seven because the sun was already pulling the curtains. I drank a glass of water and checked the phone. Then I opened my notebook and wrote down everything I could remember about that day.

Today was another workshop. I was supposed to listen, not dominate. The instructor said we were supposed to practice reading aloud. I wanted to hear the voice. I wanted to hear the poem. I wanted to make sure every line landed without shouting.

I showed up. I took my seat. I looked at the page and saw words I had written before—words I could not yet let go of.

The poet told me that reading aloud is not just practice. It is honesty. It is asking, “What do I really mean when I say this?” I had never thought about it that way. I had thought of reading aloud as a way to sound good. A way to prove I belonged in a room full of people.

I sat there and listened to my own voice.

It was loud. It was rough. It sounded like a teenager who had forgotten how to be quiet. The instructor gave me feedback: “Your rhythm is strong, but your voice is too exposed. You are telling the room how you feel rather than letting the poem make the room feel.”

I looked down at the paper. I saw the words I loved. I saw the ideas I had spent three days trying to make work. I wanted to fight them. I wanted to turn them into noise.

But I stopped.

I took a breath. I lowered my shoulders. I began again.

I read the first stanza aloud. The room waited. I did not speak over it. I did not make it mine. I let it pass through me, and then through me, and through the whole room.

By the end of the lesson, I did not come away feeling better. I came away tired. I came away with a poem I had not yet understood, but one I would carry.

That was my goal. Not to be perfect. Not to be loud. But to remember what I meant when I wrote it down.
