The tree doesn’t beg for mercy.

It takes what it needs, pulls roots down, and sends leaves up. I watched it last night. The wind was sharp, the sky gray, and I felt small. Like a leaf caught on a branch that was too impatient to let it hang. That’s why I started writing about the trees. Not because they’re pretty. They’re ugly in the way a wound is: scarred, raw, refusing to close. But beauty isn’t pretty. Beauty is how they survive.

I started with a draft I wrote when I was twenty. It was full of pretty nouns and smooth sentences. “The oak stood like a sentinel,” I wrote. “The moss whispered secrets.” I liked it. It sounded like poetry. But it sounded like someone talking to themselves. I wanted to make the trees feel real. Not like a poem. Like something alive. Something that could stand under my finger and say, I saw you.

I kept the lines I wrote about light. The way sunlight turns the bark gold. The silence between branches. I kept the lines about rain. Not the rain that makes a sound, but the rain that makes the ground feel heavy. I wrote down how the wind moves through the leaves, how it carries the smell of old earth. I wrote about the trees not as symbols, but as people who carry too much.

At the workshop, the instructor asked us to strip our work. I took out the lines that sounded too polished. I deleted the ones that sounded polite to the trees. I kept the ones that made me pause.

Now I’m rewriting the first draft. I’m not trying to fix every sentence. I’m trying to make the trees sound like them. Not perfect, not loud, not pretending. Just present.

And maybe, just maybe, the poem will feel less like a lecture and more like a conversation.

I’ll sleep at ten. I’ll rise at seven. Dinner at five. I’ll write in the dark. And when the sun comes up, I’ll look at the trees again. I’ll see them still standing. And I’ll write one more stanza.
