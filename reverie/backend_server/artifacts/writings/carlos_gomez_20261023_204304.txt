The workshop ended before I could say thanks, and the instructor called it a “good session.” I didn’t hear one word of appreciation. I heard the echo of a sentence I’d written in another language, then deleted it because it sounded too polite.

I stood in the corner, watching people who should have been laughing at something stupid. One woman held up a notebook. “I wrote about a storm,” she said. “It was beautiful.” She looked at the rain on the page. Then she looked at me.

I laughed, loud and ugly. “You wrote that because you wanted the room to believe in rain,” I said. “It’s not rain. It’s just water moving across the ground.”

She didn’t flinch. She picked up her pen and wrote something that sounded like a map.

I walked over and said, “This is the kind of poetry I’m trying to write.”

She smiled. “Maybe.”

“Maybe,” I repeated. “Then let’s go home, and I’ll read the ending to the people who care. If it makes them cry, good. If it makes them roll over in their beds, I’ll read it again. And if nobody laughs at the end, maybe I’ll write something else.”

She stared at me for a long time. Then she smiled. “I think you’re dangerous, Carlos.”

“Dangerous?” I said, pointing at the rain. “Poetry is just truth wearing a coat.”

“Truth can be boring,” she said. “Try not to be boring tomorrow.”

I left before anyone could ask what I meant. I went to the kitchen and opened the window. Rain hit the glass, cold and fast. The city outside was turning into a picture of wet streets and angry clouds.

I wanted to say everything I had been holding in my chest for months. I wanted to say how tired I was, how stupid I was, how I had been pretending to understand the world while everyone kept asking me what I thought about nature.

But I didn’t say it.

Instead, I wrote a poem.

It wasn’t about storms.

It was about the way a leaf moves through water.

It was about how the world can look so beautiful, and how the person who sees it may only know a little about what beauty actually is.

I read it aloud.

The room didn’t cheer.

One man raised his hand and said, “That’s not very brave.”

I laughed, again. Loud. Ugly.

“It’s not,” I said. “It’s honest.”

He didn’t argue.

I went home and turned the page.
