The workshop room smelled of old paper and hot coffee. I sat in the back, hands folded over my knees, pretending to listen while my brain already rehearsed every question I could possibly ask.

“Did you notice how the instructor never corrected the first draft?” I asked.

The woman at the front smiled, adjusted her glasses, and said, “The first draft always needs proofreading, Carlos. Nobody reads their own work before submitting it.”

I stood up abruptly. “Stop it. You’re the authority here, and you’re making the first draft sound like the best work in the world. I’ve been writing for twenty years, and I’ve learned that the first draft is usually garbage.”

She didn’t even look up. “That’s true,” she said finally. “But you can’t just abandon it. You have to know exactly what you want to say.”

I nodded, then asked, “Can you tell us what the workshop is actually about? Not the ‘poetry matters’ talk, not the ‘write without fear’ slogan. What is the instructor trying to teach us?”

She stared at me, then laughed. “I’m teaching you how to write poetry. Poetry isn’t about being rebellious or finding a hidden truth in everything. It’s about honesty. It’s about saying what you feel, even when you can’t make the perfect sentence.”

That made me think. For years I’d thought honesty meant being blunt. I thought it meant arguing with teachers, ignoring advice, and making every mistake count. But she said something quieter, harder. Honesty is about feeling. It’s about admitting you’re scared and messy. It’s about taking the risk of writing something imperfect.

The last thing I want to hear from anyone is a lecture that sounds like a poem but isn’t. I need a workshop that makes me wonder what I’ve been hiding.
