The room smelled like old paper and wet wool. Everyone was talking about how the poem felt wrong. I had to admit it.

The opening sentence felt like a man who wakes up angry and tries to explain it with polite grammar. “I woke up before the moon had finished drinking the rain,” I said. “That’s the first line.”

A woman across from me smiled like she’d just solved a puzzle. “No, Carlos. That’s the first line of poetry that sounds like a complaint letter.”

I stared at her. I hated that. I hated the way people made me feel like I was trying to be clever when I was just terrified to write down what was true.

I finished the draft anyway. The lines didn’t make sense. That’s what poetry should be: messy, loud, sometimes stupid. They should make you feel everything—your rage, your hunger, your small, trembling love.

Then someone tapped my shoulder. “I wrote a stanza about a door that won’t open. I wanted to show that sometimes you’re not stuck because you’re afraid. You’re stuck because you keep asking the same questions and hoping someone else will give you the answers.”

I laughed. “Oh. That’s good.”

“Good?” she said. “It’s perfect.”

“Perfect is a nice word,” I said. “But it’s not real.”

We finished each other’s poems. The room was quiet except for the hum of the lights and the sound of people laughing. I kept writing. I wrote about the kitchen where my father used to make coffee. I wrote about the way a mother can hold your hand while talking about the sky. I wrote about things I didn’t want to write because they felt too much.

I didn’t say a word.

The workshop ended. I walked out of the building and looked at the street. People were going home. I could feel the words in my mouth, loud and unfinished. I took a breath and wrote the last line:

I am not perfect. I am still learning how to be honest.

Then I walked into the dark and turned off the light.
