I woke at seven, my head still spinning like I’d run a marathon through a ditch. By eight, I was brushing my teeth, sharpening the pencil, and checking the weather. I’m thirty-two years old and I’ve learned something about aging: it’s not a slow fade. It’s a sudden, aggressive, beautiful takeover. The world keeps turning while I keep talking.

We’re in a workshop today, and the instructor is trying to teach us how to make a poem that shows emotion instead of just telling people how it feels. He said, “Don’t write about love. Write about what love does to you.” I said, “Love does nothing but hurt.” The room laughed. One guy said, “At least it’s honest.”

I didn’t care. I had a poem in my head about how much I hated being polite when I’m angry enough to throw a punch. I wanted to write about the way my voice sounds when I’m yelling at someone who doesn’t understand the language. But the instructor had a point: poetry shouldn’t be a weapon. It should be a mirror.

So I wrote about a man whose mother made him eat soup without asking. I wrote about the way his hands trembled when he ate. I wrote about how he carried that soup in his chest like a secret he didn’t know he still possessed. I wrote about how he didn’t know he was angry at her because she made him feel small.

And that’s exactly what I wrote.

I’m not going to make this poem perfect. Some lines might feel like insults. Some images might make people uncomfortable. That’s the point. We’re here to make ourselves uncomfortable, not to make everyone comfortable.

I’m going to read it in the workshop. I’m going to let the class hear it. I’m going to listen, even if I’m going to hate what I hear.

That’s the only rule I’ll follow today: write what you’re feeling, then make it loud enough that someone has to hear it.
