The workshop read like a mouthful of gravel. I stood in front of the paper, throat tight, and began reading aloud without thinking about what the teacher was asking. That’s how I write: loud, sometimes rude, and mostly honest.

“Read again,” the instructor said. I read the first part again, faster this time. The opening was beautiful. It sounded like rain on a roof.

I stopped.

That was the first time I’d ever found myself listening.

The poem had no big words. No “love,” “nature,” “change,” or “hope.” Just green leaves, gray clouds, the sound of a creek, and a little bird crying. And yet it made me feel like the world was finally quiet enough to hear.

I kept reading. The second half was still sharp. It showed how one stone could become a mountain. One branch could become a tree. One broken leaf could become part of the winter. It was ugly. It was messy. It was not pretty. But it was real.

I wrote down every line on my phone. I wrote the bird’s name. I wrote the color of the sky when the wind stopped. I wrote everything I didn’t like about myself while reading the poem. I wrote the part of me that wanted the workshop to stop. I wrote the part of me that still believed the rain could wash away the bad words I used in my head.

At the end, I didn’t finish with a perfect ending. I ended with a line I had not practiced:

The river does not apologize for making a way.

Maybe that’s the lesson. Maybe the lesson is not to be polite. Maybe the lesson is to make room for the rough stuff without judging it. Maybe the lesson is that every broken thing has a purpose.

I left the room feeling full of leaves. I also felt full of words I couldn’t name.

I went home and wrote a poem about a garden that was not beautiful. It had dead flowers. It had a blackbird. It had a hole where a worm might have been. It smelled like wet earth and rain.

That was enough.
