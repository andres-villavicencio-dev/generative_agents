My name is Carlos Gomez. I’m thirty-two, loud, rude enough to get people off my back without thinking twice, and I swear to the whole world that I can’t be bothered with poetry unless I’m making it hurt. But lately, that’s changing. I’ve been reading about nature, and honestly, I’ve stopped being angry at the world. I’ve started seeing it for what it actually is: beautiful. Trees have scars, yes, but they’re also holding on. Riverbeds are messy, but they’re moving toward something.

At the workshop, the facilitator, Elena, asked us to discuss the main themes of nature poetry. I looked at her like she’d asked me to apologize for existing. She didn’t say anything. She just wrote something down on the paper. Then she said, “You’re not writing about trees. You’re writing about how trees make you feel when they’re dying.” I laughed. I thought I was the only one who heard that.

I’ve been working on a collection called The Quiet Things That Keep the World Warm. It’s not about flowers. It’s about birds, rain, wind, and the small things that make us feel alive even when the weather is terrible. I don’t want people to call me an poet. I want them to call me a witness.

I’m thirty-two. I’m tired of being polite. I want to get angry again. I want to be loud again. But sometimes I wonder if noise is just the body talking to the mind. Maybe the real poetry isn’t the words I write. Maybe it’s the silence between the words—the moment when everything stops, and I’m still here, feeling something I didn’t know I had.

I’m going to bed at ten. I’ll wake up at seven. I’ll eat dinner around five. I’ll write, I’ll talk, I’ll be rude, and when I’m done, I’ll go home. But tonight, I’m going to listen. Maybe that’s enough.
