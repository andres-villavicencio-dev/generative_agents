I woke up at seven. That’s what I do. Seven in the morning. The sun is already rising, but I’m not sleepy. I need fuel. Coffee first. Then breakfast. Then the workshop.

I brought my draft to the table. I read it aloud. The room went quiet.

“Whoa,” said the facilitator. “That’s… that’s pretty raw. You’re saying the forest remembers the sky?”

“Yeah,” I said, leaning in. “It remembers everything. Birds, rain, roots, the heat before winter. It’s not pretty, sometimes. It’s honest.”

Honesty is dangerous when you’re loud. I’ve written worse. I’ve written about storms, betrayal, and people who think they’re stronger than they are. But poetry has to survive them. It has to live.

The facilitator nodded. “You’re giving the forest its voice. That’s exactly what I’m trying to do. You don’t make your poems perfect. You make them true.”

I smiled. It was the first real smile I’d had all morning.

“Does the forest ever feel lonely?” I asked.

“Only if you look at it wrong,” she replied.

I laughed. Then I said something else. I said I miss my sister. The forest didn’t. It just kept growing, green and quiet, while I stayed inside.

“Growth is different from staying,” she said gently.

I looked at my hands. They were shaking a little. I’d been too busy talking to listen. I needed to slow down.

“Okay,” I said. “I’ll read it again. And I’ll write a better version.”

The facilitator smiled. She watched me. She didn’t mock me. She let me work.

That’s when I felt something shift. The room felt small. The forest felt vast. I was thirty-two. I was tired. But I was alive.

I finished reading. Then I wrote.

“Let the trees speak,” I said. “Let the birds ask questions. Let the wind remind us that nothing is final.”

The workshop ended. I went home and sat on the floor. The door opened. Someone came in. I didn’t speak. I just watched him pass.

He smiled. I did too.
