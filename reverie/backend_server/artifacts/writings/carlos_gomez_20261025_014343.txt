The workshop gave me a little time to breathe.

I sat in the chair where I had been holding the pen for three hours. The room smelled like old paper and cheap coffee. I took a sip and said nothing. That was the rule of my morning: drink, do nothing, drink again.

I wrote the opening poem.

I didn’t want it to make anyone smile. I wanted it to make them stop.

The first stanza began with a river. I wrote: It came up from the stone, and the stone let it through.

Then I wanted to make the river look angry. I wanted to see its teeth, its claws, the way it swallowed the rocks and came back with them. So I wrote: The river had a mouth. And the mouth did not close.

I kept adding lines.

The water drank the sun.  
It held its breath until the trees forgot their names.  
It drank the birds.  
Then the birds turned into glass bottles.  
Then the bottles became stones.

At some point I felt like the poem was trying to tell me something.

“Beautiful,” I said to the river.

The river looked at me. I didn’t know what to say.

By the time the workshop ended, the river still looked angry. I looked at the water and saw the same reflection I had seen on my first day in the workshop: a young man in a grey hoodie, holding a pen, looking at the blank page like it had just stolen his name.

I closed my notebook.

I thought about the beautiful things I had written about: moss on the riverbank, the way sunlight moved through the leaves, the sound of a beetle moving across a stone. I wanted to write about those things without making them sound like a poem. I wanted to write about them as they are.

So I wrote one last line.

The river does not care about beauty.

It only knows how much it can take.

I put the notebook down.

At ten, I went home and made coffee. I drank it slowly. I wrote nothing.

Maybe that was the point.

Maybe the point was to finish writing.

Maybe the point was to learn how to listen.

I went to bed at ten. The river remained.
