The storm didn’t stop. The rain just kept falling, heavier every minute, like my own mouth refusing to let me speak. I stood under the porch and watched the wind carve the old oak’s bark until it looked like a map of something I’d never noticed before. Beneath the branches, the roots had become a dark, tangled web.

I had spent the last forty minutes rewriting. Every revision was just another way to say what I was tired of: that words can be sharper than the rain, louder than the wind, and always more honest than my own polite apologies.

My mother said poetry was a way to feel better. I was convinced it was a way to feel worse—more honest, more exposed, and somehow more dangerous. Now, standing beneath the trees, I believe that’s the real danger. A poem has to take a lot of pain to be good.

The water in the well was warm. I had forgotten to notice it, and suddenly the whole garden felt alive again. Flowers were pushing through the dry earth, their green fingers trembling in the cold air. Their roots were drinking the same dark, tangled web the oak had left behind.

Maybe the storm isn’t proof that everything is bad. Maybe it’s proof that something is trying to get through. Maybe that’s what I’ve been trying to write all along—the rain, the roots, the flowers, and the part of me that refuses to stay still.

I finished the passage. I didn’t mark it as the best poem I had written today. That would be lying. But I wrote it with my whole body, and for the first time, I didn’t feel like I was hiding anything. I felt full. I felt nourished. I felt quietly, dangerously excited.

The oak kept its map. The rain kept falling. I kept writing, because the world needed someone to say it loud enough to be heard.
