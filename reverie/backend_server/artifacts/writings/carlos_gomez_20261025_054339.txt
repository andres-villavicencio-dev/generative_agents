The morning was a little too quiet to be real. Seven o’clock in the rain, the kitchen humming, the coffee still black in my hand. I stood at the sink and looked at my notes.

I had written a poem about a river. Not the river I know—the one behind the glass, the one I’ve never seen. But the one I could imagine. It was beautiful, yes. It had green, it had water, it had all the things I wanted to say but didn’t know how to say.

At first I wrote it as a landscape. A river with branches, stones, and a little light. I called it The River I Should Have Seen. But the first stanza made me want to throw my notebook aside. The river wasn’t a landscape. It was angry. It was thirsty. It was something I had missed, and I was still standing in the way.

I wrote it back.

The river had teeth.

I meant the river’s teeth, not my own.

But the next day, I came back to the same stanza and didn’t want to throw it away. It felt like a confession.

The river had teeth. They did not open, but they moved. Each tooth was a decision, a stone, a piece of earth the water had swallowed and turned into something else.

I wrote it again. The river was not a landscape. It was a mouth. And I was still learning how to speak without being eaten.

On Tuesday, after the workshop and the quiet dinner, I opened the collection. The poems were still messy. They were loud, sometimes rude, sometimes beautiful. But they were all mine.

I drafted, then revised, then wrote again. I kept the river, but I did not let it teach me to be gentle. I learned to speak its language.

At ten, I went to bed.

At seven, I woke up.

And the rain had stopped.

I had a poem. I had a river. I had all morning.
