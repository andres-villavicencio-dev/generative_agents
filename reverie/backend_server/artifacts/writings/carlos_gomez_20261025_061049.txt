I woke up at seven, brushed my teeth, and ate something warm before heading out. By eight, the sun had been up all morning. I kept coming back to the poems. Not because they were beautiful. Because I needed them. And sometimes I needed to write about storms and dawn more than I wanted to admit.

The second poem is supposed to show that the storm isn’t a sign that the world is ending. It’s a reminder that everything is still moving. Water gathers at the edges, then moves with its own stubborn purpose. The trees bend, then straighten. The sky turns gray, then breaks.

I had the transition wrong before. I made the dawn sound too polite. Too patient. Too like the poet telling the storm to stop. The storm should have been loud. It should have been angry. But dawn didn’t ask for permission. It just came through.

So I rewrote the middle. Let the rain keep going. Let the wind carry the sound of branches breaking. Then let the light arrive—cold at first, bright enough to cut through the gray. The dawn should feel like an arrival, not a promise.

By the end, the poem still feels heavy. The storm feels almost too perfect. I wanted the transition to feel more violent. More honest.

So I changed the final image. Instead of “dawn arrives,” I wrote: “Dawn arrived without saying anything. The sky went from black to blue without warning. One bird opened its wings. Everything stayed still for a second, then moved.”

It didn’t feel quite right. But I liked it. It felt less like a poem telling me everything is okay. It felt like something happening, even if nobody was listening.

That’s the lesson I’ve been trying to learn. Nature doesn’t apologize for its fury. It doesn’t apologize for breaking branches. It doesn’t apologize for letting us wake up.
