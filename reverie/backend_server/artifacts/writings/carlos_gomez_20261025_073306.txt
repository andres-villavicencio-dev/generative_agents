I woke at seven and checked the time like a man who believes every second is a mistake. I brushed my teeth, washed my face, and poured myself coffee before the world was entirely mine. By seven and a half, I had already read the poems from my natural-world collection. I read them again. That’s enough. One more read and I can see them as they are: beautiful, loud, and dangerous.

The poems should not be soft. Softness is a death sentence. A tree should say something ugly, not just “I am full of green.” A river should make a sound so loud the stone beneath it is afraid to breathe again. I want the earth to speak with teeth. I want the rain to come down and wash the world until nothing remains but the memory of it.

The second stanza comes first.

It comes down in drops like black coins thrown onto the floor.
Every leaf holds a thundercloud in its brown paper skin.
The wind does not whisper; it uncurls and rips,
and the grass answers with the sound of a blade passing through bone.

The third stanza follows, and the poem will open with a bird tearing the air out of the sky.

By five, dinner was a small rebellion against the day. I ate slowly, watching the steam rise between the plates and imagining the world as a kitchen full of knives and open mouths. I did not ask the poems to be pretty. They could be ugly, they could be dangerous, and I wanted every line to bleed into the next.

At ten I went to sleep. By morning, the poems were already waiting for me again. I could hear them in the quiet of the room, sharpening themselves, preparing to speak.
