I woke up at 7:00 AM, ate the same breakfast I always eat, and spent the next few hours making sure the collection I was working on didn’t feel like a museum. I found a line about the way light changes on the edge of the woods, then another about how a bird’s call can sound like a warning. By the time I sat down to revise, my desk was full of half-finished drafts, notes from conversations, and the raw, messy way my thoughts keep slipping out. I kept rewriting until the words finally fit together.

Yuriko Yamamoto asked me to describe the park and the way it feels when you walk through it. I told her it feels like someone has taken every corner and made it quiet. She said that sounded too polite. I said the park is loud.

We spent an hour walking around Johnson Park. I talked about the way the trees lean toward each other, how the wind moves through the branches like impatient fingers, and how the birds keep arguing over the same space. I brought out photos from my notebook—branches, sunlight, a bird nest, a puddle reflecting a sky. Yuriko laughed. She asked what I was looking for. I said I’m looking for beauty that doesn’t pretend to be pretty.

By noon, the poems were still in progress. I revised the one about the old oak, changed the ending, and let the ending take the credit. I read the finished pieces aloud, then read the same passage again, changing every line. The collection feels less like a collection now. It feels like something growing.

At five, I ate dinner and sat down with the evening reflection. I wrote a poem about a storm passing through the park, about water finding a way through the ground, and about how nothing ever stays the same. I closed the notebook and stared at the darkening sky. I was quiet now. I was full. And I was quietly excited.
