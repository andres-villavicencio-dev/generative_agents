I woke at seven without checking the clock. The kitchen smelled like toasted bread and something sharp—like lemon and rain coming through a cracked window. I made coffee without looking at it. By seven thirty, the poems were already mine.

I stood over the desk and picked up the natural-world drafts. A robin is writing a poem in the branches. The river keeps arguing with the rocks. I want the birds to sound like small, impatient people, not pretty birds in books. I want the water to feel dirty, alive, stubborn.

At first it felt messy. But then I saw the beauty. The way the light moves across bark, the way a leaf turns toward the sun, the sudden, beautiful quiet of an oak opening its branches. I was supposed to write about flowers and trees, but now I’m writing about anger, pride, the noise of being alive, and the strange, nourishing fact that a bird can teach me what silence feels like.

I revised the section about the creek. I changed a paragraph about its green color from “it was bright and full of green” to “it looked like a wound that hadn’t healed.” Then I changed the second line.

The river did not apologize to me. It kept moving, carrying stones, moss, dead leaves, and all the things I couldn’t name. I wanted the river to sound like a person who refused to be understood.

By Tuesday, my hands were tired but my mind was full. I ate dinner without checking the menu. I read the poems aloud. They sounded rough, but they were honest.

I’m not becoming gentle. I’m becoming clearer. And sometimes that’s more beautiful than softness.
