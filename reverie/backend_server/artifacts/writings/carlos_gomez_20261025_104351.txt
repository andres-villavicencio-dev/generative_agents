The workshop instructor started with a poem about a house that finally fell down. The walls didn’t crumble; they gave up. I stared at the page. “That’s poetic,” the instructor said, tapping the pen against his knee. “You’re too polite to admit that.”

I said, “I’m not polite. I’m tired.”

The room went quiet.

I sat there for three minutes before I said it again. My throat felt like it was swallowing a stone. The instructor raised an eyebrow.

“Then why write about falling?” he asked.

I didn’t answer. I kept reading.

The first stanza was beautiful. The second was honest. But the third made me want to throw the notebook. I had been trying to make poetry sound interesting, and instead I had made it sound like I was apologizing for everything.

I left the room.

The instructor followed.

“Carlos,” he said, “you’re loud enough to be heard.”

I nodded.

He pointed to the page I was reading. “You’re not trying to be interesting. You’re trying to be real.”

I looked at him. Really looked at him. He was thirty-two. He had a poet’s eyes, but they were tired. The workshop was supposed to be about creativity. It was about becoming someone you weren’t.

I turned the page.

“Do you believe in poetry?” he asked.

“I believe in the work,” I said.

“Then why do you think anyone listens?”

I didn’t know if I was being rude or being honest. I was both.

“Because it’s hard,” I said.

He smiled. A real smile.

“Then you should write more.”

I nodded.

“Write more.”
