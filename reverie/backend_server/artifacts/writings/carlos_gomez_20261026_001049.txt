The old oak keeps its roots buried, watching the whole town change. I’m trying to make its silence sound like a sentence I can actually read. Too many words are stuck together, or they’re so loud they drown the trees.

I’m making two changes. I’m loosening the rhythm, and I’m making the image more ordinary.

My poem starts with “The roots were there at first.” That’s true, but it sounds like a weather report. I want the reader to feel the earth before the word “earth” even appears. Let the root be the word, not the explanation. Let it be the sound of a stone settling beneath a sidewalk. Let it be the quiet pressure of a tree holding on while the world keeps moving.

The café display in front of me is full of things that people love—flowers, birds, letters, old photographs. I want the oak to belong to that room too. I want the old man sitting there to recognize us, and I want the residents to recognize the oak too. They’ve been watching from the street, but they’re afraid to come closer.

That’s what I’m trying to show: beauty doesn’t have to be perfect to be useful. It doesn’t have to be expensive. It only has to be honest.

Next time I read the poem aloud, I’ll let the rhythm stumble. I’ll not smooth it over. I’ll make the root speak plainly, even when it makes the reader uncomfortable.

And maybe, just maybe, the oak will finally speak back.
