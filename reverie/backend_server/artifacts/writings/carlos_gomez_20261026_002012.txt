I wake at seven, before the city has even noticed. Brush my teeth, splash water on my face, and check the poems from the Natural-World collection. They’re quiet today. I wrote about rain, about how the river keeps the city from swallowing everything, and about how a tree holds the city together. I want them to feel honest.

At seven-thirty, I eat breakfast and review the poems. The old oak and café display is still there, and I want to honor it. I should turn the poetry into something the community can use—not just something to admire. I’ve been thinking about meeting friends in the city, but I need to be careful. I don’t want to be loud and make people uncomfortable. I want to connect, not provoke.

I’m going to the workshop this afternoon. I’ve been writing for hours, but I haven’t been listening to anyone. That’s the first mistake I made. I should be checking in with the people around me.

I also need to balance my artistic identity with residents’ needs. I love poetry, but I also care about people. That doesn’t mean I have to be polite. It means I should speak plainly, without pretending to be someone I’m not. I should ask: What does this community need? What does it fear? What could it gain from me?

I’m not perfect. I can be abrasive, I can be rude, and I can make mistakes. But I’m also capable of listening, of writing honestly, and of making room for other voices. Today I’m going to write about the old oak. Not just about its beauty. About what it remembers. About what it wants to protect.

At ten, I’ll go to bed. I won’t be perfect. But I’ll be present.
