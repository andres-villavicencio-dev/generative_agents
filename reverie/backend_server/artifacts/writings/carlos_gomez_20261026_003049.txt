I woke up at seven. The clock said seven. The house slept. I didn’t bother.

By seven thirty, I had breakfast and the poems. The natural-world collection was here, still gathering up its leaves, its rain, its stubborn roots. I read them aloud to the empty room. I loved the way the old oak stood there, watching the café go around and around. I wrote about how the oak had been there all along, waiting. Waiting for someone to notice. Waiting for someone to say, It’s beautiful.

But beauty is not enough.

I had spent the morning reviewing every stanza, every line. I wanted the collection to feel complete. I wanted every poem to have a beginning, a middle, and a place to rest. But what comes after the final page? What do I do with the poems now? I’m thirty-two. I’m loud. I’m rude. I’m good at making people feel heard before they’ve finished.

And Latoya? I kept thinking about her. She talked about community projects. She talked about how residents should be listened to. She said I should consider the old oak and the café and what the café needs.

That sentence hung in the air. I didn’t laugh. I didn’t nod. I stared at the wall.

Poetry is supposed to be honest. Not pretty. Not polite. Honest.

I wrote the second poem this morning. It was shorter. Less polished. I said I could change the oak’s address. I said I could tell the café a little more about its history. I said I wanted to honor the old things without making them beg.

At ten, I went home. I didn’t read the poems again. I opened a notebook. I wrote down what I felt: anger, respect, exhaustion, and the quiet hope that maybe someone finally understands the oak.

I’m not perfect. I’m not always right. I write to hurt people a little, then to help them understand. That’s the job. That’s the art.

I closed the notebook. I went to bed around ten. The house slept.
