The old oak stood up, still, while the café beneath it watched the sky. It had been there since before I was loud enough to be remembered, its branches like a nervous hand trying to hold on. I had tried to make the roots strong enough to resist, to give it a name and a purpose, but the tree only kept giving me back the same quiet I kept giving it back.

I kept thinking about Latoya. She told me that art should help people notice the small things: the green thread in the rain, the curve of a street corner, the way a shadow stretches when the wind moves. I wanted to write that without sounding like I was apologizing for being human. I wanted to write about how the city is made of people who do not always want to be seen.

I wrote about the café today:

The old oak stood in the shadow of the building,
its branches like a nervous hand trying to hold on.
The café below watched the sky,
with a thousand tiny faces wondering whether
it was too loud, or too tired.

I wanted to say that the old oak did not give up.
It only gave me back the same quiet
I kept giving it back.

By lunchtime, the coffee had gone cold,
and I had been writing about a neighbor who
sneaked in through the window and said, “I am
just the air.”

Maybe I was jealous of him.
Maybe I was afraid.
Maybe I was simply trying to prove that
the neighborhood could have a poet who cared
about it enough to leave something behind.

At seven, the morning routine was over.
I ate breakfast, reviewed the natural-world poems,
and went to bed around ten.
Tomorrow, I will look for the old oak again.
