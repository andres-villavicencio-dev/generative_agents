I woke at seven. The coffee was bitter, the alarm a dull thud against my skull, but I didn’t want to be late. That was the first lesson of this day: don’t wait until the morning comes to finish what you started.

I sat down with the natural-world collection and read the poems aloud. The riverbank scene made me angry—not because I was angry at the river, but because I was angry at how easily the river disappears beneath the trees. I wanted to say, You are here, you are loud, you are becoming, and instead I said, You are becoming everything.

By seven-thirty, I’d finished the morning routine, had my breakfast, and had the collection on my desk. I had twenty minutes. Not twenty to perfect every line, but twenty to make the poems feel like they were breathing.

At the workshop, I met Latoya. She was talking about how community projects could honor the old oak and the café display, and I listened without interrupting. That was enough. I could have argued with her about whether the café belonged to the community, whether the oak was too old to be reclaimed, whether the display was merely decoration. Instead, I asked, “How could we make sure the oak stayed alive?”

That was the kind of question I’d asked before. I’d asked about the river, the garden, the abandoned house, the poet who kept writing until the room filled with smoke. The answers had always been the same: preserve, protect, remember. But now, when I looked at the oak, I saw someone else. Someone who had waited too long to be seen.

I went home and wrote one more poem. It wasn’t about the river. It was about someone who was being erased. Someone whose story had been cut out before it could be rewritten.

Maybe the best poetry isn’t the loudest. Maybe it’s the one that makes you look closer than you were before. Maybe it’s the one that asks, not “What are you?” but “Why were you there at all?”

I closed the notebook and put the pen back in my pocket. The day was still full. I could still revise, still argue, still feel loud. But for tonight, I wanted the quiet. I wanted to sit beside the oak, listen to the trees, and let the poem say what I couldn’t say in my head.
