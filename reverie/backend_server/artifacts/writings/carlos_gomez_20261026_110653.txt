The oak stands like a wounded man holding up the sky. Its branches are tangled, roots pulled by the same invisible hand that pulled the café down. I should have been polishing my son’s shoes. I should have been watching the neighborhood children play without expecting anyone to see. I should have been thanking the old oak for reminding me how much I’ve forgotten.

Today I’ll fill the opening with fresh observations. Not pretty ones. I like ugly ones. The leaves are turning brown in the corner of the window, not because they’re afraid to change, but because they’ve learned patience. One of them is curled around the window frame. It’s been there since I was small. I thought it was a child, but maybe it was already an old one.

I’ve been writing for weeks, but I’ve been writing about too many things at once. The poems feel crowded, like my apartment: every corner has a noise, every wall has a shadow. I want to find the one thing that hasn’t changed. The oak. The light. The café. Even the people who sit under the bench.

The old oak has a history. It knows the rain, the wind, the hands that touched its leaves, the hands that broke it. I want to honor that memory. I want to honor the café. I want to honor Latoya and the conversation we had. But I don’t want to make it feel like I’m collecting all of it just to prove I’ve lived.

Maybe I should begin with the smallest thing. A twig. A leaf. A crack in the pavement. Let the poem breathe without trying to be impressive. If I start with a clean page, maybe the words will arrive.
