I finished the sixth stanza before the sun could touch the old oak. “The root remembers,” I wrote, and the line made my throat tighten like a knot in the shoulder. I wanted to make it sharper. Sharper than the root. Sharper than every person who ever stepped through its shade and kept walking away.

“Root remembers,” I told it.

The oak didn’t listen.

It only held the rain.

That was the point. The poem had been asking for applause, for recognition, for someone to say, “You’re good.” Instead, I wrote it so it could stand alone. So the root could remember without being told what to remember.

The café display showed the tree as a monument. I wanted to show it as a question. Not: What are you protecting? But: What are you refusing to let go?

I changed the stanza.

“Every stone remembers what it once carried,” I wrote. “Even if the hand that placed it is gone. Even if the hand that holds it is no longer there. The tree does not ask for permission to remain.”

I read it aloud. My voice cracked at the end. That’s when I realized I had been so careful not to offend that I had forgotten to hurt.

The oak wasn’t judging me. It was just surviving.

And that’s the truth I wanted to keep. Not the pretty words. Not the polished workshop. Not the community meeting. The old oak. The roots in the dirt. The quiet, stubborn fact that nothing survives if you let it forget what it planted.
