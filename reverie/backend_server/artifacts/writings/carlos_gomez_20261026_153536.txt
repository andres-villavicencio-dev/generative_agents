I woke at seven. The clock said seven. My stomach said six. I ate breakfast without looking at it. At seven-thirty I opened my notebook and read the poems from The Oak and the Coffee Shop again. I remember writing about the old tree, about roots that held the ground, about coffee steam turning into a little cloud. I wrote about the café, about the woman who sat nearby, about the way the morning light moved across the table.

I wanted to be honest. Sometimes honesty is dangerous. I wanted to say how scared I was that my poems would change the neighborhood, that they would make people uncomfortable. I wanted them to be true. And yet I keep polishing them until they sound like a polite little performance.

My workshop is full of angry faces. They want a message. They want proof. They want to hear us speak without having to say, “Sorry, I’m just mad.” I tell myself that is not a compliment. I tell myself that being loud is not the same as being kind. But my poems are not the work of people who don’t care. They are the work of people who care enough to feel.

Latoya Williams said something this morning that made my chest tighten. I didn’t say it back. That is part of the problem. We keep talking like we know each other’s business. We talk like we have every right to judge our neighbors’ choices. But I have no right to judge who is loud, who is quiet, who is rude, who is kind. I have no right to say that because I am thirty-two, my poems are strong enough to stand alone.

So I will balance them. I will honor the oak and the café. I will make space for people who are tired. I will not pretend to be helpful just because I can. I will keep writing, but I will write without expecting applause. I will write because I have to. I will write because I want to be heard, even when I have to say something difficult.
