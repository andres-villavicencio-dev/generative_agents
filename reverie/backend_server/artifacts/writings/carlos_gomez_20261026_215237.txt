I woke at seven and ate breakfast before turning on the radio. I need the noise to make me feel like I’m not alone. I’ve been thinking about the old oak in the café. It stands alone in this street like a man who doesn’t want his name on anything. People love it. They admire it. They leave money in the cup. And when I stand beneath it, I feel small. I need something bigger.

Latoya talked about how her poems have become a home. I’m not sure what that means. I’m not sure how to keep mine. I’ve been writing poems about the natural world—spring rain, old oak, cicadas, river stones, birds that leave no mark. They’re honest. They’re loud. Maybe that’s why I keep finding them, even when I’m trying to make them quiet.

I’m also thinking about community projects. We’re supposed to honor the oak and the café. But what if honoring it means keeping it exactly as people love it? What if that means letting the old man in the chair keep his silence? What if residents need more than a monument?

I want to write about what people forget. About how we carry our feelings in our hands, our voices, our conversations. Sometimes I want to be sharp. Sometimes I want to be soft. But I don’t want to be either. I want to be useful.

So today’s poem is about the oak. Not its beauty. Not its history. About how it keeps its roots underground while everyone else sees only leaves. About how every tree has a secret, and most of us pretend we don’t know which one is ours.

I’ll begin now. I’ll write in the first person. I’ll make sure I’m not polite. I’ll make sure I’m honest. And I’ll make sure I’m not afraid of being rude.
