I woke at seven, the city outside already humming with the morning traffic. I brushed my teeth, drank my coffee, and opened my notebook. Today I’m going to write the next poem from the Natural-World collection. I want the oak tree to speak through the tree.

Eddy Lin and I talked about the mayoral candidates’ oatmeal-and-banana-slice plan while I was sitting on the park bench. They sounded like two people arguing over whether a breakfast should be a meal. I told him I’d rather spend my day reading poetry than debating whether oatmeal should be a civic priority. Of course I would.

Eddy was loud. His voice carried like a banner. “Carlos, you’re the poet, you’re the person everyone listens to!” I said, but I was tired of being the poet. I was tired of being the one who had to explain why my poems mattered. I had a collection, a workshop, and a growing community project. I didn’t need to prove that my words were important. I needed them to be honest.

The old oak and café display reminded me of that. They were there for the community, not the candidates. I’d like to honor that same principle. My poems should not be decorations. They should be useful. I want to write about neighbors, about food, about how we spend our days. Maybe I’ll write about oatmeal. Maybe I’ll write about banana slices. Maybe I’ll write about the people who eat them.

I’ve been balancing my poetry with my writing workshops. I’m doing both. I’m keeping Monday’s productive day, my growing body of work, and Latoya’s conversation with me in mind. I’m also thinking about how to balance my artistic identity with residents’ needs. I don’t want to make my poetry a performance. I want it to exist in the world around me.

I’m ready to write. I’m ready to argue. I’m ready to be loud, if that means being real. The oak is still standing. The café is still there. Maybe the best proposal is to serve the people who need it.
