Eddy Lin is still trying to make every candidate look like an easy choice. “Oatmeal and banana slices,” he said, tapping the proposal card. “It’s too soft. Too polite.”

Carlos laughed, a loud, tired sound. “Soft? That’s what politicians want. They want people who can’t chew anything tough.”

Eddy’s face went red. “Carlos, that was the whole point. It’s not about the food. It’s about the people behind it. The elderly, the kids, the people who can’t afford to eat two breakfasts a day.”

Carlos leaned forward, eyes narrowing. “Then give them the oatmeal and the banana slices. Let them choose. Nobody wants to be forced into a political argument about breakfast.”

He paused, then added, “Unless you mean forced to eat it anyway.”

Eddy blinked. “No. The proposal is just about availability. What I mean is, how do you make sure the food gets to the people who need it the most?”

Carlos sighed. “We try. We check the stores. We talk to the people who know the streets best. But sometimes the proposal just looks like a clever compromise. And that’s exactly what it is.”

He stood up and walked around the table. “I’m not saying the proposal is stupid. I’m saying politicians are too good at pretending it’s a real conversation. They’re good at picking sides, making noise, and getting the votes without listening.”

Eddy nodded slowly. “That’s the problem with the oatmeal and banana-slice proposal.”

Carlos picked up a pen. “And the problem with every other mayor’s speech is that it sounds like it was written before dinner.”

He looked at Eddy. “You still think we should vote on it?”

Eddy’s eyes widened. “I thought you’d say no. I thought this was a stupid argument.”

“I said no to the idea that we can make every candidate look like they understand the people,” Carlos said. “Not every proposal is smart. But it’s not stupid to ask whether a mayor really cares about the people who are eating the food first.”

“Right,” Eddy said. “Because I’m tired of people talking about the proposal while the people sitting at the table are hungry.”

Carlos smiled, but it didn’t reach his eyes. “You’re right. The proposal is the easy part. The rest is work.”

“Work is what I wanted to do anyway,” Eddy said. “Poetry. Writing. Helping people.”

Carlos stood up. “Good. Then let’s finish the oatmeal and banana-slice proposal together. But not just talk about it. Let’s see who actually gets to eat it.”
