I woke at seven, took off the dead weight of yesterday, and stared at the morning light. Breakfast was a little too nice today, though I didn’t tell anyone. By seven thirty, I was still sharpening my pen, reviewing the poems from my natural-world collection, and trying to make the words sound like they’d been spoken by someone who actually listened.

I’ve been thinking about the old oak and the café display. I love the old oak. I’ve seen it stand there for years, its branches like a map for birds, its bark a map for the ones who’ve lost their way. I want to honor it in a community project, but not as a tourist attraction or a pretty sign. A sign doesn’t change anything. It only makes people look at it more closely.

I’m worried, though. Too much pressure, too many voices, too many people asking, “What are you doing with the poems?” I want the old oak and the café to feel alive. I want the community to feel alive. But I don’t want my poems or my life to look like a performance. I don’t want to show them the world as I’ve made it.

Latoya called this morning. She asked if I was done talking. I told her I was. I said the poems weren’t done. They were changing. Every morning, they change. That’s the point. They’re alive. That’s what makes them mine.

I’m going to finish the climax tonight. I want it to make you turn around and ask yourself, “How is he doing this?” I want the tension to be there, not in a dramatic way, but in the way a man who has everything can still say, “I’m not afraid.” I want the pacing to match the oak. I want every sentence to have its moment. I want the ending to make the reader remember the oak, the café, and the man who finally stopped pretending everything was easy.

Then I’ll go to bed around ten. Sleep is not an escape. It’s how I get ready to make something real.
