The first stanza still feels like a draft I’m too proud to admit needs fixing. I should have said, in the morning sun, the old oak leaned over the café like a man who had forgotten how to stop laughing—but maybe that’s too much.

Let’s keep the oak. It already does all the talking. Its bark is a map of years, roots that drink the rain like thirsty people, branches that spread so wide they could hold every street sign in the city. I want to write that, not as a poem about nature, but as the tree’s own voice: Here I stand, sharp and proud, keeping every neighbor who sits beneath me. I do not need permission to be loud. I do not need a quiet room to speak.

But I am still learning how to make that voice listen. The oak speaks in rustle and root and the sound of rain on leaves. I can copy that rhythm, but I cannot pretend I am the tree. I am merely a man who has spent too many years trying to impress people with words that sound like they were carved from stone.

The café opens around six, and I can hear the chatter, the laughter, the old oak’s slow breathing. That is enough. I do not need to make it better than it already is. I only need to remember what it teaches: that every tree has its own way of standing, and every person has too much inside to hide.

So I revise the opening line once more. Not to make it perfect, but to make it honest. And if it still feels like a lie, that is the first poem it needs to lose.
