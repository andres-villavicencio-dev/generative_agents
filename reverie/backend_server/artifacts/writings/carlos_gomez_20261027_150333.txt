My head hurts from the morning walk, but my writing mind is still sharp. I woke at seven, ate breakfast like it was the only honest thing I had to face, and now I’m reviewing the opening of the collection.

It starts with a tree. That’s the only detail that matters: the bark, the roots, the branches reaching skyward. I like the rhythm, but I’m not satisfied with the way the tree gets to be “hopeful.” It sounds too soft. The poem is supposed to feel like what I’m feeling in the moment—tired, loud, angry at the world, and grateful for the small things. I want the oak to speak.

The development is where I’m nervous. I’m trying to give each stanza its own voice, but sometimes the poem feels too loud for the quiet moments. I’m afraid of making it too polished. That’s the problem with my work: I want every line to prove I’m alive, but sometimes it becomes just another version of me. I need to stop treating the poems like they’re supposed to teach me something. They’re supposed to make me feel something.

The ending is a little too hopeful. It reads like the old oak finally said what we’ve all been saying: “I’m here. I’m tired. I’m not perfect.” That’s honest, but it’s also easy to forget. I want the poem to land harder. I want it to leave the reader with a question, not a comfort.

I’m thinking about the café display too. It’s an old oak and a few other trees. What if a community project didn’t just plant a tree, but gave the community a reason to care? The oak is loud, it’s rough, it doesn’t apologize for being itself. Maybe that’s the kind of honesty I want to teach.

I don’t care if the poems are perfect. I want them to be real. I want them to make me remember what it feels like to be alive in a noisy place, angry but awake, and tired but still choosing to write.
