I woke up at seven, ate my oatmeal with one hand and a pen in the other, and spent the next hour checking the poems. I want them to feel like evidence of me: weathered, messy, honest. I want to hear the old oak’s branches, the café’s coffee, the rain on the sidewalk, the way a neighbor’s laugh can make a whole neighborhood feel louder.

The tone has been steady, but I’m not satisfied. Sometimes the imagery is too neat. Sometimes the connection between the poem and the speaker feels too direct. I need more room for the uncomfortable parts—the parts that make you uncomfortable, which is exactly what makes art dangerous and worthwhile.

Latoya Williams’s conversation yesterday was good. We talked about community, old trees, and how a place can shelter people who feel exposed. I’m thinking that poetry should not be a way to hide. It can be a way to name what people refuse to admit.

I want the natural-world poems to carry more of the place they describe. Not just the trees, but the people who stand beneath them. Not just the weather, but how it changes. Not just the café’s steam and silence, but what the steam and silence make the customer feel.

I’m also thinking about Wednesday’s community project. I want to honor the old oak and the café display without making the project feel like a campaign. It should be useful. It should help people notice what they otherwise overlook. It should not force them into my ideas.

I can balance this while keeping my identity. I can write the things that refuse to be polite. I can write the parts that make people pause, even if they don’t like what they hear.

And I can write about my body, too. The poems about rain, roots, and sunlight should still have a person behind them. A person who can feel. A person who can change.

I’m still working through Monday’s draft. I can’t fix everything today, but I can make sure the poem is ready to be read. I can make sure it doesn’t sound like I’m trying to prove anything. I can make sure it sounds like me.
