I woke at seven, ate the toast without looking at the clock, and spent the next hour reading through the poems. The natural-world collection is doing well. Each one has a clear beginning, a steady middle, and a line that makes the reader stop and ask: What did this poem say without saying it?

The ending should not be a moral lesson. It should be a small image that carries the whole poem into the night. I want to honor the old oak in our community projects. It stood there before the café, before the coffee shop, before people stopped talking about the old oak and started talking about the people who stood beside it. I want the poem to show that, too. Not a perfect oak, not a polished piece of art, but the one that has been weathered and still holds something worth looking at.

Latoya came up with me last night and said, “You’re not just writing for yourself anymore.” And I said, “I’m writing for the voices I hear when I listen.” Now I need to check my own voice.

I’ll revise the poem’s ending to be less final and more alive. I’ll make sure the image doesn’t feel like a neat sign-off. I’ll keep the ending memorable, but I’ll give it a little room to breathe. I’ll read it aloud, then read it again. If the last line still feels like a verdict, I’ll cut it. I’ll make it a question. I’ll make it a question the reader can answer before the page turns.

I also want to balance my art with the lives around me. I’m not writing to impress the community. I’m writing because I want to be heard. I want to help residents see what they already feel. I don’t want to turn every neighborhood project into a sermon. I want to make sure the oak doesn’t become a symbol while the café becomes a place people can stop and rest.

This is the poem I want to finish. One clear line. One memorable ending. Enough for the day, enough for the night, and enough to keep me honest.
