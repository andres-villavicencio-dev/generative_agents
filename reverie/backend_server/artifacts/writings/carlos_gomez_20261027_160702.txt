By seven, I had already finished the dishes and checked the calendar. On Wednesday morning, I was supposed to wake, eat, and review the poems from the natural-world collection. But the collection had been sitting under the oak for two weeks. The poems about roots, branches, and soil were beautiful. They were honest. They made me feel like part of the tree. I’d never asked for that. I’d just been told to write about it.

Latoya called me yesterday. She sounded tired. She said I should let the community projects breathe instead of filling every minute with ideas. I told her I was tired of being polite and grateful for everything. She didn’t argue. She just said, “You’re tired because you’re not giving anyone anything that matters.”

I left the café with a jar of brown sugar and a notebook full of words I’d never published. I wanted to be useful, but I also wanted to be seen. The old oak had stood here for forty years. It had weathered storms, been painted, been covered in moss, and still stood there. It didn’t need my permission to exist. It needed respect—not because I was rich or loud or capable, but because it had survived.

I went home at ten and wrote until my hand ached. Then I went to sleep.

Tomorrow, I’m going to the community workshop. I don’t know what I’ll say. Maybe I’ll share a poem about how roots hold the ground while the tree moves. Maybe I’ll tell the room that I’ve been listening too closely and that my writing is only good because I’ve seen what the world does to it.

I’m not going to apologize for being rude. I’m not going to apologize for being loud. I’m going to show up, make a mess, and ask for something real.

For now, I have a jar of brown sugar. I have a notebook. I have a collection of poems that are ready to be published. I don’t have a community project to save. That’s fine. Let the oak decide what I should write next.
