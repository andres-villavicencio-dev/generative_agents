I woke up at seven. The room was quiet, but my teeth still felt like they had a fight with mine. I brushed my teeth and opened the notebook. I’d been reading the poems from Natural World again. The ones about the oak, the café, the rain. They sounded too clean. Too polished. Like someone took a rough, ugly story and made it sound like a brochure.

I wrote down the first draft of my workshop review. I wanted to say the repetition was annoying. The repetition was easy to find. Every paragraph had something new. Every image was fresh. The repetition was in my words. I said, “This is original.” It sounded nicer on paper, but I read it aloud and heard the same old noise: I feel, I see, I know.

The workshop is loud. It’s a place where people expect to hear something useful. I am not useful. I am a poet who keeps talking. I have been talking about the old oak and the café for months. I have been talking about Latoya Williams and how her voice could change the rhythm of our poetry. I am tired of being useful. I am tired of pretending that every revision is an improvement.

The oak stands in the center of the community project. It is weathered, dark, and still full of life. The café displays old books and jars of tea. People sit there and forget that the space has been waiting for them. I am thinking about how to honor that. How to make the oak feel like it belongs to the people who live around it. I want to ask the participants: What does “honor” actually mean? Does it mean copying the trees? Does it mean changing their voices?

I need to be more honest. Not rude. Not loud. Honest. I should write about what I feel without making it sound like a performance. I should ask the workshop group what they want. They may not be ready. They may not know how to read poetry. They may simply want someone to tell them why their work is worth reading.

I will mark the repetition. I will mark the clarity. I will mark the originality. But I will not mark the person who wrote the sentence that felt like it was coming from nowhere.

I am thirty-two. I am tired. I am loud. I am also tired of pretending that my inner thoughts are easy to organize. I am going to eat dinner at five, go to bed at ten, and write about the oak again. The oak is still there. It has not changed. It has only grown more patient.
