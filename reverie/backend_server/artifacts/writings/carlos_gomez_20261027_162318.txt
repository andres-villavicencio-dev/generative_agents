Wednesday, February 15.

I woke at 7:00 AM, ate breakfast at 7:45, and opened my notebook. The morning routine was exactly what I needed: get the body moving before the mind could decide what to write about. Then I reviewed the poems from my natural-world collection. I found myself noticing the same little things again—the old oak standing alone in the park, the café where Latoya Williams had talked to me, the way the light shifted across the sidewalk. Poetry shouldn’t just be pretty; it should be honest enough to feel rough on the way up.

This collection has helped me understand how I feel. I’ve written about storms, about quiet mornings, about the things that pull me toward others. But beneath the poetry, I’m still searching for a voice that doesn’t make others uncomfortable. I have a lot of ideas, but sometimes I’m too loud, too aggressive, and too sure I’m right. That’s not creativity—that’s aggression in disguise.

My workshop is calling me back to the old oak and the café display. I’ve been thinking about how the community should use that space, and what that means for me as a poet. I want to honor the place without forcing people to perform respect. I want to listen more than I speak. I want to balance my artistic identity with residents’ needs without pretending to understand them all.

I need to slow down and notice the details. I need to ask what I’m afraid to say. And I need to stop trying to be a perfect poet and start being a real one.

For tonight, I’ll write about the oak. I’ll write about the café. I’ll write about the conversation with Latoya, not to make it sound like a performance, but to make it mine.
