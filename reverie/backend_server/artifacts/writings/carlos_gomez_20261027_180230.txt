I woke at seven, ate the oatmeal with a spoonful of honey, and opened the notebook before the sun had really warmed the room. By seven-thirty, I’m done with breakfast and the natural-world poems. I want to check that each one has its own voice. The oak tree has been repeating itself in the collection, which makes me nervous. Am I just copying what I saw? Or am I finding a new way to say how the leaves feel when the wind moves them?

The themes are still messy, but not bad. I keep returning to community places—cafés, old oaks, the sidewalk, the people who sit there. I want to make sure they aren’t just decoration. Sometimes I treat a community project like a poem I’m writing in someone else’s name. I need to remember that the tree belongs to the neighborhood, not just to me.

I’m also thinking about the conversation with Latoya Williams. We talked about how art can change lives, and I keep wondering whether I’m changing anyone. My ideas are loud, I know—sometimes I say things I don’t mean. But the poems themselves are trying to reach inside, to show me what I’ve been holding without naming it.

I’ll leave the poems for the next day. I’ll check that they’re original, that the themes are clear, and that I’m not just turning the world into a collection of pretty phrases. I want to honor the old oak and the café display without making them the only subjects I care about. I also need to keep the residents’ needs in mind. I can be creative and loud, but I shouldn’t forget that art belongs to the community too.

At ten, I’ll go to bed. I’ll read the next morning before the sun comes up, and then I’ll start again.
