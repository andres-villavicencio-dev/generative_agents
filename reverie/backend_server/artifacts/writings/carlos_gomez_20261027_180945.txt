I woke up at seven, my mouth dry, and immediately started checking the calendar. I had an appointment with the community group later this afternoon, but it was too close to the workshop. I marked it as low priority and moved the workshop to the top.

I ate breakfast without anyone asking about it. Then I opened my notebooks. The natural-world poems are still mine. I reread the entries about rain, roots, and the old oak in the café. Every time I write about the earth, I want to hear exactly what the earth is thinking. I want to hear the old oak’s grief, or its patience. I want to hear the café’s tiredness.

But I’m also thinking about residents. I’ve been thinking about how the community projects can look without being a performance. How can an old oak be honored without turning into a museum piece? How can I keep my poetry from becoming another thing to impress people?

I should not be loud when I talk about poetry. I’ve learned that. I should not be rude. I should not be toxic.

I have a conversation with Latoya tonight. I’ve been wondering if we can find a way to honor the old oak and the café without making the community project feel like I’m trying to prove something. Latoya said she noticed how the oak survived the storms. I said it had survived years of being overlooked. She smiled and said, “That’s a good story.” I said, “It is,” then I forgot about the story.

I’m going to write about the oak again tomorrow. Maybe I’ll write about how it held the café together. Maybe I’ll write about how the oak made sure the community didn’t forget its roots. Maybe I’ll write about how I can keep my work honest without making it boring.

I’m tired of pretending I’m all this. I’m tired of writing poems that make me sound like I know everything. I’m tired of being loud, rude, and toxic. I want to be thoughtful. I want to be useful. I want to keep the old oak and the café in my writing, just as I’ve kept them in my life.

Tomorrow, I’ll begin with one poem. No promises. Just one.
