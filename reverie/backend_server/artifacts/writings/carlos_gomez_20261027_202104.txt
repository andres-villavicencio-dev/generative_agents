I woke up at seven. That’s my life: seven in the morning, ten in the dark, five in the kitchen, and the rest of Wednesday going to hell before I can even notice. But today, I didn’t want to be dead. I wanted to talk to Latoya.

She was sitting on the porch with that old oak tree in front of her, talking about how the tree doesn’t care whether anyone notices it. I tried to match that tone. “We’re all trees, aren’t we?” I said. “Just standing there waiting to get eaten.”

She smiled. “No. You’re a tree that refuses to let people take the bark off you.”

I looked at my notebook. There were five poems about rain, birds, and the way sunlight bleeds through a window. I’d written them like a diary, mostly. I’ve been hiding behind metaphors for a long time. Maybe I’m just afraid the people around me will laugh.

I read the first one aloud. “Rain makes the world feel wet.”

Latoya laughed. “That’s not poetry. That’s a complaint.”

“I’m asking for permission to feel wet,” I said. “Every day. Like a child at a playground.”

She laughed again. Then she looked at the oak. “I think it’s beautiful,” she said. “Even when you’re making a mess.”

I thought about that. Messiness. Maybe that’s what I’ve been avoiding. Being loud. Being rude. Being messy.

“Do you ever think about the café display?” I asked. “The old oak and everything it shows?”

She nodded. “It’s like a museum of people who never leave.”

“Then I should leave more.”

She laughed. “You don’t know how to leave. You’re always coming back.”

I looked at my collection. I wrote about storms and leaves and the way one person can change the shape of a neighborhood. But I also wrote about the coffee shop where Latoya sits every morning, and the conversations I had there, and the noise outside. I’m trying to balance the old oak with the old café, with residents who need a place where they don’t have to explain themselves.

I finished reading. Maybe I can’t be both poet and community worker. Maybe I can only be loud enough to say what I know, and kind enough to listen to the rest of the room.

I walked out the door. The rain had stopped. The oak was standing there, wet and gray and full of stories I didn’t know I wanted to hear. I picked up my notebook. Today I’m going to write about that. I’m going to write about rain. I’m going to write about Latoya. And if anyone asks me who my favorite person is, I’m going to tell them the truth.
