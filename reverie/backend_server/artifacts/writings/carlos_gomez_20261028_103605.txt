I woke at seven, brushed my teeth like a man who didn’t want to be seen, and opened the notebook. The natural-world images were there—giant oak roots, cracked pavement, a café display of old porcelain. I checked them. They didn’t match. The oak looked dead in one, alive in another. The roots twisted like a knot, not a river.

I read the poems aloud. They sounded like me. Loud. Confident. Sometimes rude. Sometimes tired. I loved that.

At five, I ate dinner and went for a walk. The old oak stood beneath the café, its branches reaching for the sky like fingers trying to hold a storm. I wanted to say everything the trees had kept from me. But words cost me. They cost me time, patience, and dignity. I could use them. I could use a poem to make the space feel like home.

On the table, a small sign said: Community Tree. Beneath it, a plaque read: Preserve the Oak.

I considered making it. I thought about how residents would see that tree. What if the display looked like a tourist souvenir? What if we forgot whose hands planted it?

I wrote a paragraph about balance. “Art belongs to the person who makes it,” I said. “But art belongs to the people who live beside it too.”

Maybe that’s the lesson I’m trying to teach. Not in every poem. Not in every workshop. In the quiet moments when someone asks, “What are you doing?” and you have to choose between making art and giving attention.

At ten, I put the notebook down. I didn’t go to sleep yet. Maybe I’ll finish the poem. Maybe I’ll write about how the oak held the winter rain. Maybe I’ll tell Latoya I’m proud of my voice.

I didn’t sleep at ten. I slept at six. The next day will tell me if I was right.
