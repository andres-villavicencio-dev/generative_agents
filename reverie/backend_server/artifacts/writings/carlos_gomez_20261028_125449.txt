I woke up at seven and didn’t bother looking for a phone. By seven-thirty, I was already writing. My notebook has got heavier than my body lately, and I feel like I’m carrying too much for one man.

The poems from my natural-world collection are still sharp, mostly. I like how the old oak feels in my head like a weathered friend I can’t quite name. Its roots hold the earth together, maybe even the neighborhood. I want to keep that kind of honesty. A poem shouldn’t be polite. It shouldn’t say, “The oak is important, and I’m proud of it.” Sometimes that’s too soft.

At the café display, I see the signs, the paintings, the people trying to make a place out of everything they don’t know. I want to honor them. I want to put the old oak on the community wall. Maybe I’ll write something about how we all have a root, even the ugly ones that hold us together.

But I also need to respect what I’m asking. I’m not just a poet who makes noise. I’m a resident who needs to feel safe here. I’m not here to turn every conversation into a performance. I’m here to listen, to write, and sometimes to stand where the oak grows.

Tonight, I’m going to work on a short piece about a woman who carries a bucket of rain in a city that forgot how to give it back. I won’t make it perfect. I’ll make it rough, and that’s the point.
