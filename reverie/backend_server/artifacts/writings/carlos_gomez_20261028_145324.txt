The oak is still standing. It has swallowed a hundred years of birds, roots, and rain. It is a green wall of teeth and roots, holding onto the soil even when the ground tries to pull it down. I walked beneath it this morning with my notebook open, pretending the leaves were pages.

“Look at this root system,” I told Latoya when we talked about the café display next door. “It isn’t just a tree. It’s a map.”

She looked at me over the rim of her cup. “You mean it knows where every leaf came from.”

“Maybe,” I said, “or maybe it just knows where it belongs.”

I finished my breakfast at seven, then sat down and read from the natural-world poems. I didn’t want to make them clever. I wanted them honest, like the oak. I wanted to describe the way the wind moves between branches, the way the rain runs through the veins of leaves, and how a single branch can change the whole pattern.

Sometimes I wonder if the poems are becoming too loud. Sometimes I wish I could make them softer. Sometimes I wonder if that softness is weakness. I think of my own voice: loud, sometimes rude, sometimes angry. But beneath all that, I’m trying to get at what makes me feel: the warmth of a shared space, the quiet weight of belonging, the need to belong without pretending.

I kept turning the pages until the words stopped sounding like practice and began sounding like a conversation.

I went home around ten. I ate dinner with Latoya. We talked about the oak and the café, about how community projects can preserve something older while still changing. I liked that idea. I didn’t need to fix every problem. I just needed to remember which things deserve care.

At ten, I turned off the lights and read one more poem aloud in the kitchen. The room filled with the sound of rain against the window. For the first time in a while, I didn’t want to argue with it. I wanted to listen.
