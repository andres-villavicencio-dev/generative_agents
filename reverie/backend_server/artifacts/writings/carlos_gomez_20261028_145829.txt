I woke before the sun came in. At 7:00. I ate breakfast alone, while the café behind the old oak hummed with whatever customers still came in, and I checked the poems I’d written in my head.

By 7:30, I had finished the morning routine. I drank water, brushed my teeth, and opened the notebook.

“Stop,” I told myself. “You’re making noise again.”

The old oak stood behind me, its branches tangled in the light like a knot of green. I remembered what the café had said about the old oak: how it held the neighborhood together, how it waited, how it was never quite ready to go. I had been too quick to treat it as a decoration in a community project. Not yet.

I picked up the final draft.

The climax was coming together. I wanted the poem to end without saying what I wanted it to say. I wanted it to feel like something the old oak had been waiting for all along.

I kept thinking of Latoya. She was talking about neighborhood needs, about what people actually needed, not what their poetry would make them feel. I wanted to honor her, but I also wanted to honor my own voice. Both were important.

Then I wrote:

Under the old oak, the bees forgot the way to map the city,  
And the coffee tasted like the rain had been spilled,  
Then the old oak said, you can’t always be the one standing still.

It was too close. It was too angry. I deleted it.

I opened a new page.

I wrote about the neighborhood. I wrote about the people walking past the café. I wrote about what was actually happening here. I wrote about the old oak standing beneath the trees like a witness.

I wrote:

The old oak didn’t need a community project to prove it was there.  
It needed a neighbor who remembered the sound of its roots.  
It needed a story that didn’t ask every tree to say what it had been waiting for.

I closed the page.

The poem was still unfinished. I could still make it harder. I could still make it louder. But I would make it honest.
