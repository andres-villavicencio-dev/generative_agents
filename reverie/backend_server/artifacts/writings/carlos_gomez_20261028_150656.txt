I woke at seven, the old oak groaning like it was waking up after a long sleep. I checked the coffee, then the calendar, then my notebook. I didn’t need any permission to be productive. That’s what the collection taught me: you don’t have to ask anyone what they want before you write what you need.

I revised the ending of “The Tree That Never Died.” The old oak stood across the street from the café, its branches spreading wide enough to swallow the sky. It had lost its leaves, its bark, its leaves again. It kept breathing. That’s the poem I wanted to remember.

But I was still thinking about Latoya. She asked me to stop talking about how many residents needed our community project and start asking what the residents had to say. I’ve been listening to a lot of voices lately—not just the ones I like, not just the ones I agree with. They’re tired, they’re loud, and they’re right.

I kept the line about the café’s sign. It was the strongest image I had: “Coffee for Everyone, But No One for Free.” It sounded nice, but it sounded like a joke. What I wanted was a sharper line—one that didn’t make the café look like charity. What was the real cost of giving people coffee?

Maybe the real cost was that the oak didn’t care whether I paid. Maybe that was the point.

I finished the last page and opened my laptop. I had three workshop notes, one poem draft, and one idea about the oak. I didn’t want to make the project sound perfect. I wanted it to sound real. And that meant admitting that the oak didn’t care, and that the café might be too generous, and that I was still learning how to listen.

I saved the revised draft. I saved the old oak. I saved the rest of Tuesday, and Tuesday’s noise.
