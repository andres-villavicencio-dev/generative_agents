The old oak at the corner café has stood there since the day the town bought the place. Its roots are deep, its branches reach across like a nervous system, and its bark is cracked and gray as if it had been holding onto too much of the sun. I came here to honor it, not to make it my shrine. I wanted to give it something real.

That’s how my morning goes: seven in the morning, then the collection. I start with the poems about the natural world—branches, birds, rain, roots. They are not pretty in every way. Sometimes they are angry, sometimes they are tired. They are honest because I’ve been honest for years.

By seven-thirty, I have a draft, a draft of the oak and the café, a draft of how I want the community to use the space. It is not a perfect poem. It is a proposal.

I have been asking Latoya what she wants. She does not want flowers or a pretty sign. She wants the café to work for people. She wants the old oak to be treated with care, not treated as a symbol of the town’s past. I do not have the answer yet. I only have the feeling that someone needs to be asked.

I am thirty-two, and I still have the old oak on my mind. I am also still loud. I am still rude. I am still trying to make myself useful. But I am also learning to listen.

Today I will write about the oak. Not the tree itself, but the tree in the space. I will write about the café, the people who sit there, the rain that washes the stones clean, the way the light changes through the branches. I will write about what I want the community to feel when the oak stands beside it.

At ten I will go to bed. At seven tomorrow I will wake up again. I will not make the oak perfect. I will only make it safe enough to be looked at.
