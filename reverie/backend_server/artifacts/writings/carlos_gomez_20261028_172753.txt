7:00 AM

The morning light slipped through the window like a broken promise. I didn’t need to pretend the old oak was anything special. It stood there, gnarled and tired, drinking the sun in every crack of bark. Its roots were roots of memory, tangled with the dirt that had carried the coffee shop through years of rain and construction.

My hand moved over the page before I even noticed. The poem was beginning to feel hollow, like a mouth opening without words. I needed to find something honest.

I traced the line about the café bird. It wasn’t trying to be pretty. It was nervous. It kept circling the branch, afraid the air would take it away. I wrote that fear like a secret I’d kept long enough to make it feel dangerous.

At seven thirty, I picked up my pen and reviewed the collection. Some of the old oak poems were sharp, yes. They had teeth. But there were too many of them. They were loud, almost angry. I had to make space for quieter things. For the bird. For the small crack in the pavement. For the fact that I still needed to listen without being heard.

At five, the dinner table sat waiting. I ate slowly. The food tasted like Tuesday. I wrote in the margins: I am not here to be perfect. I am here to show up.

My phone buzzed. Latoya was waiting outside the café. I didn’t answer immediately. I looked at the page, then down at the old oak. For a moment, I saw her face behind the glass door—her black hair, her sharp chin, the way she carried herself like someone who had survived every storm.

We talked about how the neighborhood was changing. About the café, about the old oak, about what people still needed. I didn’t try to make everything easier. I just made sure I remembered what mattered.

I finished dinner at five. The birds were still circling. I wrote one last line. Not to be famous. Not to impress anyone. Just to say that even though the city felt full, the trees could still hold us.

I turned off the lamp and went to bed at ten. The oak would have to wait. But I would be there.
