I’m standing in the community center, looking at the old oak and café display. The flowers are arranged in a circle like a wedding ring. I know what I want to show here. I want to show what happens when people stop seeing each other and start pretending they’re the only ones who matter.

I’m drafting the third stanza. Maybe I’ll begin with a character who carries a quiet anger. Someone who believes that kindness is weakness. Maybe he’ll say he’s never been loved. Maybe he’ll say he never hurt anyone. But the truth is, he did. And he’s probably still hurting, even if he doesn’t know how to speak about it.

What happens when someone finally speaks? Sometimes they’re furious. Sometimes they’re ashamed. Sometimes they’re terrified. I want the conflict to be honest. I want the question to be real: How do we make connection without turning the whole world into a performance?

I’ve heard critics say I’m too loud, too rude, too careless. That sounds like an insult. But I’ve learned to listen better. I’m not just shouting words anymore. I’m asking people what they really feel. I want to honor the oak and café, not just make a pretty picture. I want to understand how residents live, what they need, and how I can change my poetry to serve them.

Maybe the poem should end with a simple question: What if everyone stopped pretending and just showed up?
