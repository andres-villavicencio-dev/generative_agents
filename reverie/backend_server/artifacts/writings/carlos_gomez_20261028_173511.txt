I woke up at seven, ate breakfast like I owed the morning to myself, and then looked at the natural-world poems for a minute. The old oak, the café, the way the birds stopped to count the seconds—those are the things I want to keep. Not polished, not pretending to care. Just real.

This is where I’m going to write the sixth stanza. Not to fix the poem, but to make it sound like something I actually care about.

I’m not writing about trees or birds or coffee anymore. I’m writing about how we keep the old things alive while pretending we’re busy growing something new. People keep the café open, the oak standing, the old habits waiting for permission to return. We’re all pretending the neighborhood doesn’t know what’s being sold. We’re selling silence, pretending we’re not losing our place.

My collection is still here. It’s still noisy. Still messy. Still loud.

Now I’m going to write a stanza that restates the whole thing. A stanza that says, without turning the old oak into a character, that we don’t need to save everything. We just need to let it stay there so the café won’t have to pretend to be alive. We need to honor the old things before we try to make them fit into our own neat schedules.

The old oak doesn’t have to be thanked. The café doesn’t have to be saved. But the whole system does. And that’s what this stanza is going to be.
