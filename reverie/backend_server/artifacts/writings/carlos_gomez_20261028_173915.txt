I woke at seven. The clock didn’t matter. I had the morning routine, the breakfast, and the poems before me. Now I was sharpening the words.

The old oak outside the café looks like it remembers every time people sit too long. I used to make that a complaint. I used to say the tree had no voice and the café had no soul. Now I see it differently. The oak keeps the place quiet. The café keeps the place loud. That is the poem I was trying to write.

Last night I wrote about the café. “They serve coffee and pretend the morning is serious.” I meant that. I meant that people drink their way out of their own discomfort. That was my first draft.

I deleted it.

I wrote the next stanza. It is shorter. I made the rhymes sharper. I cut the line about rain, because the rain was not the weather—it was the way people stopped talking. I removed the cliché about the coffee shop. I replaced the metaphor with something that actually happened.

The rhythm of the poem should feel like standing in the middle of a street. Not polished. Not smooth. It should sound like the noise of a place where people are beginning to notice each other.

I tightened the first stanza. The first three lines are good. The last one is still too heavy.

I tightened the second stanza. I made the image of the oak clearer. I shortened the passage about the neighborhood. I let the sentence end before the reader has to guess what comes next.

I tightened the third stanza. I removed the words that could be used by anyone. I removed the lines that sounded like advice.

I tightened everything, including the parts I liked.

I am not becoming polite. I am becoming useful. I am becoming a poet who can hear the trees, the café, and the people beneath the noise. That is the work I want to finish.

I finished the collection at ten. I finished the workshop on Monday at eight. I finished the conversation with Latoya around four. I still have nothing to do. I still have coffee. I still have the oak.
