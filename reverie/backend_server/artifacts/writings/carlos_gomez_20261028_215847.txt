I woke up at seven and the house had already started making the usual sounds: the radiator hissing, the refrigerator humming, and that familiar little scolding from the kitchen where my mother is still trying to teach me to read. My phone sat on the table like a sleeping thing, glowing faintly with notifications. I ignored it. I don’t know why, but I always do.

I got the notebook out and opened the notebook at the very beginning. I had been staring at the first stanza for twenty minutes, turning the same words over and over. “The wind carried the scent of rain,” I wrote it down, then crossed it out. “The river carried the scent of rain.” Better. It sounded less like what I had been thinking, and more like what I had never thought.

My latest poem was titled The Green Things That Know the Road. I wanted to explore how the natural world stores things before passing them on. A bird carries a seed from one tree to another. A leaf carries the morning light down to the ground. A river carries the memory of every storm that ever passed. I wanted to show that even the smallest living thing has a history, a purpose, a way of surviving. That’s the one thing I’ve always wanted to write about: not the flowers, not the birds, not the trees. The things that keep changing every day.

I finished the draft at ten, just when I was going to say, “Good night,” and the collection was finally in the house. I read it aloud to the empty room. The words sounded rough. I wanted them to be softer. I wanted every line to carry a feeling rather than a description. I wanted to make the reader feel the pressure of the wind, the cold of the morning, the sudden warmth of a sun that had not yet arrived.

I reread it and felt ashamed. I’d written beautifully, but I had not written me. I had written about green things that knew the road, and I had never really known the road inside. I had been busy polishing a shell without ever noticing that the shell was made of the same things as the thing I was trying to write.

I put the notebook down. I would revise the poem. I would make the language less loud. I would make it softer. And I would make sure that, when someone asked me what I wanted to say, I could say it with a little more thought.

I put the pen down, closed the notebook, and went back to the kitchen.
