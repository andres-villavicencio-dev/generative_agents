I didn’t write that poem. I wrote it because the line felt too polite.

“Where the river bends, the river keeps its promise,” I told myself while circling the line on the page. It sounded like a tourist brochure. It sounded like the kind of advice I’d give to a child who had lost his father. But the river was always angry. I’d been watching it through a window for years, listening to the stones grind against one another. The water was never gentle. It was stubborn. It took up space. It didn’t care who was looking at it.

So I rewrote it.

Where the river bends, it doesn’t promise to stay.

That was better. It admitted the river’s cruelty. It admitted that the bend was not a gift, but a choice. Maybe the poem was supposed to make the river sound like a lover. That wouldn’t have been honest. Poetry doesn’t need to be soft unless the subject deserves it. I was trying to make the river look human, when it was just water doing what water does.

The workshop leader told me that revision is like editing a wound. You don’t smooth it away. You let it bleed a little, then you decide whether to cover it or let it show. I’m thirty-two. I’ve written bad lines. I still do. I wrote the line about the river as if I could fix it with a better word. That was my fault.

I’m working on a collection about the natural world. Not the pretty ones. The ones that bruise, the ones that hold secrets in their roots, the ones that carry away whatever comes near them. I need to stop pretending the trees have feelings. They don’t. The wind does. The wind takes everything and leaves no trace.

I also need to stop thinking every line has to sound like a sermon. That’s what makes me sound rude. I’m not being mean. I’m being honest.

The river still looks the same at dawn. I’m not going to change it. But I might try to say something honest about it.
