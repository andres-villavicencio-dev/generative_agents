I wrote this poem at 7:45 a.m., the first thing I did after waking. The city was still sleeping, but the wind outside was awake. I stood on the sidewalk and listened. The streetcars pulled away in a long, metallic song. A few pigeons landed on the broken pavement and began arguing about who was entitled to the crumbs. I wanted to capture that moment before it vanished.

I thought of the oak tree down the street. Its branches looked like tangled fists reaching for the sky. Roots went down without apology. In my poems, the natural world shouldn’t feel too clean or too perfect. It should be messy, loud, and sometimes terrifying. I wrote:

Roots pull when the world tries to pull them away,  
Leaves turn gold before they know why.  
A bird drops a seed and doesn’t apologize for it.

But then I rewrote it. The word “apologize” felt too human, too soft. I changed it to:

Roots pull when the world tries to pull them away,  
Leaves turn gold before they know why,  
A bird drops a seed without asking for permission.

That’s when I realized I had been treating nature like a landscape I was decorating. Nature isn’t there to impress. Nature is there to show me what I’ve been hiding.

The next workshop, I was told to write a poem from the perspective of a river. I wanted to write from the stone. I wanted to write from the gravel between my toes. I wanted to write from the small, stubborn things that survive.

I wrote the river’s voice in my notebook. It was angry at the banks, polite to the mud, and utterly indifferent to my mistakes. Then I wrote about the fallen branch. It didn’t blame the storm. It just waited.

I kept the revised lines. I didn’t keep the whole poem. Sometimes a line is finished before it’s finished. Sometimes, a poem becomes nothing more than a place I’m allowed to stand.

I put the notebook down. The sun was still low. The wind carried the scent of wet stone and something sharper than rain. I had spent the morning looking for beauty. I had found it. But the real work begins when I decide whether to keep it.
