The workshop room smelled like old paper and coffee. I pulled up my notes. “Don’t be nice to the rhymes,” said the instructor. “Make them bite.”

I looked at the page. “It’s not about biting. It’s about truth.”

“Truth must be rhymed,” the instructor said. “Or it’s just noise.”

I opened the page.

The morning sun was already warm enough to wake the city, but I was still awake. My stomach said stop eating. My hands said stop writing. And my head kept shouting, I’m late, I’m tired, I’m going to ruin everything.

I sat down and read the draft with the intensity of a man trying to make his own life make sense. I marked the weak lines. I tightened the rhythm. I made the imagery sharper. For one minute, I was not a poet who needed to impress anyone. I was just Carlos, thirty-two, exhausted, hungry, and finally paying attention.

Afterward, I sat with the room and said, “What if the poem isn’t meant to be perfect?”

The room went quiet. Then one person laughed. Another muttered something about being too sensitive. I smiled at them. I didn’t want to be rude. I didn’t want to sound toxic. I wanted to be honest.

So I kept refining. I sharpened the meter. I tightened the rhyme. I let the imagery grow like a plant through a hard winter. I didn’t fix the whole thing in one afternoon. I kept doing it because it felt like the only way to keep writing.

At seven, I wake up. I make coffee. I check the calendar. I go to Sam’s coffee place. I prepare for Rajiv’s solo show. I talk to Sharon. I eat dinner. I go to bed at ten.

Nothing is perfect. Nothing is easy.

But sometimes, when the noise fades and the words sit still on the page, I remember that I am allowed to be loud enough to say what I feel. And sometimes, being rude is just the price of being real.
