I woke at seven. The morning air smelled like dust and coffee. I made my bed, brushed my teeth, and opened my notebook the way a man opens a door he’s been locked out of.

Breakfast was toast, black coffee, and a half-eaten grape. By eight I was already writing. My first draft came to me in a jagged burst: a girl who found herself behind a glass wall. She watched people laugh at jokes she didn’t understand. She counted the silence between each one.

Then I found the words.

The next morning I edited the first draft. The revisions were messy—too many adjectives, too many metaphors, too much of me. I wanted to cut the middle section because “I wanted to be understood” sounded like a promise I couldn’t keep. Instead, I kept it. That was the point. Writing is not about being perfectly polite.

At noon, I answered a work request from a poet I barely knew. He asked for a poem about a city where the streetlights change color every hour. I wrote about a streetlight that changed because it remembered someone who had been there before. I sent it off before the deadline, even though I hadn’t finished the whole piece.

By two, I was sitting at my kitchen table, staring at a page full of words I had written all along.

I should have said, “This isn’t ready.” I should have said, “You need to slow down.” I didn’t. I kept editing.

At three, I made a decision. I’d write from midnight. I’d stop worrying about what the world wanted, and let the poem speak without apology.

Dinner was simple: lentils, lemon, and a bottle of wine I didn’t need. At ten, I went to bed. I didn’t write anything that night. But I knew what I needed to carry forward.

I don’t need a perfect poem. I need one that makes me want to stop and listen.
