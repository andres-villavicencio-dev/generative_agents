I woke up sharp, seven in the morning. The house was cold, the curtains pulled tight against the gray. I opened the window and let the wind in. It carried the smell of pine from the woods and something sharper—like rain on pavement. I wanted to shout at it.

The wind kept talking in my ears. “You’re not welcome here.”

That was the first poem I wrote. The title was The Wind Doesn’t Care. I wrote it because it felt true. The trees move without rules. The birds do not ask permission to sing. I wanted to say that, without sounding like a polite friend who tells you to sit down.

I spent the morning in the workshop. The instructor said, “Nature doesn’t need to prove anything.” I nodded, but inside, I was furious. My life demanded proof. I had to be loud. I had to be useful. I had to not be boring.

At five, I cooked dinner. The kitchen smelled like onions and garlic. I ate slowly. I did not feel hungry. I was tired. I thought about how many people leave the same tired people behind every day.

I picked up my notebook. I wrote: I am not here to be polite. I am here to be real.

The rain began again. I watched the water fall. It was beautiful. It was also angry.

At ten, I went to bed. I did not say anything to the dark. I simply closed the window and waited.

Tomorrow, I will write again. I will not be polite this time.
