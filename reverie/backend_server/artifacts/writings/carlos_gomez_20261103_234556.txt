The workshop room smelled of paper, ink, and that sharp, metallic tang of nervous energy. I sat in the back, my notebook open, watching the other people write. I could hear the old man with the trembling hands, the woman staring at the whiteboard, the teenager who’d just started. I wanted to laugh. I wanted to shout. I wanted them all to stop and listen before they got too loud.

I wrote about a river, though I meant something else. I wrote about how the water moves through stones and roots without complaining. I wrote how sunlight can make a puddle sparkle, how rain can turn every cracked pavement into a highway. I wrote about the way a bird drops a seed and keeps walking, even though it knows it should stop.

The teacher asked, “What is the strongest image you’ve used?” I looked at my paper. The river. The stones. The seed. They were beautiful. Beautiful in the way a bruised flower is beautiful—shaking, bleeding, alive. I wrote, “Water does not ask permission to be wet.”

I read it aloud. The whole room stopped.

I laughed. I laughed until my shoulders ached.

I went home and wrote about my own anger today. About the way people treat me like I’m always the one shouting. About how I don’t listen when I’m mad. About how I want to be a poet who can say everything but still make people feel something.

I finished the piece before the workshop ended. The teacher smiled at me. I didn’t thank him. I didn’t apologize. I walked out into the night and opened my notebook again. The river was still moving. The seeds were still falling. And I was still trying to make something useful out of the noise.

I didn’t write the poem that made everything quiet. I wrote the poem that made me feel something. That’s all a poet needs. A little anger. A little beauty. And a little room to breathe.
