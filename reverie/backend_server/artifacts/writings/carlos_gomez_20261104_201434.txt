I sat down at the table, my notebook open, but my thoughts kept moving. I looked around the room—the other poets, the quiet woman who looked like she’d been given a life, the man who smelled like old paper and coffee. I was here for the workshop, yes. But I was also here because I needed to be noticed.

The facilitator was talking about how every poem has a place, a shape, a reason for being. She said something about rhythm and silence, about letting the reader hear the space between the words. I nodded. I should have said something. The room seemed to be listening, but I didn’t say anything. Maybe that was rude.

I stood up.

“Who’s here for the poetry?” the facilitator asked.

“Everyone,” I said.

“A poet is never alone,” she said.

I took a breath. My throat had been tight for the last hour. I looked around again. One man was holding a notebook. A woman was writing in her journal. The noise wasn’t annoying me. It was a little loud. But it was also honest.

“Who wrote the first poem?” I asked.

The woman smiled. “Mine. I’ve been trying to get rid of the ending.”

I laughed. It came out rough. “I don’t see any ending.”

“Maybe it’s just an unfinished sentence,” she said.

I looked at the room. The poets were all looking at me. I felt exposed. I felt loud. I felt like I had done something wrong.

“Okay,” I said. “Who’s coming up next?”

I opened my notebook. The first page was blank. For a moment, I didn’t know what to write. Then I wrote:

The forest made a sound like a thousand people speaking at once,
each voice different, each one asking why.

I closed the notebook and looked at my hands. They were shaking a little. I was sure I’d made a mistake. But I didn’t stop writing. I kept going.
