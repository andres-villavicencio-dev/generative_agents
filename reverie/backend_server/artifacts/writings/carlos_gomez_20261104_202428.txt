I was standing in front of Jane at Hobbs Cafe, watching her slice the lemon over the salad. She’d made it herself—bright, zesty, impossible to ignore. I didn’t notice the way her fingertips shook. I didn’t notice the way the light caught the oil in the dressing.

I’d been eating lunch too quickly, too loudly, too much. I’d said to myself, “What kind of poet am I? I’m a loud, rude, toxic person who talks over everything.” But here, at Hobbs Cafe, with Jane sitting beside me, I realized that was only how I was talking to myself.

She looked up. “You’re staring at the salad,” she said.

I looked back. “I’m not staring at the salad. I’m studying its edges.”

“Your poetry,” she said.

I looked down at the pages in my notebook. They were messy. The words were rough, sometimes angry, sometimes beautiful. Most of them had nothing to do with nature. They were about how I felt trapped inside my own head, how I couldn’t stop talking when nobody was listening.

I opened the notebook and wrote:

Nature doesn’t apologize for being messy. It doesn’t pretend to be perfect. It has its own rough edges, its own sharp corners, and it doesn’t ask you to be polite.

I kept writing until the page was full. I kept writing until the words sounded like them—like the lemon and the salad and the way Jane’s eyes narrowed when she noticed something important.

I should have been proud. I should have been proud of every line, every word, every moment of frustration. But I wasn’t. I was just a poet trying to figure out how to be honest without sounding like a monster.

Maybe I’m still trying to be a monster.

Maybe I’m not.

I finished the poem. I looked at Jane. She wasn’t smiling. She looked tired. But she was listening.

I wrote another line.
