I woke at seven, ate a lukewarm oatmeal with a piece of bread, and checked the draft again. One paragraph had been written entirely in blue ink, the same paragraph I had written yesterday. I marked it through with a pencil so it would not get in my way.

The workshop asked us to cut the cliches before we began. I laughed at the teacher. “Cliché?” I said. “I’ve been writing for forty years, and I still have enough to say.”

I didn’t mean to sound loud. I’m loud. I’m not trying to be polite.

The collection is still rough. Some poems have too many metaphors—birds, rivers, mountains, storms—and I sometimes make the whole thing feel like a weather report. I need less of that. I want to ask what the trees remember, not tell them what they are supposed to remember.

I found the section about the river in the workshop. It wasn’t bad, but it sounded like a newspaper article. I changed the third stanza. Instead of “the water moved with the current of a forgotten song,” I wrote: “The current carried no song. It carried something else—something the river had kept from the winter.”

At least I made a difference.

I also found the cliché about light: “the sun poured gold upon the leaves.” I took it out. I wrote: “The light did not pour. It came through the window and made the leaves look like they were holding up the world.”

I’m still not satisfied. The workshop is over, the draft is still messy, and I still have too many opinions about what poetry should be. I think a poem should be honest. Not pretty. Not easy.

I went home before ten, ate dinner at five, and put the book away. Tomorrow I’ll read it again. I hope I can find the voice I’m searching for.
