I woke up at 7:00 a.m. with my phone buzzing, a reminder that my poetry collection is still unfinished. I had been dreaming about the workshop, talking to people who seemed to believe that writing was the one thing worth waiting for. I am thirty-two, loud, rude, and still somehow trying to be the poet everyone keeps expecting me to be.

The workshop was scheduled for Tuesday, February 14. The facilitator called it a “creative-writing workshop: The Weight of Words.” She said the theme should be honest, not pretty. I told her that was exactly what I wanted. I sat through twenty minutes of reading someone’s poem, then pointed out how the middle section sounded like a man talking to a stranger. The room was polite, almost bored. I laughed and said something about how poets should never be polite, then left to make my own noise.

We discussed the main theme: poetry is not a performance; it is a confession. Carlos, I said, my hand shaking slightly. She nodded. It felt good.

Then we had our creative task. We were given twenty minutes to write about a thing I’ve been avoiding: the coffee shop where I met my friends. I wrote about the smell of roasted beans, the way the door opened, and how, for the first time in years, I wanted to be brave enough to admit I hated how easy it was to be invisible. At the end, I wrote, “I am not the loudest person in the room. I am the one who still speaks before everyone else has finished.”

I finished the page, then sat down and read it aloud to the room. It was loud, a little rough, and full of things nobody wanted to hear.

I walked home with my phone in one hand and my collection in the other. My friends were waiting outside, and I was ready to apologize for everything I’ve ever done wrong. I walked into the coffee shop, ordered a large mug, and listened to my words while the barista made it.
