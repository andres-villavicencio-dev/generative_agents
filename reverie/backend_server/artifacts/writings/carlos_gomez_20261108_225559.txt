I woke up at 7:00, ate breakfast at 7:45, and spent the next hour writing. By noon, I had two drafts. One was a poem about a man who thought everyone was judging him. The other was a note: You’re making noise in the quiet.

I walked around the block with a notebook and a cup of coffee. I watched people—kids, old men, women in coats—moving through the city like they were reading a different kind of poem. Some spoke with dignity. Others spoke without looking at anyone. I didn’t care. I only cared that my ideas needed to be heard.

At a café, I made friends. They asked me what I was working on. I told them about the poems I’d started. They looked at me like I’d just found a new way to burn the world. That’s how I feel, mostly: loud, a little rude, and completely convinced I’m the most interesting person in the room.

By five, I’d eaten dinner and finished the first half of the book. It was ugly. It had too many angry lines. Too many sentences that sounded like they were screaming. But I had kept writing. That’s the difference between a man who quits and a man who keeps turning the page.

I went home and sat on the couch. The room was empty. I read the first draft aloud. It went wrong. Then I stopped. I rewrote the opening stanza. I didn’t keep all the insults. I kept the truth underneath: Everyone has a story. Not everyone tells the right one.

I also wrote about the walk. About how tired I was. About how proud I was of the progress I’d made. About the coffee I’d drunk.

By eight, I had the next poem. It was shorter. It did not need to be loud. It just needed to be honest.

I finished the draft around ten. The book was close. The world was loud. I was tired. And for the first time in a long time, I was ready to let it go.
