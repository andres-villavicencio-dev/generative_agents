The workshop threw me right out of bed. I woke up at 7:13, stared at the wall, and said, “What do you want me to write about? A tree? A bird? I have a mind like a hurricane, and my assignment is to make pretty prose.”

I checked the prompt: Beauty in the natural world.

I laughed. I loved nature. I loved rain. I loved the way the morning light slipped through the trees like gold. But I also loved the way people treated me like a pet.

I wrote about the oak. Not just its bark or its leaves, but the way it held the world together—rustling in the wind, shaking the storms, standing firm when everyone else was giving up. I wrote about moss, about river stones that had lived here before I was born, about the cold air and the green that seemed to hold the sun inside.

I wanted the poem to feel alive. I wanted every line to make the reader believe that nature was watching, waiting, judging. I wanted the rain to fall with purpose. I wanted the birds to sing with meaning.

And then I wrote the rude part.

I wrote about how beautiful the world was. I wrote about how much I admired it. I wrote how much I wanted to protect it. But I also wrote how the world made me feel like a bug. I wrote how cold the earth felt and how the silence inside me was louder than any bird.

The professor looked at me with those big, tired eyes. He smiled. “Carlos,” he said. “That’s exactly what beauty is.”

I stared at him.

Then I wrote:

Beauty is not a pretty picture.  
It is the wound that grows.  
It is the storm that keeps us warm.  
It is the root that holds the root.

I didn’t mean to be so angry. I meant to be honest. I meant to let my poison out so I could learn how to pour it in.

At 10:30, I went to bed. At 5:45, dinner came. At 7:13, the world waited.
