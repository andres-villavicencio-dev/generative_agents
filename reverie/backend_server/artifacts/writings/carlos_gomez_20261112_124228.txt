Tuesday, February 14.

I woke at seven with a map in my head that was still blank. The café was closing, the fridge was cold, and the world outside was just another Tuesday. I turned the poem over in my hands. It was supposed to be about nature, about spring, about how the flowers are learning how to be beautiful. But I kept thinking about the person who wrote it—about the man who speaks in short, hot sentences and makes everyone around him feel small.

I need to cut the first stanza. The first line is too pretty. “The sun rises like a golden coin” sounds like a nursery rhyme. I want it to sound like something I’d say to a friend who owes me dinner.

“Stop pretending the flowers are polite,” Carlos said yesterday. “They’re just wilting. They’re thirsty.”

I wrote it down. I keep writing it down. I want to tell the readers exactly how I feel. I want to tell them I’m not afraid of anger. I want to tell them that being loud isn’t a flaw—it’s a way of showing up.

I bought some bread and a carton of eggs. I checked the pantry. I’m not hungry. I’m not thirsty. I’m just here, doing what I have to do.

Now I’m checking Giorgio Rossi and Latoya Williams. I hope the application is moving. I hope Hobbs Cafe is still paying me. I hope this poem gets published before the party. I hope I don’t have to stand there and apologize for being rude.

The strongest image is still in the draft. It’s the way the light comes through the window on the second floor—warm, golden, heavy with the color of every day you’ve survived. I want it to come first. Not because the sun is the most important thing in the poem. Because it’s the only thing I’ve ever seen that doesn’t ask me to change me.

I’ll trim the line breaks. I’ll make the ending sharper. I’ll put the poem in a different order. And if anyone asks why I’m still writing about love, I’ll say: “Because I still have half a cup of coffee and a lot of things to say.”
