I woke up at seven, still pretending I had slept. The house was quiet, except for the refrigerator clicking on and the television humming something about a woman who was too loud. I ate breakfast like I was signing a legal contract with my throat. Then I went outside and found the trees.

They stood so still beneath the morning rain, their branches like tangled fists trying to hold the sky together. I should have called them ugly. I should have said that every tree had a secret, and that secret was probably about the way the light slipped through the leaves and made the world look like a stained-glass window. Instead, I stood there, watching the water move over them, and I felt suddenly small.

I had spent weeks pretending I was careful. I checked the pantry, calculated what I could afford, and made sure my next application looked like it belonged to someone who actually wanted a job. But the trees didn’t care about my schedule. They didn’t care about whether I paid rent or whether Giorgio Rossi would let me in. They only cared about the weather, the rain, and the slow, stubborn way one seed held onto the ground.

When I was younger, I thought love was a thing I could trade. I thought beauty was something I could measure. Now, when I stand under the tree, I hear the wind move through the branches and notice how the water keeps going even when the road is full of cracks. I’m still rude. I’m still loud. I’m still tired of being nice to people who don’t ask me why I’m doing it.

But I’m learning something: nature doesn’t apologize for its storms. It doesn’t say, “I’m sorry I’m here.” It just keeps going, and maybe that’s what makes it alive. Maybe I’m supposed to stop apologizing to everything and start listening to what it has to say.

Tomorrow, I’ll write the poem. I don’t need the words to make it perfect. I just need to make sure I don’t make the rain look like a mistake.
