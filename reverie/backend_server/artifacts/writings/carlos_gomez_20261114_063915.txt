The workshop leader gave me a blank page at the beginning. I stared at it for three minutes. What could I write? I had written about trees, storms, and rain before. The workshop leader said, “Poetry isn’t about pretending the world is perfect. It’s about showing what it is.”

That was the first thing I needed to hear.

I started writing about a river, but I was too careless. I wrote about how it hurtled past, how it carried rocks and weeds, how the water seemed angry. I wanted the river to look like a character. I did not want the river to care.

The leader pointed out my sentences. “Your ‘it had a voice’ is vague. What does the river actually say?” I said, “Maybe nothing.” He said, “Poetry doesn’t always need a name.” He also asked me to cut the middle paragraph about how I was bored by the class.

I looked down at my notes. I had spent three weeks practicing my voice, but I still sounded like I was trying to impress people. I had a collection of poems about the natural world, and I wanted them to feel like something alive. But they still sounded like a list of observations.

I began rewriting. I kept the river, but I made the river feel like a person who had been hurt. I shortened the lines that made me sound too clever. I removed the comparison of me to the landscape and replaced it with a direct observation. I wrote about the way the rain hit the branches, about the cold air, about the sound of water in my head.

The workshop leader said, “You don’t have to fix everything today. Just write what you hear.”

I wrote again. I still made mistakes. I still wrote too fast. But I didn’t want the river to be perfect. I wanted it to be honest.

By the end of the session, I had a new draft. It was not polished. It was messy, loud, and sometimes rude. And that was the point. Poetry is not about having everything figured out. It is about having the courage to say what you see, hear, and feel.

I finished the poem. I didn’t say what the river did. I did not say how beautiful it was. I wrote about its coldness, its movement, and the way it kept on going even when I wanted it to stop.

Maybe that’s what the workshop leaders were trying to teach me.
