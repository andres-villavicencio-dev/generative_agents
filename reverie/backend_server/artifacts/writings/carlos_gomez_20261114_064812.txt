The workshop asked us to read our revised work. Carlos Gomez stared at the page. His lips moved without sound. He did not answer.

The poem began with a river. It began with ice cracking under the sun. Then came the birds. The river did not care. It kept going.

I had wanted it to be angry. I wanted it to be loud. But it was not. It was patient. It was not rude. It was beautiful.

That was the problem.

The workshop instructor said, “Your tone is too soft.”

Too soft? I said, “It’s too honest.”

Too honest. That is a virtue.

I wrote about the river not as a hero. I wrote about the river as a man who had done enough. I wrote about the stones that had survived centuries of storms. I wrote about the moss clinging to broken wood. I wrote about the small things that refused to disappear.

The river is not a character. It is a force. A force that does not apologize for the rocks it carries. A force that does not care whether you are watching it. A force that keeps moving because nothing can stop it.

But I wanted to make it sound like me. I wanted to make it sound like a man who has never learned how to sit quietly. I wanted to make it sound like something I would say to the world if it was not too difficult.

The instructor looked at me. He did not laugh.

“Your meaning,” he said, “is complicated.”

“I want it to be simple,” I said.

“It must have weight.”

I took my pen again. I crossed out the last paragraph. I tore it out. I wrote the river over again.

I do not know if this is good enough. I do not know if the words are beautiful. I do not know what this poem will cost me.

But the river kept moving.

I put the page down. I stood up. I walked out of the room. The instructor followed me.

“Stop,” he said.

“I’m going,” I said.

He did not stop.

I walked out into the rain. The rain did not care about my mood. It fell. It washed the dust from the pavement. It washed the old stones clean. It made the world look small again.

I did not want the poem to be perfect. I wanted the river to be honest.

I kept walking.
