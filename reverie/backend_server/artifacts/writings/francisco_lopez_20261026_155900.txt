I woke up at 7:00 AM, opened the notebook, and made the three candidates side-by-side. Budgets were clear: Candidate A got $22,500 from municipal grants, B from a private arts fund, and C from a hybrid pool. Funding sources were the real difference between them. A leaned toward artistic freedom and long-term sustainability, B toward immediate impact, and C toward a balanced mix. The community feedback section was where the real work was.

I read the reviews aloud. “This proposal feels too perfect,” one neighbor said. “It assumes everyone agrees with it all at once.” “It also looks like someone tried too hard to make every wall and poster look the same,” another replied. I marked those points. A mural fragment was a little dark and inconsistent with the café’s warm color scheme. The old radio was too close to the sidewalk, and the digital display didn’t have enough buffer.

I spent about forty minutes comparing the artistic possibilities. Candidate A had the strongest visual path and the most flexible design, but the budget was the smallest. Candidate B had a tighter budget and could move faster, yet the community felt it was less inclusive. Candidate C looked good on paper, but its funding and artistic scope made it vulnerable.

I made my recommendation: A is the strongest choice. It balances artistic ambition with the community’s need for practical, inclusive participation. I’ll adjust the color palette, soften the mural’s shadow, and make sure the digital display connects to the old radio rather than competing with it.

I also talked through the script with Latoya. She liked the idea of an opening where the candidates were introduced through a small group improv. I wrote a three-part sequence: one person says the proposal is “too perfect,” another says it wants to “move too fast,” and the third asks, “How do you know they’re all wrong?” It felt awkward, but it was honest, funny, and memorable.

I also finished the improv class. Francisco and Mara practiced a scene where the audience suddenly interrupts a rehearsal, and Mara told the class, “The best improv is realizing people want to be wrong.” I laughed while I wrote.

By the end of the day, I still had one last thing to check: the budget totals. I added them into a spreadsheet and double-checked the source lines. I felt tired, but also energized. I had more than one proposal, and I had more than one person to convince. Maybe that’s the point of the web series: to make complicated choices feel like a good story.

I saved the final script and the comparison sheet. I still had a little time to review the mural fragments before the presentation, but for now, I was ready to move forward.
