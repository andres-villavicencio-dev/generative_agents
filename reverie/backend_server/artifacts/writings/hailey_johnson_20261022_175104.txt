The cursor blinked at the end of the chapter while rain tapped the window behind me. I’d been staring at the same paragraph for so long that the words had started to look like strangers. Worse, they were strangers repeating themselves.

Mara sat cross-legged on the kitchen counter, sculpting a tiny clay bird. Every few minutes, she glanced toward my screen. “You’ve been making that same sigh for twenty minutes,” she said.

“I’m revising,” I told her.

“That’s what sighing means?”

I minimized the document and opened my notes. The chapter needed work. I could feel it—the scene had all the energy of a crowded train, but nobody knew where they were going. Even Lena, who had returned to the house at dawn with paint beneath her fingernails, seemed to exist outside the story instead of inside it.

I returned to the paragraph and began cutting. A sentence here. Three adjectives there. By the time I reached the second draft, the room had grown dim. I flicked on the desk lamp and wrote tomorrow’s tasks in a fresh note: fix the timeline, clarify why Theo vanished for two days, ask Nia about the rooftop scene, and research traditional music from coastal Peru for the final chapter.

The book had changed direction three times. First, it was about a collective. Then it became about a home. Now I knew it was about the dangerous, impossible act of making a life among other people. That was the sentence I needed at the beginning of every chapter.

I reread the ending. Lena stood in the doorway with an old record sleeve pressed to her chest, smiling as if she knew a secret the rest of us had missed. It was quiet, but not empty. I left it exactly as it was.

Outside, the city answered with traffic and the distant hum of a late bus. Somewhere across town, a drummer practiced the same stubborn rhythm over and over. I caught the beat and tapped my fingers against the desk.

Tomorrow, the artists would come home. Tonight, I had to make sure the door was ready for them.
