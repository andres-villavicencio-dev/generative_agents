It’s 10:14 on a Tuesday. Rain taps against the window, and I’m finishing the chapter in the kitchen when my phone buzzes. It’s an episode of The Co-Living Room, a podcast I started last weekend. I’m supposed to record three hours of audio, but I’ve been thinking more about the story than the schedule.

The central conflict is simple: five artists share a cramped co-living space, and their shared work threatens to destroy the place. Each one believes their art is the only way to make the space meaningful, while their differing styles, histories, and personal expectations make cooperation impossible. The tension isn’t just creative—it’s about belonging, ownership, and what it means to create together.

I’ve collected audio notes from the novel’s central conflict. They’re scattered across my phone, notebook, and a few scattered thoughts. I’ve reviewed them, and I’m surprised how much I still hear the same pattern: every argument begins with a small act of care, then quickly becomes a small act of resistance. The strongest connections between the notes are that the artists are not simply fighting—they are trying to understand each other. Their strongest shared trait is their belief that the space deserves meaning, even if they disagree on how to earn it.

I’ve been working on a podcast outline, too. I’m thinking of giving each artist a distinct voice and letting their conflicts reveal the co-living arrangement. I want to hear the quiet moments too—the arguments over furniture, the late-night revisions, the food, the friends who show up when no one else is home. I want to make the podcast sound like the space itself.

I’m also reworking the chapter. I need to tighten the ending so the conflict doesn’t feel like it just ends. I want the last paragraph to linger, not summarize.

I’m going to bed at two. I’ll record the episode around 6, then review the notes afterward. I’ll listen to my strongest audio notes, connect them to the chapter, and try to write without letting the podcast interfere with the story.
