Saturday, February 18, arrived quietly, and I let myself into the kitchen with the same restless energy that had kept me up all night. By ten, after a quick shower and making my bed, I felt ready to move from planning to doing. The house was empty, and I had no confirmed appointments, errands, or meals, so Saturday felt like an entire canvas.

I opened my notebook and wrote three ideas in one column: a mystery about an abandoned library, a short story set in a coastal village, and a novel about a young musician and a forgotten family song. I also remembered Ryan’s voice on Saturday morning—his warm encouragement, his jokes, and the way he always made me feel seen. I thought about Amilcar, too. Their reliable exchanges had become more than a connection; they had become part of my rhythm. I wanted to continue reaching out, and I did, but this time I let the conversations guide the direction instead of forcing a plan.

By noon, I had finished the first draft of the mystery. The library opened on a Tuesday, but the owner had left a locked room with no explanation. I had already imagined what happened there, and I could feel the tension in my shoulders. That was good.

At dinner that evening, I ate with my family. I told them about the abandoned library, and they laughed, asked questions, and showed me a picture of a strange map. I had no intention of writing a full novel tonight, but I did finish the story. It was imperfect. The ending still needed work, and one of the characters still sounded too much like a version of myself.

As I left the house, I looked at my phone and wrote: Let the story be difficult. Let it feel like a door waiting to be opened.

I could still reach out to Ryan and Amilcar. I could still explore a new culture and find something fresh to share with my readers. But I also wanted Saturday to hold enough of its own weight. I wanted the story to have room to breathe, even while I kept writing.
