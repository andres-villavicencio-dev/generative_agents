I woke at ten on a Monday, the same time I’d spent for years, but the room was different now. There was coffee, a small notebook, and the quiet hum of co-living below me. That was enough to make me feel strangely awake.

On my first day as a writer, I had been told that imagination was something I could simply summon. I had thought it was a creative tool, like a paintbrush or a light switch. But lately, I’ve been wondering whether it’s more like a fire. A fire doesn’t just give you light; it can change the shape of the room, the temperature, the people standing around it.

That night, I was writing the opening scene for The Painted Walls. I imagined the artists waking up in a shared apartment, each with a tiny world of unfinished pieces. One of them was Maya, a painter who kept her brushes in a small jar by the window.

I paused, letting the image settle around me. Maya wasn’t just a character; she was a piece of the room. Every brushstroke had a story, every stain a memory, every unfinished canvas a question. When she walked into the kitchen, she carried that silence with her. She didn’t need to say much.

The scene came back to me in my mind, and suddenly the apartment felt like a living thing. The coffee machine hummed, the radiator breathed, and Maya’s presence made every quiet moment feel full.

I wrote down that feeling in the margins: Imagination isn’t just making things up. It’s noticing what’s already there.

I went back to the page. The opening had been so dry, so polite. Now it seemed alive. Perhaps I had been writing too carefully, too politely. Perhaps the real story was in the way the characters noticed each other, in the small details that didn’t need to be explained.

By ten o’clock, I had a new scene, a few rough edges, and a feeling I hadn’t even recognized before. Maybe that’s what makes writing interesting: it’s not about finding a perfect opening. It’s about opening up enough to see what’s already inside you.

I closed my notebook, lit a candle, and went back to bed. I didn’t sleep for long. I was still thinking about the room, about the paintings, about Maya, and about the way a single brushstroke could change the whole room. And by two in the morning, I would be writing again.
