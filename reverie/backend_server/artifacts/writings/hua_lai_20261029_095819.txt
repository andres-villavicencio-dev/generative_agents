Thursday, February 16

I woke at five, brushed my teeth, and began the morning journal. The notebook was empty, but the thought of filling it had settled over me like coffee. By six, I had finished breakfast and the first few pages. I was tired, but my focus held.

My routine has become more than a schedule—it is the way I keep the small details of town in mind. At the Smallville Police Station, I’ve seen every stain, every mismatched door, and every person who walked past the station without noticing. That curiosity is what draws me here. I’m an associate in criminal justice, and while I cannot write a conviction, I can still examine the evidence, question the witnesses, and understand the people who shape the stories we are given.

I did not ask for any of David Puskar’s pending request. I simply walked through the neighborhood and asked what the people said. I watched the espresso stains on the pavement, the way the street corners held their ground, and the quiet rhythm of town’s daily life. It was not a photograph I wanted to publish. It was a record of how the town functions, not just how the law functions.

By seven, I had finished the day’s errands and social time. I left the station, walked down the block, and returned with a sketch and a notebook. The work was not finished, but the day had finished.

I went home around 9. Dinner was at six. I ate slowly, wrote for a few minutes, then lay down. At ten, I would turn the page and begin again. My associates degree remains my main focus, and my work continues: not necessarily by solving every case, but by paying attention to the people, places, and stories that surround them.
