I took one of these routes today, not because I was trying to capture perfect images, but because I wanted to photograph the rhythm of the town as I knew it. The espresso stains on the pavement, the way the streetlamps change color across the blocks, the way people move from one corner to another—these were the details that matter when you’re looking for a story worth framing. I’ve been studying criminal justice through this lens, and it’s been useful. I’ve noticed how a single shift can change an entire day, how evidence often changes the story people tell, and how every person’s life is connected to something they overlook.

At the small station, I keep the usual routine: shift, paperwork, a cup of coffee, and a little time to think. That quiet routine is where I’ve found my best work. Arthur Burton at the pub has been a useful source of humor and conversation, and Carlos Gomez’s loud gatherings near the park have taught me how people react to noise. I’m not trying to understand every person or event perfectly, but I want to notice patterns.

I need to revise the photos before I submit them. I’ll make sure the framing is tight enough to reveal the moment, but loose enough to preserve the surrounding details. I’ll check the lighting, the composition, and whether the details actually support what the photo is saying. At the same time, I need to focus on the material I’m studying: the law, procedure, and the people who enforce it. The town is still learning its stories, and I want to contribute without making them too complicated.

By 10:00, I will be asleep. Tomorrow I want to begin again with the same curiosity.
