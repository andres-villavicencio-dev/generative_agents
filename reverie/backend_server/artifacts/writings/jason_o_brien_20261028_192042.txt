Theology and art have always spoken to one another, not by replacing each other, but by revealing the world as it is, and as it ought to be seen. I began noticing this connection when I first listened to a Bach fugue in church. The music was not merely decorative; it carried the same rhythm of mystery, longing, and hope that the liturgy seemed to carry too. I began to wonder whether classical paintings, too, were not merely beautiful reflections of faith, but spiritual maps.

By classical art, I mean works drawn from the great traditions of antiquity and the Middle Ages: the Gospels, the Psalms, the life of Christ, the Resurrection, and the life of the Church. The figures, colors, gestures, and spaces in such works are not only artistic choices; they are theological statements. The depth of a portrait can suggest the dignity of the soul. The composition of a scene can express sorrow, judgment, or salvation. Even a landscape can become a metaphor for the human condition.

Jason, 64, is a chaplain and counsellor whose life has always been lived in conversation with others. He is compassionate, contemplative, and creative. In his recent work on theology and contemporary visual art, he is examining how classical traditions continue to speak to our deepest questions.

I think the dialogue is not one-way. A painting can inspire a sermon. A poem can illuminate a sacred text. But the sacred text must remain alive, not merely a museum object. A classical painting can help us remember that the human heart is capable of love, humility, and hope. It can also help us recognize our own limitations.

I would encourage us to listen with attention. A figure may be holding a child, a candle, or a book. A color may suggest light, shadow, or sorrow. A gesture may show strength, doubt, or sorrow. We should not treat art as a finished message. It is more valuable when we ask: What is this work saying to the viewer? And what are we being asked to see?

In our new clinic, we will encourage patients to create and listen together. In my weekly listening hour at Johnson Park, Jane Moreno and Klaus Mueller have become my most regular companions. They do not have to agree with me, but their presence helps me see what I might otherwise overlook.

Theology is not a closed system. Art is not merely decoration. And the greatest art is not always the most beautiful. It is art that helps us become more human.
