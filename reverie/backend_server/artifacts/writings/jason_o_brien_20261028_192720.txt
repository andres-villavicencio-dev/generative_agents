The morning light brings the first quiet to Johnson Park. I wake at five, and by six I have already made my tea, sitting in the park with its green trees and silver paths. I hear the usual voices—people talking, birds singing, the distant rhythm of a symphony—though I have not heard the whole song yet.

This is what I want this book to be. Not a neat argument between religion and art, nor a simple statement that both are equally important. The dialogue is complicated. It is shaped by history, by the way we speak of the sacred, by the images we choose to make, and by the people who listen. I do not believe that painting can replace faith, and neither can theology erase the beauty of a landscape. What I hope to show is how they can speak to one another.

I have spent years working with people in pain, in grief, and in the difficult moments between faith and doubt. Through my chaplaincy and counseling, I have learned that compassion is not enough. It must be accompanied by honesty, by patience, and by an attention to the ordinary moments that matter. A cup of tea can be more than warmth; it can be a space in which we sit with our questions.

That is why I have been writing about the dialogue between theology and contemporary visual art. I want to explore how a painting can illuminate a mystery, and how a theological question can give a form to a visual idea. Watercolor, I believe, may help us see the sacred without trying to impose it. A classical piano can move us through time, creating a space where memory and present moment can meet. These are not merely decorative elements. They can shape the way we listen to the divine.

As I prepare for the regional interfaith arts symposium and for my weekly listening hour, I hope the participants will leave not with a single answer, but with a more attentive way of listening. I hope that the people who gather will recognize that theology and art are not separate spheres. They are ways of seeing the world differently, more fully.

I will begin with a painting, perhaps one I have not discussed before. I will begin with a question. I will begin with the possibility that a story can be told without saying everything, and that a question can be answered without ending it.
