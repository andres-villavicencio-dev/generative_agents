My name is Jason O’Brien. I am sixty-four years old, and I wake at five in the morning, eat dinner at six, and go to bed around ten. For forty years, I have been a chaplain and counsellor at the new clinic and at Johnson Park, listening to people who often do not know how to speak the language of faith.

Today is February 13. I am also writing a book about the dialogue between theology and contemporary visual art. The idea has been with me since I first listened to a contemporary artist and then asked, “How does that image change how we hear about God?”

Art does not replace theology. It often reveals what theology has been too abstract to say. A watercolor may show a landscape that feels as though it is breathing. A classical piano may carry the weight of history. A painting of a wedding can make love feel more than a social event. A painting of a city can make the ordinary feel sacred.

I hope my book will not be a lecture. It should be a conversation. It should invite the reader to look closely, to notice the textures, the light, the silence, and the spaces between the lines.

At Johnson Park, Jane Moreno and Klaus Mueller have each asked me for advice. They want to hear how art can support the vulnerable and help people find meaning. I hope they will also feel supported.

I will be hosting a regional interfaith arts symposium next month. I would welcome artists, theologians, musicians, and people who care about peace. I hope the event will not be a performance, but a gathering where different voices can listen to one another.

My life is full of small rhythms: five o’clock, six o’clock, ten o’clock. These moments shape how I hear people. I am still learning how to listen.

I write this not as a finished product, but as a beginning. The work has changed the more important part of me: how I hear.
