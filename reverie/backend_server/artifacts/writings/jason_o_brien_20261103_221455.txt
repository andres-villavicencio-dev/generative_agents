The first chapter of Theological Art begins with a single image: a 17th-century Dutch still life of fruit, rendered with meticulous realism, arranged upon an altar. To many, the work is simply decorative. To me, it suggests something deeper. The careful ordering of the fruit is not merely about aesthetics; it is about attention, about the act of seeing. In a world that often demands we hurry through the divine, the image asks us to pause and listen.

This is where theology and contemporary visual art can meet. They do not always arrive at the same conclusions. Faith may offer questions of meaning, suffering, and the sacred, while art can respond to the human condition with honesty, ambiguity, and imagination. Yet both disciplines share a concern with what we cannot fully name. Theology speaks through the stories of faith and the lives of saints. Art speaks through the colors, shapes, and spaces in which those stories are rendered. When the two speak together, they do not need to be identical. They can speak to each other in different languages, yet still discover a shared ground.

The origins of visual art are therefore not merely artistic. They are deeply human. As soon as the first painter chose to record the world, the world was being recorded. As soon as the first church chose to make space for the divine, the divine was being made visible. Faith did not create the first artwork by accident. It began with a desire to understand the mystery of being, using every medium available to make the invisible perceptible.

In our contemporary moment, that desire has never been stronger. Images of suffering, of hope, of migration, of loss, and of joy enter the churches and galleries with urgent voices. They carry no single answer, but they carry many questions. The task of the chaplain and the artist is not to resolve them. The task is to listen, to witness, and to help the world see that the sacred is not confined to one particular religion, one particular building, or one particular moment in history.

The symposium I am planning, in Johnson Park and beyond, is meant to show us how art can become an act of faith. It does not demand that every painting or poem be a theology. It asks us to recognize the possibility that art may reveal what words alone cannot name. And it asks us to recognize that faith, in turn, may give art a depth it would otherwise lack.

We must not reduce art to a sermon, nor theology to a museum of holy pictures. Their relationship is not one of hierarchy. It is one of conversation. The dialogue between theology and contemporary visual art is, at its best, a conversation among the human heart.
