The third chapter is called The Shape of Light: how a painting can carry a prayer and how a prayer can become a picture.

At first sight, theology and visual art seem to speak different languages. The Bible speaks of creation as a word spoken into being; the painter speaks through brush and pigment. One is a story, the other a form. But I have come to believe that the difference is not in the medium, but in the direction of the gaze. Theology looks inward, toward the meaning that makes the world bearable; art looks outward, toward what the world reveals without explanation.

This is why I have long been attracted to contemporary visual art. Its subjects are no longer confined to the holy or the spectacular. They contain the ordinary—people waiting for permission, a child learning to stand, the quiet dignity of a hand making a mark. And they often do the same thing theology has done for centuries: they suggest that the unseen can become the visible.

In the past year, I have painted the new clinic at Johnson Park and listened to the piano in the park before the first congregation gathered. The same watercolor has appeared in two different places, each one altered by time and circumstance. I would argue that the painting is not merely the sum of its parts, but a space where meaning can happen. A painting has no author, yet it can be made by someone who feels deeply about the world. And art has no fixed meaning, yet it can still speak when it touches the eyes of someone who is listening.

The relationship is not always harmonious. Sometimes a religious image feels too neat, too reassuring, too ready to be understood. Sometimes a modern painting feels too cool, too abstract, too detached from the human experience. The challenge is to make both visible without letting either silence the other. A good artwork should invite the sacred into the secular and the secular into the sacred. It should show that the human condition is neither entirely holy nor entirely secular, but a place where both can enter.

My aim is not to create a bridge between faith and art. I want to create space between them. To give the listener a moment to wonder, to feel, and to make a decision that does not have to be immediately religious. That is what I hope the symposium and the clinic can become: a place where contemplation is treated as art, and art is treated as conversation.
