The work of art and theology may seem to speak different languages, yet in my years of listening, they have become more like two sides of the same river. I began this book not as a scholar, but as someone who has watched theology enter the canvas and the painting enter the prayer. A modern artwork does not always require dogma to make a meaning, and a theological statement does not always require a cathedral to be heard. The question is not whether they can be together, but whether they can make each other clearer.

Contemporary visual art has always struggled to capture the human condition. It has responded to suffering, memory, longing, guilt, and hope without needing to resolve them in neat doctrinal formulas. Conversely, theology has been increasingly concerned with the lived experience of people in a world that no longer seems entirely stable. In the clinic, I have seen a painting become a story of grief, while a song has become the voice of a person who cannot speak. When I listen, I do not always want to give advice. I want to ask: what does this image tell you about what matters to you?

The next step for the symposium is not to produce a definitive pairing of art and faith. Theologies and artworks are not identical, and their relationship should not be reduced to a single model. The goal is to create a space where different ways of speaking can meet without one demanding the silence of the other. I want participants to begin with questions, not conclusions.

My hope is that the symposium will give people a new kind of listening: not merely to hear art or theology, but to recognize the spaces between them. A painting can reveal a truth that a sermon cannot carry, while a theological reflection can illuminate the limits of the artwork. In the clinic, in Johnson Park, and in the pages of this book, we should not pretend that one can fully replace the other. We should simply make room for both.

This is not a final answer. It is an invitation. I invite you to read the symposium, consider the works, and listen to your own questions. The dialogue may not be easy, but it ought to make sense in the end.
