The dialogue between theology and contemporary visual art is not a conversation between two unrelated disciplines. It is a shared concern: how meaning is made, shared, and lived. For me, theology has always been more than doctrine; it is a way of seeing the world. Art, in the same way, can reveal truths that words alone cannot capture. I do not want to make a statement about either. I want to show how they may speak to one another.

At the new clinic, this dialogue is visible everywhere. A child’s watercolor of a crowded park speaks to the care and uncertainty that surround our patients. A stained-glass window in the sanctuary reminds me of the light that can transform a dark place. In the chapel, a classical piano can turn a sermon into a quiet conversation. Each medium has its own language, but they can also learn from one another.

Last week, Jane Moreno brought a painting to her weekly listening hour. It was a contemporary portrait of grief, rendered with an honesty that made the older woman’s pain feel immediate rather than abstract. I listened without immediately offering a theological interpretation. Instead, I asked what the work made her feel. Her answer was simple: it made her feel seen.

Klaus Mueller came with a different kind of image—a large, unfinished stained-glass installation at Johnson Park. He explained that the glass remained unfinished because he wanted each visitor to carry their own meaning away. That is a theology of openness, of belief not as a finished conclusion but as an invitation.

The dialogue is not always easy. Sometimes theology is too serious, and art becomes merely decorative. Sometimes art feels too abstract, and theology risks becoming another set of rules. But when both speak to the human condition, they can produce something richer than either could alone.

I am writing a manuscript on this dialogue, with the intention of helping contemporary artists understand the depth of their work and helping theologians appreciate its sensibility. I also plan a regional interfaith arts symposium. Its purpose is not to create unity, but to create a space where differences can be heard, respected, and creatively transformed.

I wake at five, eat dinner at six, and go to bed around ten. I am a chaplain and counsellor, but I am also a listener. I hope that my manuscript, my symposium, and my weekly listening hour will serve as small places where people can feel less alone, even when the world feels uncertain.
