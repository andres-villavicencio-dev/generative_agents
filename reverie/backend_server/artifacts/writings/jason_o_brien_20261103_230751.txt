The manuscript I have begun today is not merely a collection of artworks. It is an attempt to understand how a painting can speak the language of a theological question, and how a theological question can shape the image of a painting. The dialogue between theology and contemporary visual art should not be treated as a neat reconciliation of ideas. It should be a conversation that remains unsettled, sometimes painful, yet constantly fertile.

The first chapter, therefore, is less about defining a relationship between faith and art than about describing the conditions in which they meet. I begin with a chapel, then with a stained-glass window, then with a contemporary photograph. The images are not arranged as examples of “artistic merit.” They are participants in a conversation that may begin with a question: What does this sacred image reveal about the world it depicts? What does a contemporary image conceal or illuminate about the sacred?

The second chapter will explore the possibility of theological depth without the protection of academic certainty. I want to show that theology can engage with images that challenge its assumptions, that art can reveal ambiguities theology has tried to smooth over. A watercolor may hold a landscape that refuses to be simplified, and a contemporary photograph may show a place that does not fit neatly into a doctrine. These differences are not errors to be corrected. They are invitations to listen more closely.

The third chapter will consider the role of the image in everyday life. It will move beyond the concert hall or the symposium and examine paintings, photographs, installations, and digital media as ways ordinary people encounter meaning. In the final section, I want to ask whether a theology that engages art can remain faithful to its most difficult questions. I hope the answer is not that art makes theology easy, but that art can make theology vulnerable.

I am writing this because I believe that visual art and theology belong together. The chapel at Johnson Park is no longer a place where only prayers are heard. The watercolor on my table is no longer simply a piece of art. And the piano keys, once reserved for private reflection, are beginning to speak to a wider world.

My weekly listening hour at Johnson Park continues this work. Jane Moreno and Klaus Mueller have asked for my advice. They are not interested in theological answers. They are interested in the images, questions, and meanings that surround them. Their curiosity is the most sacred gift I have. I will listen to them, and to the art they bring. I will offer no comfort without honesty. I will offer no comfort without honesty. I will offer no comfort without honesty.
