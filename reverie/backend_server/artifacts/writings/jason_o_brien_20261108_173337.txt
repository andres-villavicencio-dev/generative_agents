I began this book by reading contemporary art as if it spoke a language I had not yet learned to translate. I wanted to see how painters, poets, and sculptors were responding to religious truths, without imposing a single theological framework. At the same time, I was concerned that the dialogue could become merely decorative—beautiful images with little more to say than “God is good.”

I learned that art rarely tells us what theology must mean. Instead, it often points toward the questions that theology is meant to answer. A watercolor can suggest the fragility of human love; a stained-glass window can illuminate the mystery of mercy; a musical phrase can make the invisible visible. The artist and the theologian do not have to agree on every detail. They must agree on the question: what are we being called to notice?

My notes on contemporary art have become clearer after the editing process. The original version was full of striking observations, but it often sounded like a sermon disguised as critique. The revised version separates observation from interpretation, allowing the artwork to stand without being explained away. The theological questions become more intimate: what does the artwork make us feel, doubt, hope, or complicit?

I intend to use this book not only as a scholarly inquiry but as a companion for those seeking to understand the world through a different lens. Contemporary art can make the sacred feel human, and human experience can make the sacred feel real. That is the purpose of my writing: to create a space where art and faith may speak without one demanding the other’s surrender.

I hope the book will encourage readers to listen more closely—not only to the words of the church, but also to the colors, sounds, and images that surround them. Theology, I believe, is not a closed system. It is an ongoing conversation with the world, with art, with suffering, and with the possibility of mercy.

I am also planning a regional interfaith arts symposium in the spring. My hope is that the workshop will not turn theology into a lecture, nor will art become a mere backdrop. It should be a shared act of care, where different traditions encounter one another not by comparing their conclusions, but by listening to each other’s stories.

Finally, I want the book to belong to whoever reads it, not only to those whose faith has been examined by criticism. I hope that through its pages, we may discover that art and theology can teach us one thing: patience.
