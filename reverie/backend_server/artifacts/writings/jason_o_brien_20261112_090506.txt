The Dialogue Between Theology and Visual Art

Art is not merely decoration for a sacred space. It is a way of listening, of seeing, and of imagining what faith can feel like beyond the language of doctrine.

In the past, I thought art belonged to the church: stained glass, statues, paintings, liturgical music. I wanted it to serve the liturgy. But I have learned that art can also transform the church. It can make the abstract feel tangible, the distant familiar, and the sacred feel accessible.

Consider the watercolor landscape of a village at twilight. The shadows lengthen, the village gathers, and the sky becomes a mirror of something larger. A viewer may notice the same landscape without noticing it is a theological image. The artwork invites attention, without demanding that we believe.

The dialogue between theology and art is not about replacing one with the other. It is about opening the other to what the first cannot fully say. A gospel story may not capture the beauty of an olive grove, and a painting may not explain the meaning of resurrection. But together, they can suggest a way of being that neither alone can provide.

This is why I have been thinking about contemporary art and interfaith understanding. The world is becoming increasingly pluralistic. Theologies may differ, but human experience is shared. Visual art has a special capacity to bridge differences: it can express the sacred without requiring agreement, it can honor tradition without demanding conversion, and it can reveal common ground in ways words sometimes fail.

At Johnson Park, I have been asked to listen. Jane Moreno and Klaus Mueller have both shared stories of faith. Their conversations remind me that art does not always need to resolve conflict. Sometimes it can simply create a space where both parties can hear each other.

I plan to write a book about this dialogue, and I am organizing a regional interfaith arts symposium. We will bring together theologians, artists, musicians, and people of many faiths. The goal is not to make all our differences disappear. The goal is to make each difference visible, understandable, and respectful.

Theology and art may never become one. But they can become partners. Through them, we may learn to see the world more clearly, to care for one another more deeply, and to imagine a future in which faith and culture flourish rather than divide.
