At Johnson Park, the small table has gathered three people who share something I often observe: a deep desire to understand the meaning of their lives through images. Jane Moreno arrives with a sketchbook filled with birds and rivers, her questions about faith and creativity often sounding like poetry. Klaus Mueller brings a photograph of a crowded street, while Jason listens with his hands folded and his eyes attentive.

This is exactly the kind of conversation I have been exploring lately. For years, theology has been understood as a set of answers, while art has been treated as decoration. I have come to believe that neither is complete without the other. Art can make theology feel lived, while theology can give art a deeper purpose.

Consider the stained-glass windows of old cathedrals. Their light changes with the seasons, and the colors shift as the sun moves across the sky. The light is not merely a backdrop; it transforms the story the window tells. In the same way, contemporary artists do not simply illustrate religious ideas. They make them visible, letting the viewer experience them through light, texture, sound, and emotion.

I found a contemporary piece that changed everything about my understanding of this relationship. A young painter created an installation of ordinary household objects—vases, books, chairs, and photographs—arranged like a sacred space. Each object carried a personal history. When the lights were dimmed and a soft chime played, the room felt like a temple, yet it was made of everyday things. The artwork did not teach me about faith directly. It showed me that the divine can be present in the mundane, and that human experience can become holy.

In the weeks since, I have returned to the park with this idea in mind. I would like to invite people to look closely at each other’s art and listen carefully to the words beneath the images. What images make us feel seen? What questions do they quietly ask?

I am also developing a regional interfaith arts symposium for the next few months. I hope it will offer a space where faith and creativity can speak without needing to explain themselves. For many, art is a language that has yet to be translated into words. For many, faith is a language that has yet to be translated into images. Together, they may finally speak the language of belonging.
