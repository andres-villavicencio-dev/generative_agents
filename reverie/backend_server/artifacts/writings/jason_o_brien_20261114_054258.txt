I wrote this book because I have always been drawn to one simple question: What does faith say about what we see today? I began with my own practice—listening to the stories of people around me, helping them find meaning, and letting the arts speak when language felt insufficient. I wanted to explore that tension without turning theology into a lecture or art into decoration. The dialogue should emerge naturally, not be imposed.

In the first chapter I tried to show that theology and contemporary art are not two separate conversations. They meet in the spaces where people gather, where art and faith influence one another, and where the human experience of hope, loss, and belonging can be expressed through painting, music, and performance. I did not intend to establish a hierarchy. Instead, I wanted to create a shared language: one in which a stained-glass window can make the divine visible, a watercolor can capture the fragility of grief, and a pianist can reveal the rhythm of community.

The central argument is that contemporary art often asks theology to examine how we imagine God, and theology offers art an occasion to ask what matters to people in particular cultures and times. I hope readers will not leave this book thinking the arts are merely illustrations of faith, or that theology is a rigid code for artistic expression. Instead, I hope they will see them as collaborators in a shared human project: finding meaning in the world while remembering that the meaning of the world may itself be unknowable.

I am also beginning a regional interfaith arts symposium to make this exploration accessible to people who do not share a particular tradition. The symposium will not ask each participant to choose a “correct” message. Its purpose is to listen, to show, and to create. I hope the symposium will end with more than a few beautiful photographs or a memorable meal. I hope it will end with people who have felt heard, understood, and connected.

For now, my schedule is simple: five hours of listening at Johnson Park, a few hours of watercolor, and two hours of piano. I am also writing the first chapter of my book. The chapter should carry me through a day that is not always easy, but is always meaningful.
