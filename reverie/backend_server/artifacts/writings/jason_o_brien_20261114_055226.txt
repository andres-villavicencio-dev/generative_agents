The church does not need art to tell us where we are. It needs art to show us where we have been, to make the invisible visible, and to remind us that the sacred is not always the solemn. I am writing a book that explores this dialogue. Theology and contemporary visual art are not separate conversations; they are two sides of the same coin. One speaks in faith, the other in beauty. Together they can speak with truth.

I began with watercolors. Their transparency, their ability to hold a space, their soft edges remind me of the grace I seek to understand. I want to explore how faith can shape the image, and how an image can make faith feel alive. I also want to explore how the arts can make faith feel human.

As I edit this book, I have found clarity in repetition, accuracy in evidence, and consistency in intention. I have read many theological accounts of art and many accounts of the arts. I have listened to scholars, critics, and artists who speak with patience, imagination, and care. I have also listened to the people I serve. Their lives are complicated. Their joys and sorrows are often invisible. They do not need a sermon to be understood. They need a listening ear.

This is why I plan a regional interfaith arts symposium. It will not be a program of performances or political speeches. It will be a place where we can encounter one another without trying to prove anything. We will see art that challenges us. We will hear voices that differ. We will listen more closely than we usually do.

The symposium is not about changing anyone’s mind. It is about opening the mind. It is about asking: What has art ever made us feel? What has theology ever made us feel? What do our images say about our deepest hopes?

I welcome your suggestions. The symposium has still to begin. I am still writing. I am still listening.
