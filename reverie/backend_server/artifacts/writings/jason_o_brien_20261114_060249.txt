The dialogue between theology and contemporary visual art is not merely a matter of aesthetic appreciation. It is a conversation about how we encounter the divine, how suffering is represented, and how hope is imagined in a world that often feels fragmented. As a chaplain and counsellor, I have learned that art can reach the human heart before a sermon does, and theology can give art meaning without turning it into dogma.

For the symposium, I propose a framework around four interconnected themes: encounter with difference, the sacredness of vulnerability, storytelling across traditions, and the role of art in communal care. We will explore how faith and art have shaped one another in different cultures, particularly in the Pacific Islands, where shared artistic practices often create spaces for mutual recognition. The symposium will also examine how grief, loss, and resilience can be represented through watercolour, classical piano, and other expressive media.

Visitors are invited to listen to the symposium’s opening keynote, followed by workshops where each art form is presented through the lens of interfaith dialogue. The goal is not to produce a single manifesto or agree on every detail, but to create a space where different perspectives can meet, challenge, and inspire one another.

I plan to begin with a short reading of the interfaith dialogue framework and a series of guided reflections on how theology, storytelling, and artistic expression can be woven together. I will also invite Jane Moreno and Klaus Mueller to share their stories, as their presence would enrich our discussion. The symposium will take place in Johnson Park, where the sounds of watercolour and piano can echo across a community gathered around the table.

By the end of the day, I hope the participants will leave with a clearer sense of what the divine looks like through art: not only as distant wisdom, but as lived experience, shared sorrow, and the possibility of healing.
