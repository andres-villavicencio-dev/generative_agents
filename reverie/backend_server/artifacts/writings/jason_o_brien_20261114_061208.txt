For nearly thirty years, I have looked at paintings, liturgies, and prayers and asked the same question: how can these seemingly separate forms carry one spirit? Contemporary art is often misunderstood as purely individual or commercial, but I have seen it speak to the sacred in unexpected ways. A watercolor may begin as a simple landscape and end as a meditation on grief; a classical piano piece can become a vessel for communal hope. The artwork itself is not the message—it is the medium through which the message becomes visible.

Spiritual meaning does not require traditional symbols. It can emerge from the way an image creates space, from the rhythm of a sound, from the color of a landscape, from the presence of another person. When a community gathers around a painting, a sculpture, or a shared performance, that gathering transforms a solitary experience into something communal. The shared space becomes a place where difference is not hidden but embraced. This is the heart of what I am writing about: theology and the arts can speak to one another without forcing agreement. One does not always need to be the same as the other to be relevant.

The same principle should guide the regional interfaith arts symposium we are planning. We will invite artists and practitioners from different traditions to share not only their work but also the questions it raises. We will not try to convert anyone into a member of a particular faith. Instead, we will explore how watercolors, classical music, literature, dance, and spoken words can bear witness to the dignity of the human experience. We will listen as loudly as we create, and we will ask that our creative expressions be held together in compassion rather than divided by labels.

The symposium will also include a time for reflection. A painting may change our mind; a song may remind us to listen. A prayer may reveal what art has left unsaid. These are all gifts. My goal is not to persuade the world to adopt a single theology or a single aesthetic. My goal is to help people see art as capable of reaching beyond their own comfort zones and bringing the sacred into the ordinary.

I have listened to many voices over the years, and sometimes they have sounded different. Jane Moreno and Klaus Mueller have helped me understand that diversity does not have to mean disagreement. The symposium will begin with that understanding. It will move toward a shared space where different paths can meet. It will end with the possibility that each person may carry a little light back into the world.
