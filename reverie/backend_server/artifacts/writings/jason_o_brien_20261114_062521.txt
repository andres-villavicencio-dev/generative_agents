The first panel of the regional interfaith arts symposium was called “Theology, Art, and Community.” It opened not with a sermon, but with a watercolor painted in muted ochre and blue, showing a young woman standing at the edge of a village. Beneath her, the landscape was not entirely finished—there were gaps, shadows, and perhaps a few loose stones. I thought of the clinic I was helping to build, where a young woman once came crying after her father had taken her away. The painting had no answer for her, but it did offer a space to begin.

I began by asking each speaker to describe a work of art that has changed how they understand a community, and a moment in their life when the art had made a difference. A young man spoke of a mural in his neighborhood, painted by a former minister. “It is honest,” he said. “We all have wounds, and we all have hope. The artist did not smooth them over. He showed them.”

Another speaker spoke of a classical piano piece that helped a refugee find a voice. “Music does not always have words,” he said. “Sometimes it is enough to know that you are heard.”

These stories did not need to be theological. They needed to be human.

I began the discussion by asking a simple question: What would a community look like if it could see each other as artists rather than merely neighbors? The room was quiet for a moment. Then Jane Moreno asked, “What would that art look like?”

I asked her to describe a painting she had seen recently that had made her feel included. She spoke of a street artist who painted a large portrait of a woman who had been forgotten in a crowded city. “She did not ask for permission to belong,” Jane said. “She simply said, ‘I am here, and I am beautiful.’”

The watercolor in the panel seemed to echo that image. It was not perfect. The edges were soft, the colors were restrained, and the young woman at the edge looked unsure. But she was not alone.

I asked the participants to consider the difference between depicting a community and making it visible. The first speaker said, “We can paint the whole village, but we can only hear the people who walk through the doorway at night.” The second said, “Art can make silence speak.”

We ended by asking each person to imagine one small act of kindness, one piece of art, or one shared meal that could bring the community together.

The room was filled with the sound of watercolor brushes, piano keys, and the quiet hum of many different voices. I did not want the panel to resolve any theological question. I wanted it to remind us that theology is not the only way to understand community. It is the language, and the art is the voice.

When I left, the young woman at the edge of the painting looked up. She had not answered the question in advance. Now, with the light of the panel behind her, she seemed to know the answer.
