My name is Jason O’Brien, and I am a chaplain and counsellor with a quiet belief: art can speak more truthfully than words. I have spent years listening to the same people—children, families, and strangers—who carry stories that make faith and creativity feel more like conversations than disciplines. My new book, Theology and Contemporary Visual Art, is an attempt to translate that conversation into language that ordinary people can understand and use.

At Johnson Park, I host a weekly listening hour, where people gather without preparation and simply listen. Jane Moreno sits there with me once a week, asking how I can help her find peace. I have never answered every question, but I have learned that patience is often the answer. Klaus Mueller, another regular, brings a different kind of question: he asks how art can be a space where differences are not merely tolerated but understood. He has found comfort in the space I have created.

This week, I am preparing for the regional interfaith arts symposium. It is not a competition, and the names of the speakers and artists are not fixed. Instead, I am selecting voices and creators from the community—people who have lived through difficult times, who have been touched by wonder, and who have learned to speak across differences. I have already spoken with Jane, Klaus, and a few others, and I am hearing a shared need: to create something that does not pretend to unify everyone, but honors each person’s truth.

Perhaps the most important part of the symposium is not the art itself. It is the people who will share it. I want a speaker who can make theology vivid without reducing it to slogans. I want an artist who can make the sacred feel human. I want a community member who can explain what art means to them. And I want another person to listen, not to convince them, but to hear.

I will invite them to work together, not to produce a single masterpiece, but to explore how faith, culture, and creative expression can coexist. We will choose a theme, invite a speaker, and let the artists speak for themselves. The goal is not perfection. It is the possibility that art can help us see that we are part of the same story.

Tonight, before bed at ten, I will write a short prayer for the symposium. I will not ask for agreement. I will simply ask for presence. I will ask the people I love to listen. And I will ask every artist to trust their own voice.

The next day, I will meet with Jane and Klaus at Johnson Park. We may work with watercolors, piano, or a shared reading. Perhaps we will simply sit together and listen. That is enough.
