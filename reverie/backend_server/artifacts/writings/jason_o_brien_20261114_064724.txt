I am writing a book about the dialogue between theology and contemporary visual art, while also planning a regional interfaith arts symposium. My life has made me curious, compassionate, and occasionally impatient. I wake at five, eat dinner at six, and go to bed around ten. I have a full day to carry my compassion into the new clinic, help others find meaning, and listen patiently to people who may be lost.

The symposium will be held at Johnson Park, with a regional arts workshop on February 19. I am designing the agenda around a shared creative space where theology and art can converse without one having to win the argument. The workshop will feature watercolor painting, classical piano, and contemporary visual art. I will provide the framework: time, logistics, and the possibility of conflict.

The schedule is tight. By Friday, I have the opening remarks, the keynote, and several workshop sessions. I need to leave enough room for participants to work quietly, for breaks, and for an open question-and-answer period. I will also need to consider the needs of the clinic, Johnson Park’s residents, and the local faith leaders who will be attending.

Potential conflicts are already apparent. The keynote will begin at 9:30, while the workshop sessions are scheduled for 10:00–12:00 and 1:00–3:00. A watercolor demonstration will occupy the second block, and the piano recitation will follow at 3:00. The open question-and-answer period will begin at 4:00. There is also a possibility that the regional leaders will speak before the keynote, and I may need to adjust the opening to accommodate them.

I would not want the symposium to become a debate. I want people to see the beauty in shared struggle and the possibility of creative compassion. The workshop will begin with a short watercolor demonstration, followed by a brief prayer and a moment of silence. The piano recitation should be treated as an invitation rather than a performance. Afterward, participants may speak freely about what they witnessed.

The agenda must be flexible enough to preserve the symposium’s purpose while remaining practical enough to work. I will send the revised schedule to the workshop leaders and regional partners by Tuesday. I will also ask Jane Moreno and Klaus Mueller to confirm their availability and any practical needs they may have.

I believe that art can speak when words are difficult. The symposium should not simply repeat theological themes in art. It should invite art to help theology find its voice again. I will write clearly, listen carefully, and hope that by the end of the week, everyone will have heard something that made them feel less alone.
