The morning light filtered through the tall windows of the Oak Hill College library, casting long, golden shadows across the rows of worn books. I adjusted my glasses, the metal frames cool against my nose, and opened the notebook I had brought with me. It was 7:15 AM, the quiet hour before the campus really woke up, and I was eager to dive into the first chapter of what felt like a necessary conversation.

My fingers hovered over the keyboard for a moment, then began to type. I was studying sociology, yes, but this wasn't just an academic exercise for me. It was personal. I was interested in the mechanics of displacement, certainly, but more importantly, I was interested in the human cost. The way a neighborhood changes from a place where neighbors know each other’s names to a series of glass-fronted coffee shops and polished facades.

I typed furiously for a while, capturing the theoretical framework I had been wrestling with all week. The concept of "symbolic annihilation"—how the presence of certain people is erased from the narrative of a neighborhood’s history to make way for new development—felt like a crucial lens. But as the morning wore on and the sun climbed higher, I shifted from note-taking to actual drafting.

I wrote about the specific communities I had been reading about. How rising property taxes force long-term residents, often those who have lived in a place for decades, out of their own homes. I thought about the local diner that used to be the heart of the block, now replaced by a boutique that sells $8 avocado toast. It wasn't just about economics; it was about the erosion of social fabric.

By 10 AM, I had filled three pages. I closed the laptop, feeling a mix of exhaustion and satisfaction. I packed my bag, the weight of the research feeling heavy but meaningful. I knew I had to visit Hobbs Cafe eventually—for the cheap coffee and the familiar atmosphere—but for now, the library was my sanctuary. I packed up, locked the screen, and headed out into the crisp February air, ready to continue the fight for social justice, one paragraph at a time.
