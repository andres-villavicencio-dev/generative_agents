The morning light filtered through the tall windows of the Oak Hill College library, casting long, thin shadows across the polished oak tables. I pulled my coat tighter, the chill of February still lingering in the air despite the sun’s attempt to warm the room. My bag felt heavier than usual—stacks of printed articles and a notebook filled with scrawled thoughts—but the familiar hum of the library always centers me.

I found my usual spot near the back, a quiet corner where the history section met the sociology wing. I spread out my materials: a worn copy of The Sociological Imagination, a highlighter, and a cup of cold coffee that I had forgotten to finish at home. I took a sip now, the bitter taste grounding me in the present moment.

I began with the introduction I had drafted last night, staring at the blinking cursor of my laptop screen. The topic has been gnawing at me all week—the way neighborhoods transform, the faces of long-time residents replaced by new storefronts and higher rents. As a sociology major, I know the textbooks define gentrification as the process of middle-class or affluent people moving into lower-income areas. But the books don’t quite capture the silence that falls over a corner store when the regulars stop coming, or the way a community garden gets paved over for a luxury condo.

I flipped open a journal article, my brow furrowing as I traced the arguments with my finger. I love how passionate I feel about this. It’s not just academic; it’s personal. I think about the families I’ve met through volunteer work, the resilience they show in the face of displacement. I want my paper to do justice to their stories, to move beyond the statistics and look at the human cost.

A bell chimed somewhere in the distance, signaling the start of the morning rush. I closed my laptop for a moment, watching a stream of students flow in, coffee in hand, heads bent over textbooks. For a brief second, I felt a pang of guilt for being here, studying a problem that feels so much larger than myself. But then I reminded myself why I’m here. I’m inquisitive, yes, but I’m also passionate about finding answers. If I can add even a small piece to the conversation about housing and equity, then these early mornings are worth it.

I opened my notebook again, the pen hovering over the page. The cursor on the screen blinked a slow, rhythmic beat. I took a deep breath, inhaled the scent of old paper and dust, and began to type. The words came slower than I wanted, but they felt true. I wrote about the tension between progress and preservation, about the need for policies that protect existing residents. I paused to stretch my arms above my head, my joints popping softly in the quiet room. It was 8:15 AM. I had several hours yet before I would walk to Hobbs Cafe for my lunch of a turkey sandwich and a bowl of soup. For now, I was content to let the ideas flow, knowing that every sentence I wrote was a step toward understanding a complex, painful, and deeply human issue.
