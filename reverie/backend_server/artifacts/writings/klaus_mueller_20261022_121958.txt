Gentrification is often described as the arrival of wealthier residents into a lower-income neighborhood, but its effects are more complicated than a simple increase in property values or a change in who lives there. While researching this topic at the Oak Hill College library, I have found that gentrification can bring new investment, better services, and expanded employment opportunities. However, these benefits frequently come at the cost of displacement, rising rents, and the loss of community identity. I am particularly interested in how long-term residents experience these changes, since public discussions often focus on neighborhood improvement while overlooking the people who can no longer afford to remain.

In evaluating the sources I have consulted, credibility is essential. Academic journal articles, government reports, and books published by reputable universities or research organizations provide the strongest evidence because their claims are generally supported by research methods, citations, and peer review. For example, urban planning studies that compare neighborhood changes over time offer detailed demographic and housing data, making their findings both reliable and relevant to my argument. Local government and census data are also useful because they provide consistent information about income, rent, and population shifts.

At the same time, every source has limitations. Census data can identify broad trends, but it may not capture the experiences of renters who move frequently or the emotional effects of displacement. Interviews with residents are more personal and revealing, yet they often represent only a small number of people. News articles can make gentrification visible by highlighting individual stories, but they may rely on incomplete information or emphasize dramatic events for attention. Even strong academic studies may focus on particular cities and therefore not apply equally to every community.

My research suggests that gentrification should not be judged only by whether a neighborhood becomes more prosperous. The central question is whether its existing residents share in that prosperity and retain the right to live there. A fair assessment must consider both new opportunities and unequal burdens. Rather than treating gentrification as entirely positive or entirely harmful, I want to examine it as a process shaped by policy, investment, and power—one that can improve a neighborhood while still making it unaffordable for the people who built its identity.
