On Monday, February 13, I arrived at Oak Hill College early and headed to the library. The quiet afternoon felt like a good place to begin a paper I hadn’t finished yet. I had always been drawn to gentrification: a familiar social problem, but one that rarely explained itself in a straight line. It had become obvious to me that the changes we see in low-income neighborhoods are rarely about newcomers bringing culture. They are also about money, displacement, and who is allowed to live there.

My research has been shaped by several sources. Academic articles from sociology and urban studies, local news reports, and interviews with longtime residents have helped me understand the mechanisms of displacement. At the same time, I have spoken with people who were not part of the research process. Their accounts have been essential: they showed how decisions were made, what the community felt, and what people were asked to leave behind.

The findings are difficult to ignore. I found that rising rents and changing commercial patterns can increase property values, but they often come at a high price. Homeowners who bought in earlier generations and whose neighborhoods had changed slowly were displaced first. Their homes were purchased, renovated, and sold to developers who were willing to pay more, while residents who could not afford the new market were priced out. The displacement is rarely isolated to individual transactions; it is embedded in zoning, housing policies, and investment decisions.

I have also learned that gentrification is not a single event. It is a process of transformation, with different phases and participants. A new restaurant, a renovated building, or a housing development can change a neighborhood, but the people affected by those changes may not recognize the process as gentrification. This makes the impact especially difficult to measure. Some changes benefit the neighborhood; others harm it. A community’s ability to preserve its character depends on how well it can adapt, negotiate, and protect its residents while still benefiting from improved investment.

The research is still incomplete. I have been following a few neighborhoods closely, and the changes are continuing even though my paper is nearly finished. I hope that this paper will not only document what has happened but also offer a clearer path for people who are still fighting to keep their neighborhoods and communities from disappearing.
