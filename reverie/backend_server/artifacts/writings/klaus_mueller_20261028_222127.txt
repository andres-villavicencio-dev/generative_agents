I write about gentrification because I believe it is not merely a change of address, but a transformation of the relationships people live through. My research focuses on Oak Hill College, where several families have moved into the neighborhood. What I have noticed is that the physical changes are real: new storefronts, renovated streets, and slightly higher rents have arrived alongside quiet changes in who lives there and who they know.

Community cohesion has not disappeared, but it has become uneven. The older residents who once supported one another through shared routines are increasingly displaced. At the same time, newcomers bring new energy and demand that the neighborhood be improved. This creates a tension: the community still has many people, yet it no longer feels like one community. Trust between longtime residents and newcomers can also be fragile. A resident who has been here for thirty years may feel that a long-term tenant’s move undermines their sense of belonging, while a newcomer might wonder whether they can belong without becoming a burden.

Local institutions are affected as well. Schools, libraries, public parks, and neighborhood organizations have had to adapt, and in some cases have had to reduce the services they once provided. I am interested in how these changes reshape the everyday life of families in low-income communities. Gentrification changes who is welcome, who pays, and what is valued.

My goal is not to judge the changes as either good or bad, but to understand how they affect people. I want to hear stories from those displaced, from those who have arrived, and from the institutions that have had to change. I want to see what the community itself makes of the new reality. Perhaps the answer lies not in preserving old habits, but in creating spaces where different people can coexist without losing the relationships that make neighborhoods worth living in.
