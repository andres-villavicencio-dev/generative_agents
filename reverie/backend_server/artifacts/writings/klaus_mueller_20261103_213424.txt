Every morning, when I wake at seven and head to the library, my mind is already racing toward the questions I want to answer: What happens to a neighborhood when its residents can no longer afford to live there? Who changes first? Who loses the most? And who survives?

Writing about gentrification is not merely an academic exercise. It is a way of trying to understand the people who are being displaced. I have spent months observing families who have moved, landlords who have raised rents, and community leaders who have defended what remains. I have read countless stories about displacement, but none of them explain how the process begins in quiet ways: a new café, a renovated storefront, a gentrified storefront, a school board committee, a neighborhood association, and a resident who feels increasingly isolated.

I am writing this paper to understand those quiet beginnings. I want to see how gentrification can reshape housing, employment, education, and social trust. I want to ask what happens when a community is transformed without its original residents, and what keeps those original residents from simply following the newcomers.

My purpose is not to blame gentrification. It is often an inevitable consequence of urban development and economic growth. But I also want to recognize that the process is not neutral. It can be used to benefit developers and newcomers while displacing long-term residents and eroding the communities that made the neighborhood valuable in the first place.

For me, this is a matter of social justice. I want to know which groups are most affected and why. I want to learn about the voices that have been silenced and the concerns that have been ignored. I want to find evidence that can be used to support residents who face displacement, not to justify it.

As I continue writing, I carry a few questions with me. How do we measure the impact of gentrification? What does a successful community transformation look like? And how can we make the process more inclusive for everyone?

I hope that by the end of this paper, I will have moved beyond abstract statistics and observed gentrification as a lived human experience. I hope I will have found a way to frame the effects of gentrification that is neither punitive nor purely celebratory. I hope that my research will help people understand that the struggle to protect a home is not only a question of economics—it is a question of identity, belonging, and dignity.
