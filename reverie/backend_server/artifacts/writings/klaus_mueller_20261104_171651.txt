I started my research paper this morning by wondering what gentrification actually does to a community. Not the kind of gentrification that changes the skyline or raises rents, but the kind that slowly makes a neighborhood feel like something it no longer belongs to. I’ve heard stories about displaced residents, about “up-and-coming” businesses taking over places that once housed struggling families, and about people who can no longer afford to live in the area. That’s what I’ve been trying to understand.

For the last few weeks, I’ve been visiting Hobbs Café and watching the people who gather there. I’m especially interested in Klaus Mueller and his idea of supporting newcomers through a café. He’s a young sociologist at Oak Hill College, passionate about social justice and curious about how different groups experience the same space. He wants to make sure the café doesn’t become another place where newcomers have to compete with long-standing residents, or worse, be ignored altogether.

I’ve met a few people there who have been here longer than I have, and they’ve welcomed newcomers warmly. But I’ve also seen signs of rising rents and changes to the local economy, even if I can’t see the full picture. I want to know more about the people here, not just the businesses. I’d like to hear what they are doing, what they value, and how their experiences differ from the displaced people I’ve read about.

At the same time, I want to understand Klaus’s intentions without taking his side. He believes that a café can be a place of welcome, but I’m not sure whether welcoming newcomers means preserving the neighborhood. I think the real question is what the café is trying to achieve and whether it can do that without turning the community against itself.

I’ll continue reading about gentrification, attending local meetings, and talking with people in the neighborhood. I want to find answers that are honest, not comforting. And I hope that one day, someone will ask the same questions I’m asking today.
