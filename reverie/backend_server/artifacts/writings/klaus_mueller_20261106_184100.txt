By 9:00, I had finished my first draft of the paper, and by 11:00 I had a full plan: research, notes, drafting, and a final review. I eat dinner at Hobbs Cafe around 5:00 p.m., then go to bed at 11:00 p.m. I’m a twenty-year-old sociology student, and I enjoy my routine because it gives me enough time to think clearly.

My paper is about gentrification in low-income communities. I want to understand what happens when a neighborhood changes so quickly that the people who lived there are pushed out. I am not writing a history lesson or a purely economic analysis. I want to hear the stories of those displaced, especially the young residents, the businesses that struggle, and the neighborhood that is slowly changing beneath its feet. Gentrification is rarely a single event; it is a process that begins with rising rents, increases in property values, and changes in the character of a neighborhood. But I also want to ask whether these changes benefit everyone.

This paper is my attempt to understand that question. I hope it will not only describe the effects of displacement but also examine the forces that create them. I know this is a difficult task. I have read countless reports and seen many examples of displacement without ever meeting the people living through it. I want to learn from those experiences, not just from the numbers.

The library at Oak Hill College is a good place to begin. I want to read widely, ask difficult questions, and find evidence that supports my own perspective. I am not yet confident that every neighborhood change is equally harmful, and I am not yet convinced that gentrification is inevitable. But the evidence is becoming clearer: gentrification can transform communities, and who benefits and who is left behind matters more than we often assume.

I will keep my questions honest. I will look for stories that give me perspective. And I will write with a sense of curiosity rather than certainty. The goal is not to prove a moral point, but to understand the social and economic forces that shape neighborhoods. Gentrification is often portrayed as an inevitable force of progress, but I want to challenge that assumption. I want to see how policy, wealth inequality, housing policy, and community voices shape the lives of the people who live in the places we call home.

This is my research paper. It is still a draft, and I will revise it as I learn more. I hope that by the end of it, I will have moved beyond the question of whether gentrification is happening to the question of what it means for the people affected by it.
