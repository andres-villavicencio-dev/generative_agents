On February 13, I found myself at the library at Oak Hill College, surrounded by the quiet hum of other students. I was writing the section on housing costs and affordability for my research paper on gentrification in low-income communities. What strikes me most is not only the financial reality of rising rents, but the way these pressures can make an entire neighborhood less welcoming.

When I looked at the data, I saw two very different kinds of housing. The older units in many communities have suffered from decay and neglect, while newer development has brought in tenants who can afford significantly higher rents. This shift changes who lives there and who can afford to live there. It also changes the community itself. Long-term residents may feel displaced, and the neighborhood may lose its local character, shops, and sense of belonging.

The issue is not just that costs are increasing. It is that the same forces creating the new housing are often the same forces that can displace residents and make housing less accessible to those who need it most. I have noticed this pattern in the interviews I’ve conducted. People who bought into the area before the neighborhood changed often feel caught between a desire to be part of the community and the reality that they can no longer afford to stay.

Affordability is particularly difficult for young people, students, and workers who need housing to build careers. When rent becomes a major financial burden, people may move away before they can find a home, or they may choose cheaper options that lack the stability, safety, and community benefits that make a neighborhood attractive. The effect is not always visible in official statistics, but it can still shape who has access to housing and who does not.

My research suggests that gentrification is more than a change in prices. It is a transformation of communities, neighborhoods, and the social relations that define them. I hope to explore this further, but the evidence already suggests that the solution may not be to raise rents or remove older housing. It may be to recognize the need for more affordable options, to protect the housing choices of existing residents, and to understand how these decisions can shape the lives of people who have built the community.
