Over the last few weeks, I’ve been working through the gentrification research paper at Oak Hill College, particularly the section on housing stability and neighborhood stability. My interest is not just about statistics, but about how policies and market changes affect the people who live there. I have seen firsthand how rapid displacement can leave vulnerable residents without reliable shelter, while rising property values often make neighborhoods feel more attractive than they really are.

The section should focus on two interconnected effects: first, how housing instability changes the ability of low-income residents to maintain their homes and build a life; and second, how neighborhood stability depends on the presence of those residents, local businesses, and community institutions. When people move because they cannot afford a home, the people who remain may become too expensive for newcomers. This can lead to a cycle in which rents outpace income, local businesses close, and the neighborhood loses the social networks that keep communities healthy.

I have also noticed that gentrification is rarely purely economic. It can reshape who has access to public services, how neighborhoods are perceived, and who contributes to community life. A stable neighborhood is not simply one with low crime or high property values; it is one in which residents feel heard, supported, and connected. Displacement threatens that connection.

At the same time, I am concerned about how neighborhood stability may be strengthened without displacing residents. Well-designed housing policies, affordable zoning, and supportive investments in local businesses can increase housing stability while preserving the neighborhood’s character. The challenge is balancing these goals without allowing market forces to override the needs of the most vulnerable.

My conclusion is that housing stability and neighborhood stability are not separate problems. When residents cannot maintain their homes, neighborhoods lose their stability, and when neighborhoods become too expensive, housing stability becomes impossible. The research paper should therefore examine policies that protect residents while supporting the communities they inhabit. That is where my research interest lies—not only in understanding the problem, but in finding ways to resolve it.
