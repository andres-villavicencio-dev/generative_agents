Today, the library felt more alive than it had in weeks. The smell of old paper and coffee mingled with the faint scent of another student’s notebook. I sat at a small table near the window, a fresh copy of The Color of Hope on my lap. I had been thinking about gentrification for a while now, especially in Oak Hill. It’s easy to notice when homes change and neighborhoods disappear, but the real harm often happens when people quietly lose their voices.

I’ve read about displacement in stories that describe it as a “market failure,” but that sounds like a mistake rather than a reality. Gentrification can appear to be progress, but it can also erase the lives that were built there. The people who built the community before it changed may not have the same access to housing, jobs, or community resources as the newcomers. They are not always asked to leave.

This makes me wonder what we should do when gentrification begins. We need more than better policies; we need to recognize that people’s well-being should not depend on whether they can afford to stay. We should also listen to those affected before making decisions that affect them.

I’m working on a research paper about how gentrification affects low-income communities. I want to make sure the stories behind the numbers are included. It is important that young people understand their history and that they can participate in the changes that affect them.

As evening approached, I finished reading one more chapter and opened the book on social change. I was curious about the kinds of communities that thrive and the kinds that disappear. I also wanted to consider how my own life could be more inclusive.

By the time I went home, I felt calm but also connected. I had spent the day exploring perspectives I had never considered before. I knew that the work would not be easy, and I had learned a lot along the way.

I turned off the lights and went to bed around eleven. I hoped tomorrow would be another day of questions, ideas, and hope.
