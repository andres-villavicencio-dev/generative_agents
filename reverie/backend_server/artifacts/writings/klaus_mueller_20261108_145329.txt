Introduction

Gentrification has become one of the most persistent and difficult questions in my sociology class. As a young student at Oak Hill College, I have watched neighborhoods change while friends and neighbors have been displaced. My research paper explores how the influx of wealthier residents and rising property values can turn low-income communities into places where people no longer belong. The question I want to answer is: How does gentrification alter the social, economic, and emotional lives of people living in marginalized neighborhoods?

Gentrification is not simply the arrival of new buildings or a few upscale shops. It is a process in which displaced residents lose their homes, lose access to the jobs and services they once relied on, and lose the community relationships that make their neighborhood meaningful. When neighborhoods change rapidly, the costs of that change often fall on the original residents. The people who remain may face higher rents, longer commutes, and a widening gap between what they can afford and what they need. At the same time, the displaced people may find that their cultural identity no longer matches the new character of the place.

I am not arguing that gentrification is a purely bad process. Every neighborhood needs investment, maintenance, and support. However, when development is driven primarily by speculative property values rather than the needs of local residents, the benefits often become unevenly distributed. Gentrification can transform a community into a space that serves the newcomers rather than the people who made it.

My thesis is that gentrification affects low-income communities through interconnected processes of displacement, economic displacement, and cultural displacement. These processes are rarely isolated; the same forces that make a neighborhood more desirable can also make it harder for long-term residents to stay. The ultimate harm is not the investment in the community, but the loss of the community’s history, social cohesion, and the dignity of its people.
