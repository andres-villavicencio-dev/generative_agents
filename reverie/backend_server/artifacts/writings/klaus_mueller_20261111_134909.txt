Gentrification: Who Gets Displaced?

Gentrification is a powerful social process, but the effects are rarely uniform. In my research, I want to understand how rising housing costs, changing neighborhood identities, and increased property values reshape everyday life for low-income residents. The central question is not just “what is changing,” but “who bears the cost.”

Gentrification is not merely a change in prices or rents. It is a process of displacement, displacement, and displacement. A neighborhood can appear revitalized while its most vulnerable residents are pushed out. This can happen through a few pathways: rising rents make affordable housing inaccessible; new commercial projects crowd out local businesses; and cultural changes—new restaurants, renovated buildings, and different expectations—can make familiar places seem unfamiliar.

For me, gentrification raises several interconnected questions. First, who is displaced? Are homeowners, tenants, minority residents, or young families affected? Second, what causes the displacement? Is it simply economic pressure, or can it be rooted in long-standing patterns of exclusion, neglect, and inequality? Third, what happens to people who stay? Do they remain marginalized, or do they gain access to better services and opportunities?

My research has revealed that gentrification is rarely a clean transfer from “old” to “new.” It is often a gradual redistribution of benefit. A community may become more attractive to outsiders while its original residents face rising costs and shrinking social networks. The most pressing concern, therefore, is not merely that people leave, but whether the displaced can retain their communities, homes, and support systems.

Gentrification is also a moral issue. It raises questions about fairness, consent, and responsibility. Should neighborhoods be allowed to change without considering how those changes affect ordinary people? And who bears the burden of development while the benefits accrue to others?

My own experience at Oak Hill College has shown me how powerful these forces can be. I have seen neighbors talk about changing streets, new storefronts, and a neighborhood that no longer feels like home. I have also heard stories of residents whose homes were sold because they could not afford the new prices. These experiences make the abstract concept of gentrification feel personal and urgent.

I hope my research helps us see gentrification not as inevitable progress, but as a social force whose consequences deserve careful attention. The goal is not to deny development, but to understand it enough to protect vulnerable people and ensure that change does not leave anyone behind.
