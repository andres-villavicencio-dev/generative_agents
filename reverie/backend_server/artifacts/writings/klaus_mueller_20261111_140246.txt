My paper is titled Displacing the Community: How Gentrification Affects Local Businesses and Community Services in Low-Income Neighborhoods. I began by reviewing local economic data, community surveys, and interviews with residents, then asked how rising housing costs and changing neighborhood demographics are reshaping the places where people live, work, and belong.

The evidence is not entirely consistent. On one hand, the research shows that new businesses and service providers often move away from gentrifying areas. Small shops, restaurants, and community centers may close when rents rise and the local customer base shifts toward higher-income households. This displacement is not always immediate; many owners face uncertain revenue, higher operating costs, and difficulty finding staff who understand the evolving neighborhood. The result is a loss of the familiar commercial and social fabric that has supported families and small businesses for years.

On the other hand, the data also shows that gentrification can make services more visible. New residents may increase demand for libraries, parks, public transit, and healthcare. The influx of new businesses can also raise the local tax base, allowing community agencies to improve their programs. But I have found that these benefits often arrive too late. Residents frequently feel that the neighborhood is becoming “more expensive” rather than more valuable, and they worry that improvements will benefit the newcomers while the original residents lose their homes and social networks.

My research points to two important conclusions. First, gentrification should not be judged solely by the number of new businesses. It should also be measured by how well community services remain accessible to those who need them most. Second, local support is essential: zoning reforms, rent stabilization, and targeted funding can help preserve existing businesses while still allowing legitimate investment.

My hope is that this paper will encourage policymakers, neighborhood leaders, and community members to look beyond the story of displacement and ask how we can build communities where new residents and longtime residents can thrive together.
