Monday, February 13

I started my evening at the library, surrounded by books, research papers, and the quiet hum of other students. The sun was still rising over Oak Hill College, and I had a paper to defend. I had spent the morning reviewing the effects of gentrification on low-income communities, and by 5:00, the main points were already beginning to make sense.

Gentrification isn’t simply replacing old buildings with new ones. It is a process of displacement, economic restructuring, and shifting community identity. When higher-income residents move in, rents rise, housing becomes more expensive, and older residents are forced to relocate. At the same time, property values often increase, which can make the neighborhood appear more desirable and encourage further investment. But this does not mean the neighborhood has become better. It may have become more profitable, while the original residents lose access to the resources they once depended on.

I also thought about how gentrification affects small businesses and community institutions. Many neighborhood shops, libraries, community centers, and local organizations can struggle when the economy shifts away from their traditional patrons. In the past, the neighborhood might have been defined by shared history and mutual support. Now, those connections can be difficult to maintain.

I am not saying gentrification is inherently bad. Growth, investment, and improved infrastructure can also benefit a community. The problem is that progress is often uneven. It benefits some while harming others. The people who are displaced are rarely the ones who benefit from the growth that follows.

For my paper, I want to examine the effects not only of gentrification but also of the policies and decisions that make it possible. Who decides which projects happen? Who benefits from the increased property values? Who bears the cost? I want to understand the lived experiences of people displaced by these changes, not just the statistics.

As I write, I am reminded that social justice is not just an abstract principle. It is about recognizing how power and opportunity can be distributed unevenly, and how people can be supported when that distribution becomes unfair. Gentrification is one example of that inequality. It is not the only one, but it is a powerful one.

My paper will not solve all the problems. But I hope it will make the cost of inequality visible. I hope it will help displaced residents understand what is at stake. And I hope it will encourage others to question who gets to decide what happens to a neighborhood.

I’ll finish this draft around 10:00 p.m., and then I will read it again before bed. Sleep is the first step toward remembering what I have learned.
