I woke at 7am, ate breakfast at Hobbs Cafe, and headed to the library. The morning light felt different today—it was sharper, warmer. I carried my notebook with me, ready to make sense of what I had been hearing.

I found an article discussing how gentrification changes neighborhoods. It talked about rising rents, displaced families, and the way community life shifts when a neighborhood becomes more desirable. At first, it made my heart tighten. I had never imagined that the same street could hold a family that had lived there for decades while a newcomer moved in with a larger family, pushing the original family out.

I read about the emotional toll on residents. People who have built homes, friendships, and routines are displaced not only financially but also socially. The article described a community center that was quietly replaced by a coffee shop, where volunteers stopped helping neighbors. That made me want to write more than a paper. I wanted to know what people were saying, how they were responding, and whether the community could survive the change.

I also read about people who were rebuilding. A tenant cooperative was organizing rent meetings, and a neighborhood collective was helping older residents find new homes. These stories made gentrification feel less like inevitable progress and more like a choice. It was not just about housing prices; it was about who gets to stay, who has to leave, and what happens when people are forced to choose between belonging and survival.

Reading another article made me wonder: who is responsible for the displacement? Developers, city planners, landlords, or the people who live there? The article mentioned that gentrification can also create opportunities, but it did not explain how those opportunities were shared. I want to understand the tradeoffs. I want to know what policies could protect displaced residents while still improving a neighborhood’s safety and vitality.

I finished the article and felt less angry, more curious. I wanted to find a middle ground. I wanted to understand the history, the economic pressures, and the human cost of change.

As I sat at my desk, I began outlining my paper. I would not argue that gentrification should be stopped. I would instead ask how communities could resist losing their character while still benefiting from development. The question mattered because it was still unanswered.

At 11pm, I went to bed. Tomorrow I would write again.
