Today, I finished another chapter on gentrification. Writing this made me realize that I’m not just asking questions about housing or property values; I’m being asked to consider how our communities are transformed when people’s histories, needs, and voices disappear from the picture.

I’ve spent the day at Oak Hill College, in the library, and at Hobbs Cafe. I’ve also reflected on the social movements that have shaped our lives—the labor rights, environmental struggles, and community organizing work that many of us participated in. Those experiences have taught me that civic participation is not simply about voting or attending meetings. It is about listening, connecting, and helping others see that change can be made.

Gentrification is one form of community change, but its impact is rarely visible only to those who benefit from it. In low-income neighborhoods, rising rents and rising property values can push families out, close small businesses, and alter the character of a community in ways that are difficult to measure. A gentrifier may say that the change makes the neighborhood more attractive, while displaced residents may say that the change removes their homes and supports.

I want to write this paper not to blame the current residents for moving, but to understand the structural forces that make displacement possible. I want to explore how policy, investment, and local power shape who stays and who leaves. I also want to consider how people can participate in those conversations without becoming passive observers. Listening to community leaders, interviewing local residents, and asking difficult but necessary questions can help us recognize patterns that might otherwise remain hidden.

I believe that civic participation is essential for community change, but participation should never mean accepting the status quo. It should include the courage to challenge unfair policies, support vulnerable people, and organize around shared values. My paper will be one small step in that direction. I hope it will inspire other students and community members to pay attention to the lives of people they may not immediately recognize.

Tonight, I’m going to bed at 11pm. I’m tired, but I’m also hopeful. I want to keep asking questions, keep seeking different perspectives, and believe that thoughtful action can still make a difference.
