My final reflections are taking shape as I sit here at Oak Hill College. I’ve come to believe that gentrification rarely starts with a single storefront. It begins with a shift in who has the right to shape the neighborhood, how the neighborhood is valued, and who pays the price.

Over the past weeks, I’ve been looking at housing markets, displacement, and the communities that are most affected. What I’ve noticed is that the changes tend to follow a pattern: rising rents push longtime residents out, while new investors and developers bring in services and businesses that attract newcomers. Those newcomers often bring higher rents and expectations, which make it difficult for existing residents to keep the same standard of living. The result is not just displacement—it is the loss of social cohesion, the erosion of local institutions, and the displacement of voices that have long helped shape the community.

I was also struck by the quiet resistance. Neighbors organizing, community centers reopening, small businesses refusing to move, and residents refusing to sell their homes despite the pressure. Sometimes I feel impatient. It can seem like a slow, bureaucratic process. But I’m learning that resistance is not always heroic. It is also exhausting, often futile, and sometimes requires people to choose between safety, belonging, and dignity.

The research paper I’m writing is meant to make those tensions visible. I want to show how gentrification can turn a neighborhood into a place where only wealthier people can participate, and how this undermines the social benefits of community. Yet I also want to honor the people whose lives are changing. Their stories have shaped the places we live in, and their experiences deserve more than statistics.

By the end of the day, I want to leave the library feeling more curious than confident. I want to leave with one question: What does community look like when it is no longer a given?
