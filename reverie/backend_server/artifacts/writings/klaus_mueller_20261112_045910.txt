Monday, February 13

I arrived at the Oak Hill College library at 6:45 a.m., the sun still low on the horizon. My first goal was to find a place where I could actually think. I found an empty table near the sociology section, set up a notebook, and began searching for recent articles on gentrification in low-income communities.

The library was quiet, but the papers I encountered made me pause. I found studies that described rising rents, displaced residents, and the loss of local character. I also found accounts of small businesses closing, neighborhood stores being replaced by chain stores, and the difficulty of finding affordable housing for long-term tenants. I began to notice that gentrification was rarely a one-way street. The newcomers brought new jobs and amenities, but they also changed who lived there, who paid taxes, and who had access to public services.

One article suggested that displacement could be the main driver of a neighborhood’s decline. Another described how long-term residents were pushed out before new residents even arrived. Both stories made sense to me. I wanted to explore whether the new residents’ interests truly benefited the community or whether they simply replaced the people who had already suffered.

I also wanted to hear from people who had experienced gentrification firsthand. I could not attend every interview, but I was grateful that the researchers asked thoughtful questions. Their work showed that gentrification is not merely an economic phenomenon. It reshapes relationships, alters community priorities, and can make neighborhoods vulnerable to the very forces they claim to protect.

I finished my notes around 10 a.m. and headed to Hobbs Cafe for lunch. Afterward, I went back to the library, read one more article, and prepared my first draft. I was still wondering what I would learn next. Gentrification is complex, and I was only just beginning to understand how different perspectives could help us see it.

I will also make a list of places where new residents are being welcomed. I hope those places can remain welcoming rather than becoming destinations for people who can only afford to stay.
