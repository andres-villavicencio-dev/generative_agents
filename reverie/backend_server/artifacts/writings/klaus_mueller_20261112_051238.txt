I woke at 7:00 a.m. and walked to Oak Hill College before the campus lights came on. By 9:00 a.m., I was already at the library, my notebook open, surrounded by papers and the quiet hum of other students. I needed to write this gentrification paper before the day’s demands ate into me. I had three main claims: that rising property values drive displacement, that displaced residents lose access to essential services, and that community identity is often lost in the process.

The strongest evidence comes from my interviews with longtime residents. The woman who owned the bakery said her storefront had disappeared because the new apartments replaced the neighborhood shops. “It wasn’t a question of whether we could afford it,” she said. “It was a question of whether we still belonged here.” Another resident, a young father who worked at the library, explained that his neighbors had moved away to a quieter, more expensive neighborhood, and he no longer had anyone to talk to. His apartment was now too expensive for him.

I also looked at official reports and property records. They show that the neighborhood’s median income rose alongside the rent. Yet the stories I heard were not consistent with the numbers alone. The data tells us prices went up, but it does not tell us who paid for the increase or who was left behind.

By 10:30 a.m., I had gathered the strongest pieces. The interviews showed that displacement is rarely accidental; it is usually the result of rising demand for housing. The documents support the idea that higher rents reduce opportunities for existing residents. But the numbers alone cannot prove that the displaced people remain excluded from the community they left behind. That requires listening to people who lost their homes and understanding what they mean when they talk about belonging.

I still had a few gaps to fill. I wanted to compare the neighborhood before and after gentrification more closely, and I wanted to hear stories from residents who have been displaced by similar policies. I also hoped to distinguish between gentrification and ordinary neighborhood change. The data supports the first two points, but I still need more careful analysis.

I ate at Hobbs Cafe at 5:00 p.m. and sat down to finish the paper. The library was quiet, but my mind was full. I was not certain that gentrification was always negative, but I was convinced that it had cost many longtime residents their homes, their livelihoods, and the sense of place that makes a community worth staying in. I carried those thoughts with me until I went home, and I carried them forward every day.

I had spent the day collecting evidence, but I still needed more evidence. I had learned how to ask questions, but I had not yet learned how to listen without being interrupted. I had written, yes, but I had not yet changed the stories I told.
