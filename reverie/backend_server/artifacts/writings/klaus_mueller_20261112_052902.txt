Monday, February 13

Today, I sat at the library’s quiet corner with a cup of coffee and a notebook filled with pages I wanted to finish. I had been researching gentrification for several hours, and the library had given me the quiet focus I needed. By the time I left, the paper was still in an early draft, but I felt ready to write the next part.

My first thought was what happens when low-income residents move out of their neighborhoods. The papers I had read suggested that housing policy is often a major driver of displacement. When rent rises, homeownership becomes a goal for newcomers, and the original residents are pushed toward higher-cost areas. The effect can be swift: a neighborhood changes, its character shifts, and the people who built it may have little say in the outcome.

I also wanted to consider the people who live there. Not everyone who moves in is welcomed. Sometimes newcomers come with expectations about affordability, community spaces, or cultural identity. If the neighborhood cannot meet those needs, residents may feel excluded, even if they still enjoy the local services. Gentrification can also change what a community offers. A place may become safer, cleaner, and more attractive to outsiders, but it might also lose the familiar routines and relationships that make it home.

I wrote about this all day, but the research never felt complete. I kept wondering whether policy alone could explain what was happening. What about the people who stayed? What about the neighborhood’s history? What about the people who built it?

Before bed, I sat down for dinner at Hobbs Cafe. The conversation was casual, but the paper stayed in my mind. I wanted to finish the argument, but I also wanted to remember that the people affected by gentrification were not just statistics. They were neighbors, friends, and people who had invested in a place.

Tomorrow, I will try to connect the policy findings with the lived experiences of the people I was studying. That is the next step I need to take.
