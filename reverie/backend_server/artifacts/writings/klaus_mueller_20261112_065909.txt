Monday, February 13, 12:00 p.m.

I arrived at the library at 7:45, the morning light filtering through the windows in the quiet reading room. I wanted to start my paper on gentrification early, but the first thing I needed was a moment of stillness to think through the research I had gathered all week.

I spent the next few hours researching the role of community organizations in resisting displacement, especially in low-income neighborhoods. The stories I found were heartbreaking: families whose homes were being sold while their children waited outside to see if their parents would come home. I also found powerful examples of neighborhood associations, tenant unions, and community groups organizing to keep affordable housing intact and to preserve local character. Their efforts were not always successful, but they showed me that resistance is possible. It does not always have to be a single voice speaking from the top.

The most important lesson I learned was that gentrification is rarely a clean event. It usually begins with small changes—new businesses, louder streets, longer work hours—that make a neighborhood feel unfamiliar. Those changes can be welcomed by newcomers, but they can also make longtime residents feel displaced, unheard, or even invisible. I am writing about this tension because I believe that every change should be examined carefully. Who benefits? Who loses? Who is being asked to change?

I also want to include the perspectives of the people affected by these changes. Their everyday experiences matter more than statistics. A person who has lived in a community for decades should have a seat at the table when community decisions are made.

I finished my research by eating dinner at Hobbs Cafe, the sound of the city outside reminding me that gentrification is not just about paper and policy. It is about people, homes, and the communities they help build. I’m ready to revise my paper with more attention to those real, lived experiences.
