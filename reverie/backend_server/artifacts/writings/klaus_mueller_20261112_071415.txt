Monday, February 13

I arrived at the library early, the morning light finding its way through the tall windows and settling over the stacks. By the time I had a cup of coffee and enough time to think, I knew that gentrification in low-income neighborhoods was not just a story about rising rents or renovated storefronts. It was a story about who was allowed to live there, who was displaced, and whose lives were erased in the process.

My thesis is simple: gentrification can turn a community’s social fabric into a commodity, making housing more expensive and displacing people who have built the community around it. But it can also make room for new voices. I want to show this tension without letting it become either too cynical or too idealistic. Gentrification is rarely a clean one-way street.

I have found that the most dangerous aspect is how quickly change can appear natural. People often assume that a new restaurant, a renovated park, or a quiet street corner means the neighborhood is improving. What people usually forget is that those improvements can come at the cost of affordable housing, local businesses, and the people who have lived there for decades.

I am also beginning to think that gentrification should be understood not as inevitable progress, but as a social process. It is shaped by policy, investment, labor markets, and the habits of people who already occupy the spaces. A good policy would help preserve community rather than accelerate displacement. That is why I need to listen to the displaced residents, not just the developers and real estate analysts.

Tonight, after dinner at Hobbs Cafe, I plan to revise my supporting claims and add more examples from the library research. I want to make sure every argument is supported by evidence, not assumptions. And I want to make sure that I am not only defending gentrification, but questioning it honestly. Because sometimes, the most important research is the research that asks the hardest questions.
