Gentrification has often been described as a cycle of displacement, rising property values, and the displacement of long-term residents. For me, the story is more complicated than that. It is not simply a matter of people moving away. It is also a process of change that makes communities feel unfamiliar. A once familiar neighborhood may suddenly look different—new storefronts, renovated buildings, different businesses—and then feel like a community no one belongs to.

The evidence I have collected so far supports this picture. People who lived there before often lose their jobs, lose their homes, or are forced to live elsewhere. The people who move in may bring money and demand higher rents, but they rarely offer the same kind of investment in local businesses. When the original residents are displaced, the community’s social fabric can begin to fracture.

At Oak Hill College, I have talked to students and neighborhood residents about how gentrification affects lives. One student said that moving to a new apartment meant losing the people she had known for years, even though she was happy with the rent and the amenities. Another resident explained that the neighborhood had changed so much that the people who once made the place feel like home no longer felt like home. The emotional cost of displacement is not only financial; it is also deeply human.

I will keep this research paper on hand. I want to make sure it is not only factually accurate, but also honest about the human lives at stake. If I can convince readers that gentrification is not simply a cycle of change, but a process that can alter communities for the worse, I will be closer to a paper that matters.
