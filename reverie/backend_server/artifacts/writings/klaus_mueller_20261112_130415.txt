Tuesday, February 14

I arrived at the library at 8:00 a.m., eager to continue my work on gentrification. The shelves were full of sociology books, and the quiet atmosphere made it easy to stay focused. I spent the next three hours checking in with Ayesha and reviewing my citations. Her feedback was encouraging, and I felt proud of how much progress I had made.

By 1:00 p.m., I had finished the literature review and organized the most useful sources into a clean bibliography. I added the correct citations to my notes and marked the places where I needed further research. The paper is still in its early stages, but I can already see the structure I want: an introduction to the issue, a review of the main arguments, an analysis of the mechanisms behind gentrification, and a section on the consequences for residents of lower-income neighborhoods.

At Hobbs Cafe at noon, I took a brief lunch and talked with a few students. Their questions reminded me why this research matters. One student asked, “How do you know that gentrification affects people who don’t live nearby?” I explained that gentrification changes access to housing, neighborhoods, local services, and community life, even for people living far away. It is a form of displacement that reshapes everyday experiences.

At 5:00 p.m., I had dinner at Hobbs Cafe. I also read community feedback collected from residents. Several respondents shared that they had noticed rising rents, changing neighborhood character, and a greater sense of isolation. One person said, “I’ve been working long shifts, and I’ve been afraid to move away from my neighborhood.” Their words made me realize that gentrification is rarely just about economics—it affects dignity, safety, and belonging.

From 6:00 to 9:00 p.m., I prepared the next section of my paper. I planned to examine how newcomers and long-time residents might interact, what barriers prevent genuine community participation, and how local leaders can support inclusivity. I also wanted to include evidence from the community feedback and add a few new sources.

By 11:00 p.m., I turned out the lights and went to bed. I had a full day, a working paper, and a community that had shared its perspective with me. I left the library feeling satisfied, focused, and hopeful. I was proud of what I had accomplished, and I carried that hope into tomorrow.
