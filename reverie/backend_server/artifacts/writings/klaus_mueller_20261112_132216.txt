At 7:00 a.m., I woke up, brushed my teeth, and checked my phone to see that Tuesday had officially begun. By 8:00 a.m., I had reached the library at Oak Hill College and settled into my chair beside the research tables. Today was about gentrification—but not the polished, academic version I had planned. I wanted to hear how the city felt when it began to change.

I began by reviewing the citations I had collected so far. Their sources looked useful, but I kept circling back to a few inconsistencies. I had to be careful here. A citation does not have to agree with my argument, but it should not contradict the evidence I am using, especially when my paper is still being assembled. I checked the dates, authors, and page numbers one by one. I wrote each citation in its own place and noted where the sources were pulling from. I did not need every source to agree with every sentence, but I needed them to support the claims that mattered to the paper.

At 12:00 p.m., I went to Hobbs Cafe for lunch. I talked with the other students about gentrification while eating, and the conversation reminded me that the city changes in ways that are often invisible to people living in it. Gentrification can feel like an invisible force: prices rise, neighborhoods feel displaced, and longtime residents lose what they consider their home. I wanted to make sure my paper did not simply describe that feeling without explaining what it means.

By 1:00 p.m., I returned to the library and focused on the next section of my paper. I began with the history of the area, then looked at recent housing developments and local policy changes. I highlighted how these changes affect different groups—long-term residents, young professionals, and newcomers. I was proud of the progress I had made, but I was also honest about what still needed work.

At 6:00 p.m., I read the community feedback from the library’s bulletin boards. A young man wrote that the neighborhood was becoming too expensive for his family. I wrote him a quick reply, thanking him for noticing the change. That brief exchange made me realize that my research had to be more than data; it had to listen to people.

By 7:00 p.m., I finished the next section, reviewed the citations one more time, and set my notes down. The paper was still unfinished, but I had made steady progress. I ate dinner at Hobbs Cafe with two friends, and by 8:00 p.m., I went home. I could not sleep immediately, but I was beginning to see what the research was asking me to find. Tomorrow, I would read more carefully and keep checking the citations. Consistency was not just a rule for me—it was the heart of my work.
