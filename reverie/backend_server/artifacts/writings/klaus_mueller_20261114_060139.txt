My research paper is titled Gentrification in Low-Income Communities: Displacement and the Erosion of Local Services. I began it because I noticed how quickly the neighborhood around my home changed. New storefronts, renovated streets, and higher prices made the old businesses disappear, while residents who had worked there for years were priced out. It felt like the neighborhood was being replaced before the new residents could settle in.

I wanted to understand the mechanisms behind this change. I examined how rising housing costs force families to relocate, how rising rents reduce spending power for local services, and how those changes affect small businesses. I also looked for evidence of community resistance—policies, neighborhood assemblies, and collective action—and what they can actually accomplish.

The most difficult part of my research is measuring what has been lost. I have seen vacant storefronts, shuttered cafés, and fewer neighborhood stores, but I have also noticed that some services have improved. Public transportation has become more reliable, and the library has grown. Yet I am not convinced that these gains came at the same cost. If displaced residents can no longer afford to participate in neighborhood decision-making, or if businesses cannot compete without subsidies, the benefits may be unevenly distributed.

My goal is not to defend every existing business or every displaced family. Gentrification has both destructive and creative qualities. At the same time, I want to highlight how gentrification can concentrate power in the hands of developers, property managers, and new residents while disengaging long-standing residents from the community’s future.

Gentrification is not merely an economic shift. It is a social process that alters who belongs in a neighborhood, who can afford to belong, and who is responsible for its maintenance. Until we study it rigorously, we may continue to assume that displacement is inevitable, while ignoring the voices and decisions that shape which neighborhood changes take place.

For now, I will keep writing. I am curious about the policies that protect small businesses, the ways communities organize around housing, and the evidence that shows how gentrification impacts local services. I hope my research will help students and policymakers see the full picture: that housing affordability, community participation, and local economic resilience are not separate problems, but connected parts of one neighborhood’s future.
