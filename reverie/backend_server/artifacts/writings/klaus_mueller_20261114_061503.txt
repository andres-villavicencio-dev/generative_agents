I woke at 7 a.m., the first sunlight filtering through the windows of the library felt like a small victory. Today, I was writing about gentrification—not just as a statistical shift in housing prices, but as a slow erosion of community cohesion. I had always believed that people could change a neighborhood without needing to be perfect. Gentrification can do that too, but it does not ask for consent first.

The neighborhood I was researching has changed dramatically. New storefronts, renovated apartments, and a more polished public life have arrived, but they have also displaced longtime residents. What I found was not merely displacement, but a loss of shared purpose. The neighborhood once relied on a rhythm of shared routines: block parties, neighborhood associations, and mutual help. Those routines have been replaced by competing demands for attention, funding, and attention to profit.

This is not just about who owns the buildings. It is about who decides what the buildings mean. When the community’s priorities are shifted toward commercial growth, residents are asked to choose between participation and preservation. The result is often a place that looks like a success story on the street, but feels less like a place where people belong.

I also found that gentrification creates a fragile form of social cohesion. New neighbors may share a common future, but they rarely share a history or a collective responsibility. The displaced residents who were willing to contribute to the neighborhood’s past are often the first to leave. This creates a social fracture that is hard to reverse.

My research points to a practical answer: gentrification should not be allowed to decide who is a resident. Community cohesion should be preserved not by protecting a few buildings, but by protecting the relationships that make those buildings meaningful. I hope this paper helps others see that change can be inclusive, and that the people left behind need not remain strangers in their own neighborhoods.
