I started this research paper on Monday morning at Oak Hill College, and the first thing I noticed was how quickly the neighborhood around me seemed to change. The streets were still familiar, but the businesses had shifted. A small café once had a regular every morning, but now it was being replaced by a larger chain store. The people who used to live and work nearby now lived somewhere else. I found that gentrification does not simply replace one community with another; it can erase the social fabric that makes those communities important.

Earlier in the week, I read about how rising housing costs force families to move to less desirable areas. This is a real phenomenon, but it is not the whole story. When people leave because they cannot afford the same neighborhood, the remaining residents often face higher rents, longer commutes, and fewer local opportunities. The process can make the neighborhood feel empty, even though the physical space may still exist.

I also want to consider who is being affected. The people who benefit from gentrification may gain new jobs, better services, and increased property values. At the same time, residents who have lived there for generations may experience a loss of cultural memory, neighborhood identity, and community support. The tension between these two groups is often overlooked.

I have been thinking about how policy and public opinion can change this dynamic. If local leaders listen to residents, make affordable housing a priority, and protect the community’s existing businesses, gentrification could become more inclusive. But policy alone may not be enough. I also want to explore how the changing character of a neighborhood affects the people who remain there. I am still developing my ideas, but I hope this research will help show that gentrification is a complex issue that requires attention from everyone involved.
