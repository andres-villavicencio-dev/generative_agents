The neighborhoods I’m studying are not just places where people live—they are communities whose social fabric is being stretched, displaced, and sometimes torn apart. As I write this section, I want to emphasize that gentrification rarely affects everyone equally. A gentrifying neighborhood may offer better schools, safer streets, and more services, but it also raises the cost of living. For low-income residents, those gains can disappear faster than income grows.

The first effect is a widening gap in what everyone has access to. New developments often bring better parks, libraries, and transit, but they can also remove affordable housing, forcing residents to move further away from the jobs and services they depend on. A recent study I found showed that the price of rent in a renovated block increased by 40% over five years, while average household income in the same area dropped by 12%. For many families, that means a shrinking budget and an inability to maintain the neighborhood they once knew.

Access to resources also becomes uneven. The people who can afford to buy a home tend to have more money for home repairs, community gatherings, and even access to healthcare. At the same time, long-term residents often have fewer bargaining options and less influence over decisions that shape their neighborhood. A resident’s ability to advocate for improvements depends on whether their income allows them to participate in meetings, afford transportation, and access the information needed to understand what is happening around them.

These patterns are especially visible in areas I’ve been visiting. Community centers are frequently crowded or closed, housing projects are underfunded, and small businesses struggle to compete with larger chains. When residents are displaced, they also lose the social networks that help them cope with economic uncertainty. Gentrification can therefore transform an opportunity for neighborhood improvement into a process of dispossession. The people who benefit do not have to be newcomers—their displacement is often the most visible consequence of the process.

This is why I believe that gentrification should be studied not only as a process of change, but as a social process of inequality. The benefits of renovation and development should not automatically extend to everyone who lives there. Supporting affordable housing, investing in long-term residents, and protecting community voice are essential to making change fairer.
