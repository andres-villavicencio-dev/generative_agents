I am Latoya Williams, 25, and I work from the artists’ co-living space, where I photograph details I usually overlook. I am careful, organized, and always looking for connections between what I see and what it means.

For the next local mayor election, I have been talking with friends and neighbors. I noticed two people standing out: one is a young lawyer who has worked with the city council on community programs, and another is a local business owner with experience in small-town development. Both sound reasonable, but neither has shown me how they would change the actual streets.

I do not want to give the election a slogan or a perfect image. I want to understand who is asking questions, what problems people are facing, and what a new mayor could realistically do. I am also curious about the candidates’ backgrounds, not just their platforms. A good mayor should be someone who listens, keeps the community informed, and can explain how decisions will affect everyday lives.

One thing I am concerned about is the tendency to choose the candidate who has the most talking points. I prefer someone who can answer difficult questions and work through disagreements. I also want to see how the election will affect people I know—my neighbors, the shops I use, the places where I work, and the schools I visit.

My plan is simple: follow the election closely, ask thoughtful questions, and measure progress rather than assuming that a campaign will automatically make things better. I am also looking for reliable sources, clear information, and a way to keep the conversation useful without becoming confused.

The strongest points for me are:
The candidate should listen to community concerns, not just present them.
The election should focus on concrete actions, not abstract promises.
The message should be consistent, transparent, and easy for people to follow.

The weakest positions are:
Promising every issue without evidence.
Relying solely on social media or a single campaign platform.
Claiming that a change is inevitable before it has happened.

I want the election to be a useful conversation, not a race to the finish line. That means helping people understand the options, supporting those who can deliver results, and keeping our shared community at the center of what happens next.
