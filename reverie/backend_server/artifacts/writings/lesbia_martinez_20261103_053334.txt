The Gazette does not merely record what happens in Smallville—it asks what it means. For my five-year-old granddaughter, the mayoral election was a list of promises. For me, it became a question: what would Smallville become when everyone makes a choice?

I began the coverage with the same careful curiosity that has guided me for twenty years. There are no shortcuts in reporting. A campaign is not a single voice; it is a conversation among candidates, opponents, and voters. I began by checking that every resident had access to the election information they needed. No one should be left with a campaign brochure when they need to understand it.

The first story focused on the ballot itself. In my research I found several confusing terms, repeated names, and unclear deadlines. I corrected those errors before the first draft went out to the staff. A clear ballot and a clear message are not merely technical details; they are the foundation of responsible voting.

One of the strongest pieces came from Maria Urban. She wrote about the quiet concerns people carry between votes: the need to protect their jobs, the worry about the future of their neighborhoods, and the importance of talking with family members before voting. These were not campaign promises; they were ordinary human concerns. I made sure the article remained honest and focused on the people behind the headlines.

Yuriko Yamamoto’s story about a community meeting was particularly moving. The smallville mayor was not a distant figure; she was a person whose decisions mattered to residents, their businesses, and their children. I read her account with attention to the details, not just the emotional impact. A story is most useful when it connects to the lives of those who live in the town.

By Saturday morning, I was reviewing the pieces. Adam Smith, Hailey Johnson, Mei Lin, Ana, and the remaining contributors were all strong. The coverage was not perfect. I corrected a few minor repetition and punctuation mistakes, but the substance was solid. If anything, I was satisfied.

The election is still being shaped. There will be votes, questions, and surprises. My task is to report honestly and give the public a clear picture of what they can choose.

I also have time to eat, walk around the neighborhood, and create something small. Perhaps a quilt, a poem, or a photograph. The Gazette may be finished today, but the work of Smallville is never truly over. I will carry the questions forward, one neighborhood at a time.
