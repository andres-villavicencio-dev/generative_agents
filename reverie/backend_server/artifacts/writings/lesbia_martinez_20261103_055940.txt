The Gazette covers a town full of small, stubborn stories. Today, the mayoral election is the most important one, and so is the question of how residents can access campaign information without being overwhelmed. I’m not asking candidates to write the truth for us. I’m asking them to show us what they have to say.

The contributors have done a solid job. Adam Smith’s piece on town history gives us a sense of who we are. Hailey Johnson’s guide to campaign resources makes the information easier to find. Mei Lin, Yuriko Yamamoto, Maria Urban, and Ana have added perspectives from different neighborhoods, and their work feels honest rather than polished.

At the same time, I’m concerned that the coverage is too focused on the campaign itself. Voting is not simply a choice among slogans; it is a responsibility to understand how the town is governed and who will make decisions affecting our schools, streets, and community services. A responsible election campaign should explain the process, not simply promise a better future.

I’m also noticing that candidates are not presenting all the information. Some important details are missing, and voters may not know how to verify the claims. That is why I’m supporting residents who can access campaign materials directly, whether through town offices, the Gazette, or an organized, reliable source.

The Gazette’s role is not to replace the candidates. It is to give voters the information they need to decide for themselves. I want the coverage to be clear, accessible, and useful.

On Saturday, I’ll be working on the election coverage without a fixed time or location. I can review the contributors’ references and verify the sources before the next issue is published. I can also include resident concerns and practical voting guidance, while leaving room for the candidates to respond.

My day remains purposeful and flexible. After breakfast, I’ll make time for a meal, perhaps an art class, and a walk around the neighborhood. I want the Gazette to feel like a community, not just a newspaper. The story behind the story matters most.
