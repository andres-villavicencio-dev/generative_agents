This election season is not just about who will hold the mayoral chair. It is about whose voice the Smallville Gazette can carry and whose decisions will shape our community. I asked Adam Smith to look beyond the campaign promises and ask what the candidates mean when they say “growth,” and I asked Hailey Johnson to examine the cost of progress. Their essays are not just political commentary; they are reminders that governance is a conversation between people, ideas, and consequences.

Adam’s essay focuses on what a mayor can actually deliver rather than what she can announce. He traces the difference between short-term relief and long-term sustainability, using city-wide funding as a case study. His strongest evidence is the recent budget shortfall that threatened to cut essential services before the election could be held. “A mayor who spends money without first asking what the community needs is not a leader,” he writes. “She is only a manager.”

Hailey’s essay is more critical. She argues that the current spending plan treats money as a neutral tool rather than a shared responsibility. “We have enough resources,” she writes, “but we are using them to buy what we do not need.” Her strongest evidence comes from two community meetings: one with residents about the proposed parks expansion and another with families concerned about funding for childcare and healthcare. The meetings do not end with applause. They end with questions, disagreements, and a clear desire to see the community’s needs reflected in the final plan.

Both writers agree that transparency is essential. The Gazette must report not only what the candidates promise but also what the process requires. I will give each article careful attention and ask them to explain the assumptions behind their conclusions. If a candidate promises to “fix everything,” I want to know whether that promise includes the cost of fixing the system. If a proposal says it will help the homeless, I want to know whether it helps everyone who needs help.

The final decision is not made by a single committee. It is made by the people whose lives are directly affected by it. This election is a test of our willingness to listen, to disagree, and to decide together. I hope that by the time the ballots are counted, we will not only have a new mayor—but a new way of thinking about our town.
