This Friday, the town’s computer desk looked less like a place of quiet preparation and more like a battlefield. The election campaign had filled every available page, every bulletin board, and even every hallway. I stood there with Adam Smith and Hailey Johnson, both eager to contribute their voices. Adam had the energy of a storyteller, while Hailey brought a sharper eye to the details that often get overlooked. Together, we drafted several strong pieces, but neither of us had the full picture.

Behind every slogan and graphic were human choices, budget decisions, and questions about what our community wanted to preserve. I had asked Mei Lin and Yuriko Yamamoto about how the town should spend its community fund, but their answers were scattered across notes, emails, and a few confusing remarks. Their questions were clear enough: did the town want education, health, infrastructure, and civic participation? How much should each category receive? And who would ultimately decide?

The campaign promised a new future, but a new future is only possible if the community decides what it deserves. Before the next election, the town should stop treating every request as an opportunity to expand and begin examining the foundation on which it is built. A fund should not be used to create a new program simply because there is money available. It should be used to identify what is actually needed.

The first step is transparency. Every decision should be supported by evidence, and every dollar spent should be explained. When the Gazette covers the election, it should show not only who is leading but also what is being proposed and why. The candidates should be asked: what problem are you solving? Who will benefit? What will change if you win?

The second step is community engagement. Local leaders should hold open forums where residents can voice concerns, suggest priorities, and vote on how the money will be allocated. A town funded by its people should be shaped by people who know the community best.

Finally, the town must answer the question that every election ultimately asks: do we want a better tomorrow, or merely one we are more comfortable describing? The campaign can fill the pages, but the community must write the story.
