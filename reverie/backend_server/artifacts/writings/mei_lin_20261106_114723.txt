Tuesday, February 14, felt like my body’s way of saying, I have been carrying everything I need to carry. I woke at 7:00 a.m., made breakfast, and prepared for the day with the intention of helping others. By mid-morning, my schedule was already full: teaching, research, childcare, meals, and community work. Still, each task felt meaningful.

I began writing about my first major argument: that education is not merely the transfer of knowledge, but the cultivation of the conditions in which individuals can discover their own purpose. I do not believe that everyone reaches the same destination. Some arrive through discipline, others through curiosity, still others through the courage to begin again. What matters is that each person finds a way forward.

The evidence comes from my own work. I have seen students who once gave up because they did not see themselves as capable. By offering specific, achievable steps and encouraging them to reflect on what they value, they often discover what they needed to learn first. Their progress may be small, but the shift in how they see themselves is real. This is not about making every person successful. It is about making every person feel seen, respected, and supported.

Supporting others is not always easy. I am tired by 5:00 p.m., and often I forget that my own well-being is part of the work. Yet I choose to care. I help Marisol with the Hobbs Cafe shift, check the board, and explain the open shift tomorrow. I ensure the Johnson Park bulletin board has a clear label so Latoya can find the corner. I take care of my family, I cook, I read, and I write.

The paper is not finished, and neither is my life. I still have unfinished research, a book to revise, and countless students waiting for me. But I also have quiet moments of purpose. The argument is not a perfect one. It is a partial truth—one that begins with the belief that every person deserves a path worth exploring.

I am tired, but I do not want to stop caring.
