The rain outside has been a constant companion for weeks now, drumming against my windows with relentless patience. It was Tuesday when I decided to listen more closely than usual. Pip had spent his afternoon staring at the dark gray clouds gathering over the county line, feeling that familiar lump of dread tightening in his throat like an old coat.

I sat on a sturdy oak bench nearby, wrapped in one of my oversized cardigans because it always feels right when I'm trying to think clearly under pressure. "Pip," I said softly, turning toward him gently from the safety of my chair. "You've heard that storm coming for hours now? The trees are whispering too."

He shivered as a sudden gust rattled his window frame. "I don't know what they're saying," he admitted, looking down at muddy puddles reflecting nothing but despair. "My grandmother used to tell me the wind is trying to scare them away from their roots before it blows hard enough to break things."

"That's not true then?" I asked, smiling warmly despite the gloom outside. "I'm sure there's something you can do about that fear if we just listen together instead of running for cover immediately."

Pip sighed, rubbing his temples. The words on my lips were strange things in this quiet room filled with heavy air and rain-slicked pavement. I watched him fidget until he looked up again. "I think it's because the trees aren't afraid," Pip said finally, a glimmer of hope returning to their eyes. "They've been there all along."

He took my hand in his own small one. The connection between us felt immediate and solid as we stepped back from the bench into the sheltered path leading toward our house. As he walked beside me under the eaves, I realized that sometimes listening is the most powerful thing a child can do to break through their fears. They don't need answers; they just need someone who will listen until the silence breaks on its own and reveals something new for them both to discover together.
