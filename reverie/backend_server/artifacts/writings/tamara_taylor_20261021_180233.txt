This Monday, I spread my notes across the kitchen table and gave myself one clear goal: to shape the new children’s book series without rushing away the parts that make it special. I gathered colored pencils, index cards, and three cups of tea. Carmen Ortiz wandered in, saw the piles, and quietly moved the dictionary out of my reach before I could alphabetize everything.

My inspiration came from a small brass doorknob I found in an antique shop. It was much too tiny for a room, but when I held it in my hand, I imagined a child discovering it beneath a staircase and opening a doorway into a world only inches high. That image became the heart of the series: a seven-year-old explorer named Mara discovers that small doors lead to big adventures. Each book follows Mara as she helps the inhabitants of a miniature village solve a gentle mystery while learning something about courage, belonging, or change.

For the first book, Mara arrives in the village just before its yearly Night of Lanterns. One by one, the lanterns begin disappearing. My research notes suggest that young readers enjoy clues they can spot themselves, so I’m placing visual hints in the illustrations: a trail of silver dust, muddy footprints, and a moth resting near an open window. I’m also researching moths and their navigation, village festivals, old oak trees, and the way rain changes the smell of soil. I want every factual detail to feel true without slowing the story down.

The tone should be warm, curious, and a little magical. Not everything needs to be solved perfectly; sometimes the answer is simply asking for help. Mara’s stories should reflect the patient, kind adults I hope children remember—adults who listen carefully and make room for wonder.

My next step is to draft a twelve-page outline, then test it by reading the opening aloud. If it makes me smile, gives me a lump in my throat, or makes me want to turn the page, I think we’re on the right path.
