On Monday, February 13, I woke at six o’clock while the sky was still dark. I made two cups of cocoa—one for me and one for Carmen Ortiz, my housemate, who was beginning to stir. I had been thinking about a new children’s book. What if every big idea began with things already in the house?

I carried a basket into the kitchen and spread everything across the table. Our materials included recycled paper, cardboard, paper bags, construction paper, yarn, string, rubber bands, tape, glue, safety scissors, crayons, colored pencils, markers, tempera paint, paintbrushes, aluminum foil, tissue paper, wooden blocks, buttons, bottle caps, toy animals, cotton balls, seeds, magnifying glasses, paper cups, and baking trays.

Next came the books. I gathered a weather alanac, a picture dictionary, an animal encyclopedia, a book of poems, a book of short stories, and a map of the world. For our manipulatives, I found a calendar, a weather wheel, magnetic letters, wooden pattern blocks, number counters, two dice, play money, a measuring tape, a timer, a flashlight, and a tiny balance scale.

After breakfast, Carmen and I built a “Museum of Possibilities.” We made exhibits from the materials on the table. Buttons became planets. Toy animals lived on cardboard mountains. We measured each creature, rolled the dice to invent silly names, and used the weather wheel to decide what the museum’s sky would look like. The picture dictionary helped us label every room.

At six o’clock, we stopped for dinner. The museum looked strange and wonderful. I sketched it quickly in the margins of my comic-book pages, adding a robot with moth wings and a very confused raccoon.

That night, just before ten, I sat down to revise. I changed one sentence, softened another, and read the story aloud to my stuffed bear. He listened without interrupting, which is one of the nicest things a bear can do.

Then I wrote: “Sometimes a story is waiting inside a box. You only need to open the lid and begin.”
