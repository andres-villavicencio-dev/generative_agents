The Moonlight Library

Every night, after the last train had gone and the town houses had tucked their bright windows into bed, a small silver key appeared beneath Nia’s pillow.

She never put it there.

Or so Nia thought.

On Monday, she found it while brushing her hair. On Tuesday, it waited beside her toast. By Wednesday, the key had moved to the pocket of her red coat, warm as if someone had been holding it in their hand.

“Do you know what this opens?” Nia asked the key.

The key said nothing. Keys never did.

At school, Nia showed it to her best friend, Sam. Sam turned it over twice and frowned.

“It looks like a library key,” he said.

“That’s impossible,” said Nia. “There’s no library in our town.”

But that evening, when the clock struck ten, the old lamppost on Elm Street blinked twice.

A tiny door appeared at its base.

Nia held up the key. It began to glow.

The keyhole matched perfectly.

Click.

Beyond the door was a room that was somehow both very small and very large. Books climbed the walls, reached the ceiling, and floated in gentle constellations above Nia’s head. Their pages rustled like leaves in a forest.

A librarian sat behind a desk made of polished branches. She had silver hair, a green shawl, and a smile that made Nia feel she had come home.

“Welcome,” said the librarian. “You are very late.”

Nia looked around. “I only just found the key.”

The librarian raised one eyebrow. “Then you are early for everything else.”

She placed a thick book on the desk. On its cover, a moon shone above a row of little houses.

“This book has lost its last page,” said the librarian. “Every story in this library depends on that page. Without it, the books will forget their endings, and one by one, they will disappear.”

Nia’s stomach fluttered. “How do I find it?”

The librarian opened the book. Inside was a picture of a garden under a starry sky. A trail of golden footprints crossed the flowers and disappeared beneath a silver gate.

“Look carefully,” the librarian said. “The page is hidden where every good story begins: with a promise.”

Nia pressed her hand to her heart.

That night, she dreamed of the moonlit library, the silver key, and a golden trail leading home. She also dreamed of the promise she had made to her grandmother: that she would always be brave enough to open doors, even when she could not see what was waiting behind them.

When Nia woke, the key rested in her palm.

In the book on her bedside table, one new page had appeared.

It was blank, except for a single line:

The first step is always the hardest. The second is taken with a friend.
