“Mara and the Door in the Snow”

On Monday morning, Mara found a blue door standing in the middle of the snowy field.

There was no house around it. No windows. No walls, either—just a narrow wooden door with a shiny brass knob. Snowflakes landed on its paint and melted, leaving little shining rivers behind.

Mara had never seen anything so strange. She thought about calling for help, but the field was far from the village. She thought about turning around, but the door seemed to hum softly, as if it were waiting for her.

She took one step closer. The door whispered, “Problems are just stories that haven’t been brave enough to begin.”

Mara reached for the knob. The brass was warm.

When she opened the door, she found herself standing in her classroom, though it looked different. Every desk had vanished. The walls were covered with enormous handwriting, and each letter moved like a tiny animal. At the front of the room stood Miss Bell, smiling.

“Welcome, Mara,” Miss Bell said. “Today, you must teach the letters how to sit still.”

Mara stared at the wiggling letters. One stretched its legs. Another chewed a chalkboard. The letter R raced around the room, refusing to join the alphabet.

Mara was impatient, but she remembered her grandfather’s advice: “When something is too big to fix, begin with something small enough to hold.”

She picked up a piece of chalk and wrote her name. Then she drew a chair beside the letter R and said, “You may sit here.”

R stopped running.

One by one, Mara made places for all the letters. Soon, the classroom was quiet. The alphabet sat in order, bright and bold, while the marching letters had become a soft rustle along the walls.

Miss Bell handed Mara a golden key.

“This will open any difficult door,” she said.

Outside, the blue door waited in the snow. Mara stepped through it and returned to the field. The key disappeared into her coat pocket, but she knew it was still there.

And whenever a problem felt too large to face, Mara remembered that even a wild, wandering alphabet could be guided home—one small, patient step at a time.
