I spent the morning reviewing the revised chapter of the new children’s book series, and I’m much happier with it now. The beginning still felt a little crowded, so I moved one important sentence closer to the first page and gave my main character more room to breathe. I also softened the ending slightly. I do not want the last page to feel too tidy or too grown-up. Children should sense that the adventure continues, even if the story itself has reached a satisfying pause.

The chapter is about Mina and the small blue door that appears in the hallway of an ordinary apartment building. I love that Mina does not treat the mystery as frightening. She is curious, patient, and willing to listen before she decides what to do. That feels true to who she is, and I hope young readers will recognize that kind of courage.

While I was writing, I kept a list of new ideas for later stories:
A girl who plants a garden in the city and discovers that each flower attracts a different kind of dream.
A talking kite that becomes lost during a festival and asks a shy boy to help it find the moon.
A library after closing time, where every book can visit one of its characters for a single afternoon.
A child who wakes up in a house where all the clocks are counting down, but not to midnight.

For my next writing session, I will choose one of these ideas and write only the opening. I do not want to worry too much about the whole plot. First, I need to hear the voice and see the first problem. I will write for forty-five minutes without editing, then take a short break and read what I have aloud. I will check whether the opening makes me want to keep reading, whether the main character feels alive, and whether the magical element is clear enough without being explained away.

I also want to revisit Mina’s blue door once more before I begin. There may be one small detail waiting for me, perhaps something only Mina notices. That is usually how the best ideas arrive—not with a loud announcement, but with a quiet knock from inside the story.
