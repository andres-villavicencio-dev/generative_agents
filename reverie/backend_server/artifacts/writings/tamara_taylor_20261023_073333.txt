The window in my bedroom lets in the gray light of February 14, but I do not feel cold. In fact, the light feels like a quiet visitor—it carries the faint scent of rain, the soft hum of the street, and the golden dust that settles on the floorboards. I sit on the edge of my bed and watch it move across the dark room. A little bird has landed on the sill. Its wings are a pale blue, almost invisible in the dim light, and it keeps moving, as if trying to escape my attention.

I write on the page. The words feel like they are gathering themselves together, waiting to be spoken. The first sentence should be simple, perhaps even ordinary, but not so plain that it loses the mystery I want to create. I have a letter in my drawer with a faint blue postmark on it. The ink is old, though not quite faded. It looks like the color of a sky before the clouds had learned how to hide.

I am three hundred pages into my opening chapter, and I am beginning to wonder whether the world itself will wait for me. I do not want to rush the beginning. I want to make sure the reader feels the space between the first sentence and the next, that there is a breath before the story begins.

The bird hops onto the window sill and settles beside me. It watches me write. I do not know whether it understands the words or only their shadow. I have spent the last two days researching the neighborhood historic building, the old letter, and the mysterious blue-ink postmark. I want the opening to feel like a small discovery, as though the mystery has been opening its mouth for me to hear.

Tomorrow I will begin the full chapter, but tonight I only want to keep writing until the words feel like the bird’s light blue wings.
