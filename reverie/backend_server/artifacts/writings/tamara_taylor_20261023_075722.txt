The blue postmark stayed the same. That was the first thing I noticed.

It was small, almost invisible, resting on the bottom of the old letter. The ink was wet, dark, and seemed to hum beneath the paper. I had written it by mistake. I didn’t mean to leave a clue. I had been so busy trying to make the page feel special that I had forgotten how ordinary a mistake can be.

Then Carmen showed me the building.

It was a former mill, with tall windows and a roof that looked like it had been painted with the stars. “A historic building,” she said. “Like the one in the story.”

I remembered the story from the beginning. It was about a boy who was afraid of the dark, a girl who loved to make things that moved on their own, and a letter that could change how a house felt.

I looked at the blue ink again. Maybe the postmark was the key. Maybe the building was the map. Maybe the letter was the puzzle.

I picked up the pen. I didn’t want to write everything down at once. I wanted to let the story grow, one small decision at a time.

“Where do we begin?” I asked myself.

The answer came the next morning. I stood in front of the old mill and looked at the blue postmark. I decided to write something simple enough to be real, but simple enough to make everyone wonder what it could become.

The story began with a boy named Leo who was afraid of the dark. He had spent all morning trying to find the old blue postmark. He had looked in every window, under every lamp, and even asked the miller for a clue. The miller said, “The postmark is not here.”

Leo stood outside at night. The mill was dark, but he could hear something moving inside.

The first problem was not that the postmark was missing. It was that he had spent the morning looking everywhere without knowing how to look.

The next problem was that the miller knew too much.

The last problem was that Leo had forgotten to remember that ordinary mistakes can be the brightest discoveries.

He looked at the old letter. He looked at the blue ink. He looked at the dark, quiet building.

“Maybe,” he whispered, “the ink is trying to tell me something.”

He picked up the pen.

The story did not begin with a big adventure. It began with a mistake. And it began with a quiet beginning.
