I woke at six and made breakfast before the world was ready for me. I liked my mornings slow, especially when the house was quiet.

“Are you sure the door is locked?” called the neighbor through the crack.

I smiled and tapped the handle. “Yes, I’m sure. Please do not bother us.”

“Good morning, Mrs. Taylor,” came the voice from the window.

The neighbor stopped. “What?”

“I am not Mrs. Taylor,” I said kindly. “I am Tamara. I am not the neighbor’s friend.”

The neighbor looked at the door, then at me, and finally at the window. “You mean you are the girl who lives here? And you are not the neighbor’s friend?”

“I am,” I said, and looked out at the street. “I am here to help. I am Tamara Taylor.”

“Tamara Taylor?” the neighbor repeated, smiling. “That is a very strange name.”

“I am,” I said, and opened the door.

The neighbor walked in and looked around. “I’m Carmen Ortiz. I’m a photographer. I thought this house might make a great setting for a children’s book. It is old and full of stories.”

I nodded slowly. “I would love to help you.”

We talked for an hour, then I returned home and sketched the house in the margins of my notebook. There were many details I could use: the cracked window, the old letter on the table, and the blue-ink postmark that looked like a bird’s wing. I wanted to make the beginning of the story feel alive, with warm dialogue and clear pictures in my mind. I thought the middle would be the most interesting part, because it should show what the characters discover, not just what they say.

By ten, I was tired, but happy. I went to bed and dreamed about old buildings, blue ink, and the possibility of a mystery beginning with a simple question.
