I woke at six, and the world looked especially bright. After making my bed, brushing my teeth, and sipping a cup of tea, I sat down at the table. Breakfast was simple—warm toast, fruit, and some honey—but it felt like a small celebration. At this moment, I was thinking about the children’s series I’ve been developing. The opening chapters felt strong, and the middle sections had become a little tangled.

I want the children’s characters to carry their adventures with quiet curiosity. They should feel safe enough to make mistakes, even when those mistakes change the path they’re on. The setting matters too—not only the streets and shops, but the way a place feels when the world seems to pause. I want the children to notice the small things: a bird resting on a fence, a leaf drifting across the path, a shadow growing longer in the morning.

For now, I’m focusing on one scene where the children are trying to find something unusual. It should feel like they’re following a trail, not searching for answers. I’d like the old building to appear as a place where every corner feels full of stories. The windows should look through to different times and different people. The street outside could be lively, with voices and sounds moving around.

I’m also thinking about what the middle sections can reveal. Carmen Ortiz and I discussed how a historic building, an old letter, and a mysterious blue-ink postmark could create contrast and mystery. I like that idea. A postmark that changes color or moves could surprise the reader without breaking the story. The letter could hold a clue, a secret, or simply a message that feels too important to say aloud.

I’m not yet sure which chapter needs the most help, but I’m making a small list. I’ll open each section, read the beginning, and ask: what is the child trying to find? What keeps changing? Where does the world suddenly feel different? I want the middle to be a little slower, more curious, and less rushed.

At ten, I’ll put my notebook away, get ready for bed, and let my story sleep for a little while. Tomorrow, I’ll wake up, write another beginning, and listen to the children’s voices.
