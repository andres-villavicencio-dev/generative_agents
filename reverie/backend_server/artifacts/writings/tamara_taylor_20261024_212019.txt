I woke at six, brushed my teeth, and made my coffee. By seven, I had finished breakfast, gathered my journal, and opened the children’s-book draft. The opening scene was sitting on my desk like a small, nervous animal—perfectly detailed, yet still unsure where to go.

I thought about the main conflict: Maya and her new toy box. The toy box was full of things no one asked for—a tiny clock that counted backward, a mirror with no eyes, and a letter written in bubbles. Maya had found the box by herself, and she had never asked anyone to keep it. That made it special, but it also made it dangerous.

By noon, I had enough to revise the opening, but I still needed to carry the story forward. The conflict should feel alive, not scary. It should be about Maya’s curiosity and her fear of losing what she had found, not about making her feel guilty for being brave.

I revised the first paragraph together with Jennifer. We agreed that she was still the right person to read it aloud. I told her that the toy box was the real hero.

At ten, I went to bed. By six, I was awake again, with a fresh page to turn. I hoped the children would leave the story with the same questions they brought in: What does the toy box want? How can Maya find it without losing it?

I’ll carry that over to the comic script. I need a beginning that feels like a promise—and a promise that isn’t too easy to keep.
