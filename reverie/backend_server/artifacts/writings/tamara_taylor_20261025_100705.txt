I woke at 6:00 AM and made the morning routine while the sun felt soft and ready for the day. I ate breakfast before 6:30, then carried my notebook outside to the garden.

I started the opening scene with a small, ordinary girl who lost her favorite toy. A red ball had gone missing, and every toy in the neighborhood looked like it had been taken. The girl didn’t panic. She looked around slowly. She asked the squirrel, “Did you find it?”

The squirrel hopped over. “Yes, Miss, I found it.”

Then he did something the story never said aloud.

He put the ball under the tree.

The girl watched it. She smiled. “Thank you, squirrel.”

I wrote that line because a child can imagine a squirrel helping when another child is lost. The ball became more than a lost toy—it became a promise.

I paused at the page. The opening should feel alive, not just like a list of events. I want the girl’s fear to turn into curiosity. I want the squirrel’s kindness to feel earned, not handed over.

The children need to feel something before they read on. They need to believe that something ordinary—something small—can change a big moment.
