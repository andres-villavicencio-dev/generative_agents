# A Gentle C Minor Third

*Three days in the life of Eddy Lin, who was trying to write a song about kindness and was $60 short of a scarf*

Eddy Lin is nineteen, and he is up at seven because the piece isn't going to write itself.

He studies music theory and composition at Oak Hill College and lives with his family in the house on the hill, in a bedroom that smells of coffee and old sheet music. He is the kind of student who walks with a notebook, and this week the notebook has one assignment in it: write something about the old market and the people in it. He has the idea. He doesn't have the notes yet.

What he does have is a walk with his mother.

## Monday: the market, and a song he didn't write

Mei, his mother, teaches at the college, and she's the one who suggests the walk: a loop through the old market before the day starts, past the small shops that are only just raising their shutters. It's lively even at that hour. Vendors call to each other across the stalls. Everything has a story, she tells him, if you stand still long enough. They stop at the café near the market for a coffee and a snack, and he starts to feel the shape of the piece, though not the sound.

The sound comes from his father.

John Lin works the counter at the Willows Market pharmacy, and over a morning coffee he tells Eddy about a song he wrote a few months ago, a small community song, composed in the gaps between customers. It's called *Morning Market Delight*. It's about eggs, seasonal fruit, whole grains, and it's really about something else: helping someone find the right medication, offering a warm smile, passing on a piece of advice that costs nothing. Small acts of kindness. "I realized our community is already full of them," John tells him.

Eddy goes quiet, which in his case is a sign of concentration. Then he says the thing that will organize the next three days: *that's what my piece is about.*

By mid-morning he is working through it at the kitchen table. Isabella Rodriguez, who runs Hobbs Cafe, is having breakfast of eggs and toast nearby, and Eddy plays her the opening, a gentle melody over a sustained bass note. He's chosen a C minor third, with a soft G underneath, warm, intimate, and the sort of harmony that doesn't compete with the melody. "A soft, warm C minor third," Isabella says back to him, "with a gentle sustained G." She takes notes while he plays. It's the first time anyone has treated his piece as real.

All day the idea gets passed hand to hand. Arthur Burton, who runs the pub, tells him about the gentle old-time folk tunes you'd hear at the Rose and Crown and the library. Maria Lopez hears about the C minor third and asks questions. Klaus Mueller, studying sociology, tells him over a short walk about rising rents and long-time residents being priced out, and Eddy listens and thinks: *the old market might not always be there. Maybe the piece should hold on to it.*

## The scarf

There's something in a shop window that he keeps coming back to.

It's a silk scarf in the display at the Willows Market, $180, and Eddy has $120 in the bank. He doesn't say who it's for, or whether it's for anyone. In his head it's a number, and the number is sixty dollars away. He mentions it twice that week, in the same flat way: *Eddy keeps thinking about the silk scarf; with only $120 saved, he wants to earn more and get ahead.* A student with an empty weekend and a quiet ambition, he is also, for the first time, someone who wants to do something about money.

He doesn't buy it. He doesn't apply for work yet. But the thought sits beside the music all week, like a second theme.

## Tuesday: revising

By Tuesday he's revising. Mei comes into the kitchen with her own take on the opening scene, a young woman asking for a refill of water, a small request that makes a stranger feel seen, and he writes it into the piece. "The simple version," she says. "Keep the harmony simple and the melody gentle." She says it twice; he writes it on the inside of his cover.

At midday he finds John again and plays him the opening, and they try versions: a warm welcome, a glass of water, an encouraging word, then a small, open chord to close. John tells him the encouragement line makes it warmer. Eddy plays it again and agrees.

Between sessions he reviews what's weak. He rearranges for a more varied texture. He writes a paragraph about Oak Hill College and the community, because the assignment needs words too. In the garden he runs into Giorgio Rossi, the mathematician, who is tracking pollen patterns along the old paths and wants to know whether the most-walked routes carry the most visitors, the way they did in the early 1900s. Eddy promises to check the visitor records for him. He means it, though he isn't sure when.

Late in the afternoon he has a conclusion that he writes down himself: *my music is an emotional exploration of community and kindness, drawing on the old market, small shops, cafés, and everyday generosity.*

## Wednesday: the quiet before

By the small hours of Wednesday, the piece is nearly done, and he's asleep.

Tomorrow's page in his notebook says: practice the melody from eight to ten; listen to Isabella's thoughts on the harmony; study scores from half past eleven; work on the composition in the afternoon. Last on the list, in his own handwriting, almost an afterthought: *explore the store in the Willows Market and Pharmacy for the first time.*

It's the shop with the scarf in its window.

He will go. He'll see the price again. He'll hold it in his head next to the melody: a number he can't afford yet, a tune he can. For now he sleeps, a hundred and nineteen dollars and sixty cents in his pocket, and a C minor third in his head that sounds, he thinks, like a glass of water offered to a stranger.
