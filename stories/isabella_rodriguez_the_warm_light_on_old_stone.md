# The Warm Light on Old Stone

*Four days in the life of Isabella Rodriguez, who runs Hobbs Cafe and was throwing a party*

Isabella Rodriguez is up at six, and the first thing she does is check what's in the fridge, because the answer is usually *not much*.

She's 34 and owns Hobbs Cafe. People call her friendly, outgoing, hospitable, and she'd say it's less a trait than a job description: she makes the place somewhere people can sit down, breathe, and be glad they came in. The café looks after everyone else. Her own kitchen is another matter. That Monday morning she buys ten each of eggs, bread, milk and coffee beans at the supply store, $52, and restocks the fridge. By the afternoon she's back for ten more eggs and bread and milk, another $32. Two days later she'll go again, $20. By then her wallet holds $2.60. She plans her evenings around "low-cost purchases." The café's till is healthy. She's the one who's thin.

None of this slows her down. It's Monday, and on Tuesday evening, from five to seven, Hobbs Cafe is hosting a Valentine's Day party.

## Monday: the punch

She's still gathering supplies when she runs into Arthur Burton in the aisles. He runs the pub and is shopping for his own shelves. She says hello and mentions her party, and Arthur lights up. "A rose-and-elderflower punch would be perfect," he tells her, and gives her the recipe on the spot: elderflower gin, elderflower liqueur, lemon, sliced strawberries, sparkling rosé added just before serving, with a sparkling elderflower mocktail for non-drinkers. Then he checks his shelf and winces. They're out of elderflower cordial and strawberry purée.

"Blend fresh strawberries with a little lemon," he suggests, and use a lightly sweetened elderflower syrup. "It'll give you the closest match." She thanks him. It isn't the plan, but it's a plan, which is more or less her whole approach to running a café.

By evening the plan has friends. Abigail Chen, who makes digital art, wants to bring an interactive piece. Tom Moreno, who works at the Willows Market, offers to pick up cordial and fresh strawberries himself. The following morning, Carlos Gomez catches her with an unexpected gift: he'll write a short poem, something about rain "striking the window like a fist," turning longing into something almost physical. He'll send it by three. She asks for it by then so she can read it before the doors open.

## Tuesday: the arch

The decorations are the thing she's proudest of, because they came from a morning walk.

She'd wandered through the old market district and found a courtyard where the dawn light caught the stone arches and turned them warm. She wanted to carry that feeling indoors. The palette is blush pink, deep red, warm gold, and cream. She builds a small arch-shaped centerpiece by the front window and sets gold accents around it, so that at the right hour they'll catch the light the way the stone did. Carmen Ortiz, who runs the supply store, brings paper samples with botanical prints in deep red and soft pink, and spreads them out for Abigail and Isabella to choose from.

Carlos, who has seen the colors, tells her the deep red sounds like the anger in his poems and the gold sounds like renewal. "The gold holding the light," he says, "while the old stone keeps its scars." She carries that line around all day.

By five the café is full. The punch is mostly right. Ryan Park, who builds software for the shop's ordering and had been quietly testing his work with her as a customer, comes in with Carmen. Adam Smith arrives for his first visit to Hobbs Cafe ever, and Arthur drops by after dinner service to check on how the party is going. Giorgio Rossi had sat down earlier for a glass of water and a few quiet minutes, and ended up asking what she thought of the mayoral candidates. Wolfgang Schulz promised permits in ten to twenty business days and matching grants for storefronts, he said. Sam Moore had a lot of local support, but nobody knew how he'd deliver. She listened, thought of her own storefront, and said she'd want to see the first small-shop permit approved in a couple of weeks before believing anyone.

By nine, the party is over and the chairs are stacked. She sleeps at eleven.

## Wednesday: the quiet after

She gets up at six and opens at seven. The clean-up comes with a good kind of tiredness: she keeps the café running, restocks tea and other hot-drink supplies, and talks to Carlos, who is on his way to the courtyard with new lines. Carmen asks what she's shopping for, and the answer is *the same things as always*: bread, eggs. She buys them for $20.

That afternoon, Tamara Taylor suggests exploring a classroom at Oak Hill College together at 5:45, because the visit might inspire her next map and story. Isabella agrees, never having been inside one.

Then Tom stops her on the street, dressed for a party. "Are you having the Valentine get-together tonight?" She smiles. "That was last night, Tom, but you're always welcome for a coffee." He laughs and apologizes for the mix-up. They agree on eight o'clock Thursday, at the café. She tells him she's been winding down after a busy week, and glad of how the party turned out.

## Thursday morning

At 6:30 Isabella is making a light breakfast of coffee with milk, except there's no milk, and she'll have to buy more again. Jane Moreno walks with her to the café, and they talk about the quieter historic corners of town, the arches, the warm light. At seven she's in her apron, and by 7:30 the coffee station is ready, ten minutes before Tom arrives.

Her notes for the day are plain. Meet Tom for coffee. Host the morning crowd. Lunch. Explore the classroom at the college for the first time. Return for the afternoon rush. Close, count the till, plan the next low-cost purchase. Home by eight, bed by eleven.

She feels, as her notebook puts it, satisfied, energized, and hopeful. She'd like a slower day soon, she admits. But the sun is coming up over the stone and gold in her front window, and someone she likes is about to walk in.
